Introduction

A constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) is a problem stated as three things: a set of variables, a domain of possible values for each variable, and a set of constraints that say which combinations of values are allowed. Once a problem is in that form, a general-purpose solver can attack it without knowing anything about maps, puzzles, or factories. The hard and interesting work, then, is modeling: deciding what the variables should be, what values they can take, and how to express the rules of the problem as constraints.

This topic walks through the canonical formulations used throughout the course. Map coloring gives the cleanest example of a binary CSP and its constraint graph. Sudoku introduces global constraints such as all-different over rows, columns, and boxes. N-Queens shows how a smarter choice of variables (one variable per column rather than one per square) can collapse an enormous search space. Cryptarithmetic motivates auxiliary variables for carries and higher-arity constraints, and job-shop scheduling and timetabling show CSPs applied to resource and time allocation.

While watching, keep asking three questions of every example: what exactly are the variables, what is in each domain, and what is the arity of each constraint? Also notice when a speaker chooses one encoding over another and what that choice buys — fewer variables, smaller domains, constraints that are easier to check, or symmetry that can be eliminated. Those decisions are what make the difference between a solver that finishes instantly and one that stalls.

About this video

A short explainer that uses the 4-Queens version of the classic Eight Queens puzzle to introduce constraint satisfaction. The smaller board keeps the state space small enough to follow by hand, so the video can show how queens are placed, how conflicts are detected, and how the puzzle maps onto CSP variables and constraints.

Why this video

It targets the N-Queens subtopic directly and, by shrinking the board, makes visible the modeling choice at the heart of that subtopic: how you define variables (and thereby rule out whole classes of illegal placements) determines how much search is left to do.

About this video

A conference talk from Roguelike Celebration in which Pierre Vigier describes using constraint satisfaction to populate and generate rooms for a procedurally generated game. It is an applied case study: the speaker explains how the design requirements for a room become variables, domains, and constraints that a solver can satisfy.

Why this video

It shows the modeling process on a messy real problem rather than a textbook puzzle, which is exactly the skill this topic is building — going from an informal problem statement to an explicit CSP formulation, and seeing that formulation choices matter in practice.

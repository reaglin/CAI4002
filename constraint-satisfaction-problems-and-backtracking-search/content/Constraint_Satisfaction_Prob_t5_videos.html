Introduction

Backtracking search assigns one variable at a time, and the order in which you pick those variables is not fixed by the problem — it is a choice you make. That choice can change the size of the search tree by orders of magnitude, even though every ordering eventually finds the same solutions. This topic looks at the standard heuristics for making that choice well: minimum remaining values (MRV), also called the most constrained variable, and the degree heuristic, which breaks ties by counting constraints on still-unassigned variables.

The underlying idea is the fail-first principle. If some branch of the search is doomed, you want to discover that as early and as cheaply as possible, so pick the variable most likely to fail soon — the one with the fewest legal values left. The degree heuristic complements this at the start of search, when all domains are still full, by choosing the variable that constrains the most others. In practice solvers combine the two: MRV first, degree as a tie-breaker.

While watching, pay attention to three things. First, how domains shrink dynamically during search (this is what makes MRV a dynamic rather than static ordering). Second, the distinction between choosing which variable to assign next and choosing which value to try — they are separate decisions with separate heuristics. Third, count nodes or branches in the worked examples, because node counts are how we will measure whether a heuristic actually helps.

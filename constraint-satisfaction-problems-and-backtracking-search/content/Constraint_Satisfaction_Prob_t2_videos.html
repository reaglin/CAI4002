Introduction

Every constraint satisfaction problem is defined by three things: a set of variables, a domain of possible values for each variable, and a set of constraints that restrict which combinations of values are allowed. This topic focuses on that third element. Constraints are usually classified by how many variables they touch at once: unary constraints restrict a single variable (for example, "Western Australia cannot be red"), binary constraints relate exactly two variables ("adjacent regions must differ"), and higher-order or n-ary constraints involve three or more (the carry-digit relationships in a cryptarithmetic puzzle, or an AllDiff constraint over a whole Sudoku row).

The classification matters because it determines how we draw and reason about the problem. Binary CSPs map cleanly onto a constraint graph, where nodes are variables and edges are constraints; n-ary CSPs need a hypergraph, where a single hyperedge touches several variables at once. Since most inference and consistency algorithms in this course are stated for binary constraints, you should also know that any finite-domain n-ary constraint can be converted to binary form, typically by introducing auxiliary variables whose values are tuples. Finally, not all constraints are hard: preference or soft constraints assign costs rather than forbidding assignments, turning the problem into a constrained optimization problem where we seek the best solution rather than just a feasible one.

About this video

The first lecture in Stanford CS221's CSP sequence, which introduces constraint satisfaction problems as a distinct model from state-based search: variables, their domains, and the constraints (expressed as factors) that score or forbid assignments. Based on the title and series, it gives the overall motivation for CSPs and works through canonical examples to show how a problem is encoded and how constraints over one, two, or more variables are drawn as a graph. It sets up the vocabulary used later for consistency and backtracking.

Why this video

It is taught by Stanford Online as part of a university AI course, so the formalism is accurate and matches the textbook treatment used in this module. The overview framing is exactly what you need before classifying constraints by arity and reading constraint graphs, and the factor-graph style of drawing constraints generalizes naturally to hypergraph representations of n-ary constraints.

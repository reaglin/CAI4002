Introduction

Most of the search methods you have seen so far build a solution step by step from an initial state, keeping track of paths and frontiers. Local search works differently: it starts with a complete candidate solution — every queen already on the board, every task already assigned to a machine — and then tries to improve it by making small changes. To set up a problem this way you need three things: a complete-state formulation (what counts as a state), a set of move operators that define a state's neighbors, and an objective or heuristic cost function that scores how good a state is.

Once those pieces are in place, it helps to picture the whole set of states as a landscape. Each state is a point on the surface, its height is its objective value, and a move operator lets you step to an adjacent point. Searching becomes a matter of walking uphill (maximizing) or downhill (minimizing). This metaphor gives you vocabulary for the things that make local search hard: global and local peaks, valleys, flat plateaus, shoulders that look flat but lead somewhere, and ridges that force awkward sideways progress. Those features are exactly the failure modes hill climbing and simulated annealing are designed to cope with.

As you watch the videos below, focus on three questions. First, how is a state represented, and what single change produces a neighbor? Second, what function is being maximized or minimized, and is it a true objective or a heuristic estimate of cost? Third, what does the surface being drawn on screen actually represent — which axes are decision variables and which axis is the score? Keeping those distinctions straight will make the hill-climbing variants in the rest of the module much easier to follow.

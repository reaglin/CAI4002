Foundations: Agents, Environments, and Agent Programs

▶

Artificial intelligence, viewed from an engineering standpoint, is the study of how to build agents — entities that perceive an environment through sensors and act upon that environment through actuators. This framing is deliberately broad. A thermostat is an agent; so is a warehouse robot, a spam filter, a chess engine, an autonomous vehicle, and arguably a human being. What makes the framing useful is not that it draws a sharp line between "agents" and "non-agents," but that it gives us a shared vocabulary for specifying what a system should do, separately from how we will make it do that. This topic builds that vocabulary from the ground up: percepts and percept sequences, the agent function and the agent program, the architecture that hosts the program, the PEAS method for describing a task environment, the properties of environments that constrain design, and finally the reasoning chain that leads from an environment analysis to a defensible architectural choice.

The central discipline this vocabulary enforces is design before implementation. A great many AI projects fail not because the algorithm was weak but because nobody stated precisely what counted as success, what the system could actually observe, or what it was physically permitted to do. The concepts below are the antidote to that failure mode.

What Is a Rational Agent?

An agent is anything that can be viewed as perceiving its environment through sensors and acting upon that environment through actuators. The sensors deliver percepts — the raw informational inputs available at a moment in time. The actuators execute actions, and those actions change the state of the environment, which in turn changes what future percepts will contain. This creates the fundamental closed loop of agency:

  environment state
        |
     [sensors]  --> percept --> [agent program] --> action --> [actuators]
        ^                                                            |
        |____________________ changes state __________________________|

A vacuum-cleaning robot might have a dirt sensor, a bump sensor, and a wheel-odometry sensor; its actuators are two drive motors, a suction motor, and perhaps a brush. A trading agent's "sensors" are market data feeds and news APIs; its "actuators" are order-submission calls to an exchange. A medical triage assistant's sensors are structured patient records and lab results; its actuators are the recommendations it writes into a chart. The physical form varies enormously; the loop does not.

The key normative concept is rationality. A rational agent is one that, for each possible percept sequence, selects the action that is expected to maximize its performance measure, given the evidence provided by the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge the agent has. Unpacking that definition carefully pays dividends, because each clause rules out a common misconception.

  • "For each possible percept sequence" — rationality is evaluated over the agent's entire behavioral repertoire, not over one lucky episode. An agent that behaves well on the inputs it happened to receive but would behave absurdly on a slightly different input is not rational; it is fortunate.
  • "Expected to maximize" — the standard is expectation, computed with the information available at decision time. It is not "the action that, in hindsight, turned out best."
  • "Performance measure" — an externally specified criterion of success. The agent does not get to grade its own homework.
  • "Given the evidence and built-in knowledge" — an agent cannot be faulted for failing to use information it had no way of obtaining.

A critical design rule follows from the third clause: the performance measure should be defined over environment states, not over the agent's internal beliefs about how well it is doing. If you reward a cleaning robot for its own internal "dirt collected" counter, you have created an incentive for the robot to dump dirt on the floor and re-collect it — a behavior that maximizes the stated measure while defeating the purpose. If you reward it instead for the proportion of floor tiles that are clean, averaged over the working day, the perverse strategy earns nothing. The same trap appears everywhere: a recommender optimized for "clicks the system logged" rather than "user satisfaction" learns to produce clickbait; a dialogue agent optimized for "user said thank you" learns sycophancy. Specifying the measure over the world rather than over the agent's self-report is the first and cheapest defense against misaligned behavior.

Designing the measure is itself subtle, because the measure encodes trade-offs you may not have noticed you were making. Consider the vacuum world again. "Amount of dirt collected in an eight-hour shift" rewards thoroughness but also rewards noise, energy waste, and the dirt-dumping exploit. "Average cleanliness over time, minus one point per unit of electricity, minus a large penalty for colliding with a pet" is a richer measure that expresses the real goal. Notice that the designer, not the agent, is responsible for these clauses. Rationality is always rationality relative to a measure; an agent cannot be more aligned than its measure.

Rationality is not omniscience, and it is not perfection. An omniscient agent would know the actual outcome of each action in advance; no real agent can. Suppose you are crossing a wide, empty street in clear daylight, having looked both ways. Mid-crossing, a cargo door falls from a passing aircraft and flattens you. Your decision to cross was rational — it maximized expected utility given the available evidence — even though the outcome was catastrophic. We can reasonably criticize an agent for failing to gather cheap, relevant information, or for ignoring evidence it had; we cannot reasonably criticize it for not predicting the unpredictable. Rationality maximizes expected performance; perfection maximizes actual performance. Only the first is a buildable engineering target.

Two further behaviors fall out of the definition as consequences rather than add-ons. First, information gathering, or exploration, is rational whenever knowledge is incomplete and the information is useful enough to justify its cost. Looking both ways before crossing produces no immediate progress toward the goal of reaching the other side; it is rational because it modifies the expected value of the subsequent action. A robot that nudges a door to learn whether it is locked, a medical agent that orders a diagnostic test, a reinforcement-learning agent that occasionally takes a non-greedy action — all are performing the same computation: paying a small known cost to reduce uncertainty that is costing more. Second, learning is rational whenever the environment is unknown or changing. An agent whose built-in knowledge is a complete and permanently accurate account of its world need not learn; no such world exists outside of toy problems. Where the designer's prior knowledge is partial, an agent that fails to update from experience is systematically leaving performance on the table. We therefore say an agent is autonomous to the extent that its behavior is determined by its own experience rather than purely by the designer's built-in assumptions. A reasonable engineering compromise is to ship an agent with enough built-in knowledge to survive its first hours and enough learning capacity to improve thereafter.

Percepts, Percept Sequences, and the Agent Function

A percept is the agent's perceptual input at a single instant — a single snapshot of whatever the sensors report. A percept sequence is the complete history of everything the agent has ever perceived, in order. We write a percept sequence as an element of P*, the set of all finite sequences over the percept set P.

The distinction matters enormously, because an agent's choice of action at any moment can depend on the entire percept sequence, but never on anything it has not perceived. This is the formal statement of the limits of knowledge. If a fact about the world has never shown up in any percept and is not built into the agent, the agent's action cannot be a function of it.

The agent function is the mathematical object that captures behavior completely:

f : P* --> A

It maps every possible percept sequence to the action the agent will take in response. The agent function is an abstract, external description: it says nothing about memory layout, data structures, or algorithms. It is behavior as seen from outside.

Consider the classic two-square vacuum world. Squares A and B may each be Clean or Dirty; the agent perceives its current location and whether that square is dirty; the available actions are Left, Right, Suck, and NoOp. A partial tabulation of one reasonable agent function looks like this:

Percept sequenceAction
[A, Clean]Right
[A, Dirty]Suck
[B, Clean]Left
[B, Dirty]Suck
[A, Clean], [A, Clean]Right
[A, Clean], [A, Dirty]Suck
[A, Dirty], [A, Clean]Right
[A, Dirty], [A, Clean], [B, Clean]NoOp
… (indefinitely many longer sequences)…

Two features of this table deserve attention. First, it is already unwieldy for a world with two squares and two cleanliness states. Second, and more importantly, it is infinite: percept sequences can be arbitrarily long, so P* is countably infinite even when P is tiny. The agent function therefore defines behavior exhaustively but is, in general, unimplementable as a literal lookup table. It is a specification device, not an implementation.

From the external definition follows a sharp criterion of equivalence: two agents with identical agent functions are behaviorally equivalent, regardless of their internal design. Suppose Agent X implements the vacuum behavior above with a four-line if cascade, while Agent Y implements it with a deep neural network, and Agent Z with a 10,000-entry hash table. If, for every percept sequence, all three emit the same action, then no external observer interacting with them through the environment can distinguish them. They may differ dramatically in memory footprint, response latency, energy use, explainability, and maintainability — and those differences are the proper subject of architecture selection — but they do not differ in behavior, and therefore not in performance as measured over environment states. This is a liberating fact: it means we can argue about what the agent should do without yet committing to how.

That separation is the practical payoff of the concept. Specifying the agent function first divorces the behavioral contract from the computational method. In a design review, you can write down, in English or pseudocode or a partial table, what the system ought to do in each situation of interest — including the awkward edge cases — and get stakeholders to agree. Only then do you ask the engineering question: which program, running on which architecture, approximates this function closely enough, fast enough, and cheaply enough? Projects that skip the first step tend to discover, late and expensively, that the team disagreed about the intended behavior all along.

A useful exercise is to notice when the agent function genuinely requires history. For the fully observable two-square vacuum, the "right" action depends only on the last percept, so a history-free program suffices. But suppose the agent has no location sensor and perceives only Dirty or Clean. Now [Clean] is ambiguous — the agent might be in A or B — and the correct action may depend on how many moves it has made and in which direction. The agent function now genuinely depends on the sequence, not just the last element, and that fact will dictate the architecture.

Agent Program vs. Agent Architecture

The agent function is abstract. To build something real we need two concrete things, and it is worth being pedantic about which is which:

  • The agent architecture is the computing device with its physical sensors and actuators — the body and the hardware. It supplies the sensor suite, the actuator suite, the processor, memory, power budget, operating system, and real-time guarantees.
  • The agent program is the software that runs on that architecture: it accepts percepts and returns actions.

The standard slogan is agent = architecture + program. A path-planning program that is excellent on a laptop is useless if deployed on an architecture whose only sensor is a bump switch, because the percepts it needs do not exist. Conversely, a lavishly instrumented robot with a trivial program will behave trivially. Neither half determines behavior on its own.

There is an important interface detail that trips up beginners. The agent program receives only the current percept as an argument, not the percept sequence. This is a deliberate modeling convention that mirrors reality: sensors report now, not then. Consequently, if the agent's behavior must depend on history, the program must store that history itself, in internal state that persists between calls. A generic skeleton makes the point:

function TABLE-DRIVEN-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: percepts, a sequence, initially empty
                table, a table of actions indexed by percept sequences

    append percept to the end of percepts
    action <- LOOKUP(percepts, table)
    return action

Note the persistent declaration: the variable percepts survives across invocations. That single mechanism — persistent internal state — is what allows a program whose interface is memoryless to implement an agent function whose definition is history-dependent.

The table-driven agent is also the canonical example of why naive implementations fail. A literal table-lookup implementation is intractable. If P is the set of possible percepts and the agent runs for T steps, the table needs an entry for every sequence of length 1 through T, which is

|P|^1 + |P|^2 + ... + |P|^T  =  O(|P|^T)  entries

The numbers become absurd immediately. Consider a modest automated taxi with a single camera producing 640×480 pixels at 24-bit colour, 30 frames per second. One frame alone can take roughly 224×640×480 values; the number of distinct percept sequences for even one hour of driving exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe by an unimaginable margin. Even in chess — a tiny, discrete, fully observable world — the number of reachable positions is on the order of 1040+, far beyond any conceivable storage. Scale illustrates the point:

Beyond storage, a literal table has three further fatal defects: no designer could ever write all the entries; no agent could learn them all from a feasible amount of experience; and the table offers no generalization — two nearly identical percept sequences are unrelated entries, so experience in one tells the agent nothing about the other.

The entire enterprise of AI programming can therefore be described as compressing the agent function — producing a short program that behaves like a huge table. The module's architectures are exactly five families of compression strategy:

  • Condition–action rules compress by ignoring history and grouping percepts into a handful of situations (if car-in-front-is-braking then initiate-braking).
  • Internal models compress by summarizing an unbounded percept history into a compact state estimate plus a transition model.
  • Goals compress by replacing enumerated responses with a target condition plus a search procedure that derives the response on demand.
  • Utility functions compress by replacing enumerated preferences with a scalar scoring function over states, from which preferences are computed.
  • Learned parameters compress by fitting a parametric function (weights of a network, coefficients of a linear policy) that generalizes across unseen percepts.

Each buys compression at a different price in computation, data, assumptions, and explainability — which is precisely why architecture selection is a genuine engineering decision rather than a matter of taste.

Finally, note how architecture choice determines what the program can retain and reason over. An 8-bit microcontroller with 2 KB of RAM cannot maintain a probabilistic belief distribution over a large state space, so a model-based design is off the table regardless of its theoretical merit; you will be writing reflex rules. A platform without a clock cannot reason about elapsed time. A robot whose only sensor is a bump switch cannot maintain a map except by dead-reckoning, with drift. If the architecture provides a wheel encoder and an IMU, the same program becomes viable. Architecture is thus not a downstream implementation detail: it is an upstream constraint that prunes the space of realizable agent functions.

The PEAS Framework for Task Environments

Before choosing an architecture, you must describe the problem. The standard tool is PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. Writing a PEAS specification is the first concrete deliverable of agent design, and it is cheap, fast, and remarkably effective at exposing muddled thinking.

  • Performance measure — the criteria that define success, stated in terms of environment states over the relevant time horizon. Good measures are explicit about trade-offs and about horizon ("averaged over a shift," "per trading day," "per delivery"). Bad measures are vague ("be helpful") or self-referential ("maximize the agent's confidence score").
  • Environment — the world the agent operates in, including terrain, objects, physical laws, regulations, and crucially other agents (cooperative, competitive, or indifferent). Omitting other agents is a frequent and expensive mistake, because their presence changes the environment class from single-agent to multi-agent and often from static to dynamic.
  • Actuators — an enumeration of the actions the agent can physically perform. This is a hard constraint. If "notify a human supervisor" is not in the actuator list, no amount of clever reasoning will make it available.
  • Sensors — an enumeration of the percepts the agent can actually obtain, with their resolution, latency, noise characteristics, and failure modes. Again a hard constraint: an agent cannot condition on what it cannot sense.

Notice how much design information is already visible in these tables, before a single line of code is written. The delivery robot's performance measure weights pedestrian safety heavily, which tells us immediately that a purely greedy "shortest path" policy is wrong and that a utility formulation with a large negative term — or a hard safety constraint layered beneath the planner — is required. The trading agent's environment entry mentions adversarial algorithmic counterparties, which signals a multi-agent, partially observable, stochastic setting where game-theoretic reasoning and conservative risk limits matter more than point forecasts. The deepest value of PEAS, though, is that writing it early exposes gaps — especially goals that cannot be measured by the available sensors. Read the Performance row and the Sensors row side by side and ask, for each clause of the measure, "can the agent (or the evaluator) actually observe this?" Mismatches of three kinds fall out:

  • Unobservable goal clauses. The vacuum's measure includes "fraction of floor clean." But the dirt sensor only reports dirt under the robot, right now. The robot cannot directly perceive global cleanliness; it must infer it from a map plus a history of where it has been and when — which is a direct argument for a model-based architecture. If you cannot afford that, you must either accept a proxy measure (coverage of the floor plan) or add a sensor.
  • Goals requiring unavailable actions. Suppose the delivery robot's measure penalizes undelivered parcels, but the actuator list has no way to contact a human when a lobby door is locked. The agent is structurally incapable of achieving the goal in a common case; the fix is an actuator ("send message to operator"), not an algorithm.
  • Measures the agent can game. If the trading measure were "realized profit this week" with no drawdown term, the rational agent would take on enormous hidden tail risk. Writing the measure in a table next to the environment description makes such omissions conspicuous.

A practical habit: after drafting PEAS, write one sentence per performance clause explaining which sensor stream supplies the evidence for it and which actuator can influence it. Any clause that fails this test is a specification defect, and it is far cheaper to find it now than after deployment.

Environment Properties That Constrain Design

Task environments vary along a small number of dimensions, and those dimensions have direct, predictable consequences for what kind of agent program can possibly work. The dimensions are properties of the environment as the agent experiences it, which means they depend on the sensor suite as much as on the world itself — a point that is easy to miss.

Fully versus partially observable. An environment is fully observable if the agent's sensors give it access, at each point in time, to all aspects of the environment state that are relevant to the choice of action. It is partially observable if noise, limited coverage, or missing instrumentation hide relevant aspects; it is unobservable in the extreme case where the agent perceives nothing at all. Chess with a board reader is fully observable; poker, with hidden cards, is not; a robot with a forward-facing camera cannot see what is behind it and so operates partially observably. The design consequence is sharp and non-negotiable: partial observability forces the agent to maintain internal state, a running estimate of the parts of the world it cannot currently see. That requirement rules out simple reflex agents, which by construction map the current percept to an action. The classic demonstration is the vacuum robot with a dirt sensor but no location sensor: the percept Clean is consistent with being in square A or square B, and no fixed rule from Clean to an action can be rational in both cases. A deterministic reflex agent either stalls or oscillates forever; the only reflex fix is randomization, which is a poor substitute for knowing where you are.

Deterministic versus stochastic. An environment is deterministic if the next state is completely determined by the current state and the agent's action; otherwise it is stochastic. (A third label, nondeterministic, is used when outcomes are uncertain but no probabilities are attached — the agent must then plan for the worst case rather than the average case.) A sliding-tile puzzle is deterministic; a robot's wheels slipping on a wet floor, a parcel recipient who may or may not be home, and a limit order that may or may not be filled are stochastic. The design consequence is that stochastic outcomes motivate reasoning over expected value rather than guaranteed outcomes. A goal-based agent asks "does this plan reach the goal?" — a question with no clean answer when every action has a distribution of outcomes. A utility-based agent asks "which action maximizes expected utility?", a question that remains well posed. This is exactly why uncertainty pushes designs up the ladder from goals to utilities. Note also that an environment can appear stochastic merely because it is partially observable: a deterministic world whose hidden variables you cannot see is, from your vantage point, unpredictable. The two properties interact rather than decomposing neatly.

Episodic versus sequential. In an episodic environment, the agent's experience divides into atomic episodes; in each, it receives a percept and performs one action, and crucially the action taken in one episode does not affect later episodes. A defect-classification agent on a production line is episodic: misjudging part 400 does not change part 401. In a sequential environment, current decisions influence all future ones — chess, driving, inventory management, and conversation are all sequential. The design consequence is that sequential environments require lookahead: the agent must reason about consequences that unfold over time, which is the province of search, planning, and multi-step value estimation. Episodic tasks need none of that machinery, which is why they can often be handled by a classifier wrapped in a thin reflex layer. A useful warning: tasks that look episodic sometimes are not. A recommender that treats each impression independently is ignoring the fact that today's recommendations shape tomorrow's user interests — a sequential effect masquerading as an episodic one.

Static versus dynamic. A static environment does not change while the agent is deliberating; a dynamic one does. A crossword puzzle is static; driving is emphatically dynamic. The intermediate case, semi-dynamic, describes environments where the world does not change but the agent's score does — chess with a clock is the standard example, since the position is stable while you think but your time is draining. The design consequence is a hard bound on deliberation: in dynamic environments, thinking is an action with a cost, and a perfect answer delivered too late is worse than a good answer delivered on time. This favors architectures with fast reactive components — a reflex-level safety layer that can brake instantly — possibly combined with a slower deliberative layer that refines plans in the background. Many real robots use exactly this layered design, and the split exists because of the static/dynamic axis.

Discrete versus continuous. This distinction applies separately to the state of the environment, to time, and to the agent's percepts and actions. Chess is discrete in all three; taxi driving has continuous state (positions, velocities), continuous time, and continuous actions (steering angle, throttle). The design consequence lies in representation and algorithm choice: discrete domains admit enumeration, tabular methods, and classical graph search; continuous domains demand function approximation, numerical optimization, differential models, or deliberate discretization — and discretization introduces its own error. Many practical systems compromise: a self-driving stack may plan over a discrete lattice of candidate trajectories, then hand the chosen trajectory to a continuous controller.

Single-agent versus multi-agent. If other entities in the environment are best modelled as agents maximizing their own performance measures — particularly measures that depend on your behavior — the environment is multi-agent. Sub-classify it as competitive (chess, adversarial trading, where one agent's gain is another's loss), cooperative (a fleet of warehouse robots avoiding deadlock), or mixed (driving, where everyone cooperates on collision avoidance and competes for road space). The design consequences are substantial: competitive settings make randomized strategies rational (predictability is exploitable), demand adversarial search or game-theoretic equilibrium reasoning, and typically make the environment both dynamic and partially observable, since other agents' intentions are hidden. Communication and convention also become possible actions.

Known versus unknown. This axis is often confused with observability, but it is categorically different: known/unknown refers to the designer's or agent's knowledge of the rules — the outcomes (or outcome probabilities) of actions — while observable/partially observable refers to the agent's access to the current state. The combinations are all realizable: a new video game with full on-screen information is fully observable but unknown (you must learn what the buttons do); solitaire with face-down cards is known but partially observable (you know the rules perfectly, you just cannot see the cards). The design consequence is the motivation for the entire learning paradigm: an unknown environment requires the agent to learn a model, a value function, or a policy from experience, and to explore in order to obtain that experience. If the designer cannot write down the transition model — because the world is too complex, too variable, or simply not understood — then the agent must acquire it.

From Environment Analysis to Architecture Selection

The payoff of all the preceding machinery is a repeatable decision procedure. Write the PEAS specification; classify the environment on each axis; then let the hardest constraint drive the architecture. The guiding principle is choose the simplest architecture whose capabilities cover the environment's demands — unnecessary sophistication costs compute, data, latency, and debuggability, while insufficient sophistication produces an agent that is structurally incapable of rational behavior no matter how well it is tuned.

Simple reflex agents apply condition–action rules to the current percept: if dirty then suck; if car-ahead-is-braking then brake. They are appropriate when the environment is fully observable (so the current percept carries all relevant information), when the task is episodic or close to it (so no lookahead is needed), and when the correct behavior can be expressed as a small set of rules. Their virtues are extreme speed, tiny memory footprint, and transparency — you can read every rule and audit it, which is why safety interlocks are almost always implemented this way. Their failure mode is equally sharp: given partial observability, they cannot distinguish states that look alike, and they fall into infinite loops. The locationless vacuum is the textbook demonstration. A thermostat, an industrial emergency-stop circuit, and a packet filter that accepts or drops based on header fields are all legitimate simple reflex agents, and in each case the simplicity is a feature.

Model-based reflex agents add internal state updated by two pieces of knowledge: a transition model ("how the world evolves, and how my actions change it") and a sensor model ("how the world's state is reflected in my percepts"). The state is a running best guess — a map, a tracked object list, an occupancy grid, a probability distribution — that summarizes the percept history. This is the architecture mandated whenever there is hidden state: unobservable regions, sensor noise, objects temporarily occluded, or facts (like "which squares have I already cleaned") that are simply not sensed. A real vacuum robot building a SLAM map so it can cover the floor systematically, a driving stack tracking a cyclist who has disappeared behind a van, a network monitor maintaining per-connection state — all are model-based. The cost is real: you must author or learn the models, state estimation consumes memory and compute, and errors in the model accumulate as drift. But without internal state, the agent literally cannot represent the distinction it needs to act on.

Goal-based agents add an explicit representation of desirable situations and use search or planning to find action sequences that achieve them. They are called for in sequential tasks with explicit targets, where the right action now depends on consequences several steps ahead. The decisive advantage is flexibility: because the goal is represented declaratively and separately from the behavior, changing the destination requires changing one data item rather than rewriting rules. A delivery robot asked to go to 14 Oak Street instead of 7 Elm Street needs no code change; a reflex agent encoding routes as rules would need a rewrite. A route planner, a block-stacking planner, and a chess engine searching for checkmate are all goal-based. The costs are computational — search can be expensive — and representational, since you must model actions and their effects well enough to simulate them.

Utility-based agents replace the binary "goal achieved / not achieved" test with a utility function that assigns a real-valued score to states (or histories), and they choose actions that maximize expected utility. Two circumstances make this necessary. First, conflicting or graded objectives: a delivery robot's route can be fast, cheap, or safe, and no binary goal predicate expresses the trade-off among them; a utility function with weighted terms does. Second, uncertainty: when actions have stochastic outcomes, "will this plan reach the goal?" has no yes/no answer, but "what is the expected utility of this plan?" does, and maximizing it is the decision-theoretically correct response. Utility functions also naturally encode risk attitude — a concave utility over money produces risk-averse trading behavior automatically, without a special rule. The trade-off is that eliciting and calibrating the utility function is genuinely hard, and a badly specified utility is the most direct route to confidently wrong behavior; the mapping from stated measure to implemented utility must be checked carefully against the PEAS performance row.

Learning agents add four interacting components — a performance element (the agent program chosen from the above families, which selects actions), a critic (which judges the agent's behavior against a fixed external performance standard and produces feedback), a learning element (which uses that feedback to modify the performance element), and a problem generator (which proposes exploratory, deliberately suboptimal actions to gather informative experience). This architecture is indicated whenever the environment is unknown, too complex to model by hand, or changing over time, and whenever the designer cannot anticipate every situation. A trading agent whose market regime shifts, a recommender whose user population drifts, a robot whose motors wear and whose calibration degrades — all argue for learning. The costs are data requirements, non-stationarity of the agent's own behavior (which complicates testing), reduced predictability, and the need for safe exploration: a problem generator that experiments freely on a sidewalk robot is a hazard, so exploration must be sandboxed, simulated, or tightly bounded.

In practice these are layers rather than rivals. A production self-driving stack contains all five: a reflex-level collision-avoidance interlock that can brake within milliseconds; a model-based perception and tracking module maintaining state about occluded road users; a goal-based route planner; a utility-based trajectory selector trading comfort, progress, and risk; and offline learning components that improve perception and prediction from fleet data. The environmentanalysis does not pick one box; it tells you which capabilities must be present somewhere in the stack, and the engineering judgement lies in deciding which layer supplies each capability and how they arbitrate when they disagree.

A worked example makes the procedure concrete. Return to the sidewalk delivery robot. Its PEAS performance row weights on-time delivery, energy, customer rating, and — heavily — pedestrian safety. The environment classification is partially observable (occlusions, limited sensor range, GPS dropout in urban canyons), stochastic (pedestrians are unpredictable, wheels slip in rain), sequential (a wrong turn costs minutes later), dynamic (the world moves while the planner thinks), continuous (positions, velocities, steering), multi-agent and mixed (pedestrians, cyclists, other robots), and partly unknown (pedestrian behavior cannot be fully specified in advance). Read off the consequences in order: partial observability rules out a pure reflex design and mandates internal state; sequentiality mandates lookahead, so a planner is required; graded conflicting objectives plus stochasticity mandate a utility formulation rather than a binary goal test; dynamism mandates a fast reactive safety layer beneath the planner, because a 300 ms planning cycle is too slow for an emergency stop; unknown pedestrian dynamics mandate a learned prediction model with a carefully sandboxed problem generator — exploration happens in simulation and in logged data, never by experimenting on a live pavement. The resulting architecture is not a matter of preference; nearly every element of it was forced by a specific line of the environment analysis.

Contrast this with a production-line defect classifier whose PEAS analysis yields fully observable (the part is fully imaged), episodic (parts are independent), effectively static during the inspection window, single-agent, and known enough that labelled data exists. Here the forced capabilities are far fewer: no internal state across episodes, no lookahead, no utility trade-off beyond a decision threshold balancing false accepts against false rejects. A learned classifier wrapped in a thin reflex layer — if defect-probability > threshold then divert part — is the right answer, and deploying a planner here would add cost, latency, and failure modes while improving nothing. Restraint is as much a part of good architecture selection as sophistication.

Finally, a note on how to defend a choice. A justification is only complete when it names the alternatives considered and the specific environment property that disqualified each. "We used a model-based agent" is an assertion; "we used a model-based agent because the dirt sensor cannot report previously cleaned squares, so a simple reflex agent cannot distinguish a freshly cleaned room from an unvisited one and would re-cover ground indefinitely; we rejected a full planner because the floor plan is small and greedy coverage over the map meets the performance target at a fraction of the compute budget" is an argument. The vocabulary of this topic — percepts, percept sequences, agent functions, programs, architectures, PEAS, and the environment axes — exists precisely so that such arguments can be stated crisply and checked by someone else.

Simple Reflex Agents

▶

The simple reflex agent is the most austere of the standard agent architectures: it looks at what it is sensing right now and acts. Nothing is remembered, nothing is predicted, and nothing is weighed against alternatives. Despite this poverty of mechanism, the architecture is far from a toy. It describes thermostats, over-current trip circuits, collision-avoidance bumpers on warehouse robots, spam filters that act on a single message's features, and the low-level reflex layers inside otherwise sophisticated autonomous systems. Studying it carefully is valuable for two reasons: it isolates the minimum machinery needed for rational behaviour in a restricted class of environments, and its failure modes motivate, with unusual clarity, every more elaborate architecture that follows.

Definition and core principle. A simple reflex agent implements an agent program that is a direct mapping from the current percept to an action. The mapping is expressed as a set of condition–action rules, conventionally written if <condition> then <action> and also called production rules or situation–action rules. Formally, where a general agent function has the signature f : P* → A (mapping a whole percept sequence to an action), a simple reflex agent collapses this to f : P → A, mapping only the most recent percept. That collapse is the defining commitment of the architecture, and every advantage and every limitation flows from it.

Three consequences follow immediately:

  • The agent is memoryless. No internal state persists between perception–action cycles. If the agent visits the same percept twice, separated by an hour or by a single tick, it behaves identically on both occasions. It cannot know that it has "already tried that", that a door was open a moment ago, or that it is halfway through a multi-step procedure.
  • The agent needs no world model. It carries no transition model describing how the environment evolves on its own, and no action model describing the effects of its own behaviour. It never asks "what will happen if I do this?" — the rule simply fires. This is why the architecture is cheap, and also why it cannot plan.
  • The rules are authored, not learned. In the classical formulation, the designer writes the condition–action rules by hand, encoding domain knowledge directly into the program. The agent performs no credit assignment and has no learning element, so its competence is fixed at design time and is exactly as good as its designer's analysis of the task.

The canonical illustration is the two-square vacuum world. The agent perceives a pair (location, status) and the whole agent program is three rules:

function REFLEX-VACUUM-AGENT([location, status]) returns an action
    if status = Dirty      then return Suck
    else if location = A   then return Right
    else if location = B   then return Left

This program is rational — it maximises the expected performance measure of "squares cleaned per time step, penalising movement" — in the specific environment for which it was designed. Note how little it knows: it has no idea how many squares exist, whether dirt reappears, or what it did on the previous step. It simply cleans what is under it and otherwise shuttles.

The implementation pattern. Textbook pseudocode for the architecture separates perception, matching, and action into three crisp stages:

function SIMPLE-REFLEX-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: rules, a set of condition-action rules

    state ← INTERPRET-INPUT(percept)
    rule  ← RULE-MATCH(state, rules)
    action ← rule.ACTION
    return action

Each stage carries real engineering weight:

  • INTERPRET-INPUT turns raw sensor data into a symbolic state description that rules can talk about. A camera frame of 2 million pixels is reduced to CarInFrontIsBraking = true; a 12-bit ADC reading becomes Temperature = Too-Hot. This abstraction step is where most of the engineering difficulty actually lives: the rules are trivial, but defining the features that make them expressible is not. Note that state here means a description of the current percept, not a remembered state of the world — it is derived afresh each cycle and discarded.
  • RULE-MATCH scans the rule base and returns the first rule whose condition is satisfied by the interpreted state. Matching may be a simple linear scan over predicates, or something far more efficient (see below).
  • Conflict resolution. When several conditions match simultaneously, the agent needs a deterministic policy for choosing among them. The simplest scheme is textual ordering: the first matching rule wins, so the designer places specific and safety-critical rules above general ones. Alternatives include explicit numeric priorities, specificity ordering (the rule with the most conditions wins), and recency. Whatever the scheme, it must be stated, because without it the agent's behaviour is underspecified and therefore unverifiable. In an autonomous vehicle's reflex layer, if obstacle_within_2m then emergency_brake must dominate if lane_clear then accelerate, and ordering is what guarantees it.
  • Default/fallback rule. A well-formed rule base ends with a catch-all condition that matches anything, so that no percept can leave the agent without an action. The fallback should be the safe action for the domain — halt, maintain_current_output, raise_alarm — never an undefined or null return. Omitting the default is one of the most common defects in practice: the system behaves correctly for months and then encounters an unanticipated sensor combination and silently does nothing.
  • Lookup implementation. Logically the rule set is a function from percepts to actions, so it can be realised in several ways with very different cost profiles. A full lookup table indexes every possible percept and gives O(1) access, but its size is the product of the ranges of all percept variables and becomes intractable fast. A hashed rule index keys rules on a discriminating feature so only a handful are ever tested. A decision tree evaluates a logarithmic number of tests and is the usual compromise. A linear scan over an ordered list is simplest and fine when there are tens of rules. In hardware, the whole thing may be compiled into combinational logic or a programmable logic array, giving a decision in a single clock cycle.

A small worked example — a reflex rule base for an automatic door controller — shows the pattern end to end:

OrderCondition (interpreted percept)ActionRationale
1obstruction_in_doorwayHoldOpenSafety rule, must dominate all others
2motion_detected AND door_closedOpenPrimary service behaviour
3no_motion AND door_openCloseEnergy conservation
4fire_alarm_activeHoldOpenWould be unreachable if placed below rule 3 — ordering matters
5true (default)NoOpFallback so every percept yields a defined action

Rule 4 deliberately illustrates a defect: because rule 3 can fire first in a no-motion situation, the fire-alarm rule may never be reached. In a real design it belongs at position 1 or 2. Detecting this class of bug by inspection is easy with five rules and essentially impossible with five hundred — a point we return to under scaling.

Advantages: speed and simplicity. The architecture's virtues are exactly the ones you want in embedded and safety-critical control:

  • Bounded, predictable decision latency. With a table or hashed index the decision is O(1); with a decision tree it is O(log n); with a linear scan it is O(n) over a small fixed n. In all cases the worst case is known at design time, which is precisely what a hard real-time system requires. A deliberative planner, by contrast, may take milliseconds or seconds depending on how hard the search instance turns out to be, and such variability is unacceptable for an airbag trigger or a motor-current trip.
  • Minimal memory footprint. No history buffer, no belief state, no search frontier. The agent needs storage for the rules and one percept. This makes the architecture viable on 8-bit microcontrollers with kilobytes of RAM, and it eliminates an entire class of memory-growth and stale-state bugs.
  • Transparency and verifiability. Behaviour is a finite, inspectable mapping. One can enumerate the percept space (when it is small), exhaustively test every input, prove coverage and non-contradiction with simple static analysis, and present the rule table to a certification authority. In regulated domains — avionics (DO-178C), medical devices (IEC 62304), industrial safety (IEC 61508) — this auditability is worth more than cleverness. "I can show you every input and the action it produces" is an argument that a neural policy or an online planner cannot easily make.
  • Graceful degradation as a safety layer. Reflex agents compose well beneath deliberative components in a layered or subsumption-style architecture. The planner proposes a trajectory; the reflex layer retains authority to veto it when a proximity sensor trips. If the planner hangs, crashes, or produces nonsense, the reflex layer still brakes. Nearly every fielded autonomous system — industrial robots, drones, self-driving cars — has this structure, which is why the architecture remains practically important rather than merely historically interesting.

The requirement of full observability. The sharpest statement of the architecture's precondition is this: a simple reflex agent can be rational only if the correct action is a function of the current percept alone. That requires the environment to be fully observable with respect to the task — the percept must carry all the information needed to choose correctly.

When that fails, we get perceptual aliasing: two distinct world states produce the same percept but demand different actions, and the agent, having no way to distinguish them, must get at least one of them wrong. Examples:

  • A vacuum agent with a local dirt sensor but no location sensor perceives only [Dirty] or [Clean]. On perceiving [Clean] it cannot tell whether it is in square A (where the right action is Right) or square B (where it is Left). Any fixed rule is wrong half the time.
  • A delivery robot in a building with identical corridors sees the same camera view at two junctions that require opposite turns.
  • A car's automatic braking system that perceives only "object ahead" cannot distinguish a stationary vehicle from a steam plume or an overhead sign — states that are visually aliased at low resolution but call for very different responses.
  • A thermostat reading 20°C cannot tell whether the room is warming or cooling; that distinction requires the previous reading, i.e. memory.

Note the characteristic pathology: the agent does not hesitate or report uncertainty. It matches a rule and acts confidently and wrongly. Hidden variables and missing sensors do not produce visible error signals; they produce silent misclassification. This is why "the system seemed fine in testing" is such a common prelude to reflex-agent failure — the aliased state simply did not arise during test.

There are two routes out. The first is architectural: add internal state, which abandons the simple reflex design. The second is sensory: add sensors until the percept disambiguates the states. Giving the vacuum agent a location sensor restores full observability and the three-rule program becomes rational again; giving the delivery robot an RFID reader or a ceiling-fiducial camera makes the identical corridors distinguishable. This second route is often the right engineering answer, because a cheap sensor is usually cheaper than a state-estimation subsystem and preserves all the verifiability benefits. The design question is therefore not merely "which architecture?" but "can I buy observability instead of complexity?"

Failure mode: infinite loops. Determinism plus memorylessness has a structural consequence. If the agent returns to a percept it has seen before, and the environment is deterministic, it will take the same action and arrive at the same successor state, and so on forever. The behaviour is not a bug in any individual rule; it is a theorem about the architecture.

The classic case is a reflex agent in the aliased vacuum world described above. Suppose its rule is if Clean then Right. Starting in square B (clean), it moves right, bumps the wall, stays in B, perceives [Clean] again, and moves right again — forever, never discovering the dirt in A. A wall-following robot whose rules are if wall_on_left then forward; if no_wall_on_left then turn_left will circle a free-standing pillar indefinitely. A trading bot keyed to a single price threshold can thrash buy/sell around that threshold.

Three responses, in increasing order of principle:

  • Randomisation. Replace a deterministic rule with a stochastic one: if Clean then choose uniformly from {Left, Right}. This breaks the symmetry that sustains the loop. In the aliased vacuum world the randomised agent will reach and clean every square with probability 1, and its expected performance strictly dominates the deterministic agent's. Randomised reflex agents are genuinely useful — they also make an agent harder to exploit in adversarial settings such as game playing or security patrolling, where a predictable policy can be gamed.
  • But randomisation is a workaround, not a solution. It adds no knowledge. The agent still does not know where it is or where it has been; it merely stumbles out of traps eventually rather than never. Expected completion time can be poor (a random walk's expected cover time grows quadratically in the number of states for a line, and worse in general), and the behaviour is no longer deterministically verifiable — a serious cost in certified systems. It converts a guaranteed failure into a probabilistic, slow success.
  • The principled fix is internal state. If the agent keeps even a single bit of memory — "the last direction I moved" or "I have already visited A" — the loop dissolves, because the agent's decision now depends on the percept sequence rather than the percept. Adding that persistent state, together with a model of how the world evolves and how the agent's own actions change it, is exactly the definition of the model-based reflex agent. The infinite-loop failure mode is thus the standard pedagogical bridge from the simplest architecture to the next one.

Scaling limits of rule sets. Even in a fully observable, deterministic world, the architecture runs into a representational ceiling.

  • Combinatorial growth. The number of distinct percepts is the product of the ranges of the percept variables. Ten Boolean sensors give 210 = 1,024 percepts; twenty give over a million; a modest robot with a dozen continuous sensors discretised into ten bins each gives 1012. A full lookup table is then physically impossible, and even a compact rule set must cover the space with generalising conditions that the designer must get right. This is why Russell and Norvig note that a table-driven agent for even a trivial task such as chess would need more entries than there are atoms in the observable universe.
  • Maintainability collapse. Large rule bases suffer from conflicts (two rules match and prescribe incompatible actions), gaps (percepts no rule covers), subsumption (an earlier general rule shadows a later specific one, as in the fire-alarm example above), and unintended interactions that emerge only in rare percept combinations. The complexity of checking these properties grows far faster than the rule count. Expert-system history is full of knowledge bases that became unmaintainable at a few thousand rules, and modern "business rules engines" rediscover the same wall regularly.
  • No representation of goals. A reflex agent's objective is implicit in its rules; it is nowhere written down. Consequently, changing the objective means rewriting the rule base, not editing a goal. A delivery robot whose reflex rules encode "go to room 12" cannot be redirected to room 7 by changing a variable — the routing behaviour is spread across dozens of condition–action pairs. Goal-based agents separate "what I want" from "how the world works", which is precisely what makes them reconfigurable.
  • No lookahead and no trade-offs. Lacking a model of action effects, the agent cannot evaluate consequences, cannot compare two plans, and cannot sacrifice immediate reward for a better outcome later. It also cannot weigh competing desiderata — speed versus energy, safety margin versus throughput — because it has no utility function over outcomes. Any apparent trade-off must be pre-computed by the designer and frozen into the rule ordering.

When to choose this architecture. A disciplined design justification names the environment properties that make the choice defensible, rather than appealing to simplicity in the abstract. The architecture is appropriate when:

  • the environment is fully observable for the task, so no hidden variable determines the correct action;
  • it is deterministic (or near enough) so the same action reliably has the same effect, and episodic, so the current decision does not depend on earlier ones;
  • the percept space is small or compressible into a few discriminating features, keeping the rule base inspectable;
  • there is a single, fixed objective with no outcome trade-offs to weigh;
  • the latency budget forbids search or inference at decision time — microsecond-scale control loops, interrupt handlers, hardware interlocks;
  • certifiability and exhaustive testability are first-order requirements.

It is inappropriate when history matters (sequential tasks, state estimation, tracking), when the objective changes or must be specified declaratively, when outcomes must be traded off against one another under uncertainty, or when the environment is partially observable in ways that cannot be fixed by adding sensors. There is no memory of past percepts, no model of how the world works, and no explicit goal — just "if this condition holds, do that." It is fast, cheap, and easy to verify, and it works well when the environment is fully observable and the right action really is determined by what the agent can sense right now. When observability is partial, the same percept can require different actions, and the agent either stalls or cycles forever in an infinite loop — the standard motivation for adding internal state in a model-based reflex agent. Rule sets also scale badly: every new situation adds rules, and interactions between rules get hard to maintain.

Model-Based Reflex Agents

1

Model-Based Reflex Agents

▶

A simple reflex agent is the most economical design in the agent taxonomy: it reads the current percept, matches it against a set of condition–action rules, and fires the first rule that applies. Its entire decision procedure can be summarised as action ← RULE-MATCH(INTERPRET-INPUT(percept), rules). This works beautifully — but only under a demanding assumption: that the current percept contains everything the agent needs to know in order to act correctly. In formal terms, the environment must be fully observable with respect to the agent's task, so that the percept uniquely identifies the relevant world state. The moment that assumption breaks, the reflex design degrades from "elegant" to "broken". The model-based reflex agent is the minimal repair: keep the condition–action machinery, but give the agent an internal state that summarises the percept history, and the models it needs to keep that state honest as the world and the agent itself change.

Why reflex agents fail in partially observable worlds. A percept is a snapshot — a thin slice of the world, taken through whatever sensors the agent happens to possess, at one instant. It is not the world state. Consider the classic two-square vacuum world, where the agent perceives [location, status], e.g. [A, Dirty]. Here the percept actually does reveal the agent's location, so a simple reflex agent can succeed. Now remove the location sensor, so the agent only perceives [Dirty] or [Clean]. The agent in square A with a clean square A and the agent in square B with a clean square B produce the identical percept [Clean], yet they demand different actions (move Right versus move Left). This is the defining pathology of partial observability: perceptual aliasing — distinct world states that collapse to the same sensory signature.

Perceptual aliasing has two consequences worth separating. The first is simply choosing the wrong action: with no way to tell A from B, any fixed rule if Clean then Right is correct half the time and wrong the other half. The second is more insidious: infinite loops. Because a deterministic simple reflex agent maps percept to action with a fixed function, the same percept always yields the same action. If [Clean] → Right and the agent is already in the rightmost square, it bumps the wall, perceives [Clean] again, and moves Right again — forever. The agent cannot escape because the information needed to break the symmetry (which square am I in? have I already cleaned the other one?) is simply not being sensed. Randomisation ([Clean] → Right or Left with probability ½) can rescue the agent from the loop and is a genuinely useful trick, but it is a blunt instrument: it trades a guaranteed failure for an expected-time-to-success that may be exponentially bad, and it still never lets the agent know anything.

Other everyday examples of the same failure mode:

  • Blind-spot lane change. A car's side camera sees empty road because the overtaking vehicle is momentarily occluded. The percept "lane clear" is identical whether the lane is truly clear or whether a car is hidden alongside. A pure reflex agent merges into a collision.
  • Brake-light ambiguity. A single camera frame showing a car's rear lights illuminated cannot distinguish "the car just started braking" from "the car has been braking for two seconds and is nearly stopped". The appropriate deceleration differs sharply between the two.
  • Corridor navigation. Every junction in an office building looks alike to a laser scanner. Without memory the robot cannot tell junction 3 from junction 7 and will wander.
  • Thermostat without memory. A naive thermostat sees 20 °C and does nothing — unaware that the temperature has been falling at 2 °C per hour and that the room will be uncomfortably cold in fifteen minutes.

The remedy in every case is the same and is almost obvious once stated: the agent should remember something about what it has seen and done before. The information that disambiguates the present is often available in the past.

Internal state: remembering what sensors cannot see. The internal state is a data structure held inside the agent program that carries information forward across decision cycles. Three properties make it practical rather than merely conceptual.

  • It is updated incrementally, not recomputed. On each cycle the agent takes the previous state and revises it in light of the last action and the new percept. It does not replay the entire percept history from the beginning. This keeps the per-cycle cost constant rather than growing without bound, which matters enormously for an agent that runs for hours or years.
  • It compresses the percept history. In principle the ideal agent function maps the whole percept sequence to an action; storing that sequence verbatim is infeasible. The internal state is a sufficient summary: a compact representation that preserves whatever the decision rules actually need. A robot does not store ten million laser scans; it stores an (x, y, θ) pose estimate. A vacuum does not store its whole movement log; it stores a bit-vector of which rooms are clean.
  • It restores distinguishability. Two world states that look alike to the sensors become distinguishable in the agent's extended description (percept, internal state). Returning to the sensorless-location vacuum: if the agent remembers "I started in A and have executed one Right", then percept [Clean] now means "B is clean", which is different from the same percept with state "I am in A". The rule set can key on the pair, and the loop dissolves.

The transition model: how the world evolves. Memory alone is not enough, because the world does not politely freeze while the agent is not looking. A remembered fact decays in value: "there was a cyclist on my left" is actionable one second later and almost meaningless thirty seconds later. To keep stored state useful the agent needs knowledge of how the world changes. This knowledge is traditionally split into two parts, and the split is conceptually important.

  • "How the world evolves independently of the agent." These are exogenous dynamics — things that happen whether or not the agent acts. Other cars continue along their trajectories; a braking vehicle decelerates at roughly the rate observed; dirt reappears in rooms over time; the outdoor temperature drops after sunset; a stock price drifts. For example, a car-tracking module can propagate a tracked vehicle forward with a constant-velocity assumption: x' = x + v·Δt. The tracked object persists in the internal state through an occlusion precisely because the model says where it should have gone.
  • "What my actions do." These are endogenous effects — the consequences of the agent's own behaviour. Executing Right in the vacuum world changes the current square from A to B (unless the agent is already at the right edge, in which case nothing changes — a detail the model must capture or the state estimate drifts). Turning the steering wheel 10° changes heading. Commanding Suck makes the current square clean. Placing a buy order reduces available cash and increases held inventory.

Together these two bodies of knowledge constitute the transition model of the environment. Its representation can be chosen to suit the domain:

  • Symbolic rules: IF action = Suck THEN status(current_square) ← Clean. Readable, easy to author, brittle under noise.
  • Equations / difference equations: dead-reckoning for a wheeled robot, θ' = θ + (v_right − v_left)·Δt / L. Compact and precise, but accumulates drift.
  • Probabilistic transitions: P(s′ | s, a), the conditional distribution over next states. This is the formulation used in Markov decision processes and in Bayesian filters such as the Kalman filter and particle filter. It is the honest choice when actions sometimes fail — wheels slip, grippers drop objects, a Right command succeeds 90% of the time.
  • Learned models: a neural network or regression model trained on logged (state, action, next state) triples, used when the dynamics are too complex to write down.

Why insist on a transition model at all? Because without it the stored state goes stale and becomes worse than no state. An agent that remembers "the road ahead was clear" and never ages that belief will drive confidently into a queue of traffic. Stale memory is actively dangerous because it carries the same authority as fresh observation. The transition model is what converts memory from a snapshot archive into a continuously maintained estimate of the present.

The sensor model: interpreting percepts. The third ingredient answers a different question: given that I received this percept, what does it tell me about the world? The sensor model encodes the relationship between underlying world states and the raw observations the hardware delivers — in probabilistic form, P(percept | state).

  • It says what a percept reveals. A bank of pixels is not a fact about the world until something interprets it. "The region of the image corresponding to the vehicle ahead has become significantly redder and brighter, therefore its brake lights are on, therefore it is decelerating" is a chain of sensor-model reasoning. Likewise "the ultrasonic sensor returned 0.4 m, therefore there is an obstacle roughly 0.4 m in front" — the word "roughly" is doing real work.
  • It says what a percept leaves ambiguous. This is just as important. A forward-facing camera says nothing about the blind spot; a motion sensor that is silent is consistent with both "room empty" and "occupant sitting still"; a bump sensor fires identically for a wall and a chair leg. A good sensor model makes these limitations explicit so that the agent does not over-interpret silence as evidence of absence.
  • It is the mechanism for reconciling prediction with observation. The transition model produces a prediction of the current state; the sensor model converts the new percept into evidence; the update step fuses the two. In Bayesian terms this is predict-then-correct: belief⁻ = Σ P(s′|s,a)·belief(s) followed by belief(s′) ∝ P(percept|s′)·belief⁻(s′). A Kalman filter is exactly this loop with Gaussian assumptions; a particle filter is the same loop with sampled hypotheses. Even a non-probabilistic implementation performs an informal version: "I predicted I'd be at the third doorway; I see a doorway; good, confidence up."
  • It forces honesty about uncertainty. Because sensors are noisy, partial, biased, and occasionally broken, the resulting internal state is a best estimate, not ground truth. Robust designs represent this explicitly — a covariance matrix, a confidence score, a set of candidate hypotheses — and let the decision rules degrade gracefully when confidence is low (e.g. slow down when the obstacle estimate is uncertain rather than steering aggressively on a guess).

The agent program: update state, then match rule. The model-based reflex agent program is the simple reflex program with a state-maintenance stage bolted on the front. Its canonical form:

function MODEL-BASED-REFLEX-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: state,          the agent's current conception of the world
                transition_model, how the world evolves and what my actions do
                sensor_model,     how the world is reflected in percepts
                rules,            a set of condition-action rules
                action,           the most recent action, initially none

    state  <- UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept, transition_model, sensor_model)
    rule   <- RULE-MATCH(state, rules)
    action <- rule.ACTION
    return action

function UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept, transition_model, sensor_model)
    predicted <- PREDICT(state, action, transition_model)   // step 1
    state     <- CORRECT(predicted, percept, sensor_model)  // step 2
    return state

The four steps of each cycle, spelled out:

  • Step 1 — Predict. Using the previous action and the transition model, project the stored state forward to the current instant. "I commanded Right, so I am probably now in square B." "0.1 s has elapsed, so the tracked car ahead has advanced about 2 m." This step applies both halves of the transition model: the agent's own effects and the world's independent evolution.
  • Step 2 — Correct. Fold in the new percept through the sensor model, refining or overriding the prediction. "I predicted B, and I see a wall on my right — consistent, confidence up." Or: "I predicted B, but the floor pattern matches A — my Right action must have failed; revise." Disagreement between prediction and percept is information, not an error to be suppressed.
  • Step 3 — Match. Run the updated internal state (typically together with the current percept) against the condition–action rules and select an action. Note the crucial difference from a simple reflex agent: the rules are conditioned on state, not on percept. That single substitution is what buys the extra power.
  • Step 4 — Record. Store the chosen action in the persistent variable so that the next cycle's prediction step can account for its effects. Omitting this step is a classic bug: the agent then predicts as though it had been standing still, and its state estimate drifts away from reality in exactly the way the model was meant to prevent.

The prediction step propagates the tracked vehicle to ≈ 40 m using constant velocity; the correction step, guided by a sensor model that says "occlusion produces absence of detection, which is not evidence of absence", leaves the track alive with reduced confidence. The rule if gap < safe_gap then decelerate continues to fire sensibly. A simple reflex agent, seeing "no vehicle detected", would have accelerated.

Strengths, limits, and when to choose this architecture. The model-based reflex agent occupies a well-defined sweet spot in the design space.

Choose it when: the environment is partially observable or the sensors are noisy, and yet the correct action is still a direct function of current conditions rather than of some desired future outcome. If you can write down "in situation X, do Y" rules — and you only need memory to work out reliably which situation X you are actually in — this is the right architecture. It is substantially cheaper than planning: there is no search, no lookahead, no goal expansion, just one state update and one rule match per cycle, which makes it realisable in hard real-time loops running at tens or hundreds of hertz.

Its principal limit is the absence of goals. The agent has a model of how the world works but no explicit representation of how the world ought to be. It cannot reason "if I take this action, I will be closer to a state I desire", and it therefore cannot plan, cannot handle novel objectives without rewriting rules, and cannot trade off competing considerations. A model-based reflex taxi at a junction knows exactly where it is, what the other cars are doing, and what turning left would do — but nothing in its architecture tells it where the passenger wants to go. That requires a goal-based agent; ranking partial successes requires a utility-based one.

Scalability of the rule set is the second limit. Giving the agent more internal state increases the number of distinguishable situations, and the rule table must cover them. Rule sets grow combinatorially, develop overlaps and gaps, and become difficult to test and maintain; conflicting rules require arbitration schemes; and a change to the state representation can ripple through hundreds of conditions. This is a real engineering cost, and it is often the practical trigger for migrating to a goal- or utility-based design where behaviour is derived rather than enumerated.

Third, everything rests on model quality. The internal state is only as good as the transition and sensor models that maintain it. A wrong model does not produce visible confusion — it produces confidently wrong actions, which is the most dangerous failure mode of all. Dead reckoning with a slightly mis-measured wheel radius yields a robot that is certain it is in the corridor while it is actually in a wall. Mitigations include periodic re-grounding against unambiguous landmarks, explicit confidence tracking with fallback to cautious behaviour, divergence detection (flagging persistent prediction/percept disagreement), and model learning from logged data.

Typical deployments span exactly this profile of "partial observability, reactive decisions":

  • Automatic emergency braking and adaptive cruise control — maintain tracks of surrounding vehicles through occlusion and sensor dropout; brake according to current estimated gap and closing speed.
  • Occupancy-aware thermostats — remember the last motion event and model how a house cools, rather than reacting only to the instantaneous sensor reading.
  • Vacuum and lawn-mowing robots — maintain a coverage map so that already-cleaned regions are not revisited endlessly, and odometry-based pose so that the map stays aligned.
  • Industrial process controllers —estimate unmeasured internal variables (tank levels, reactor temperature profiles) from a handful of sensors plus a process model, and actuate valves on the resulting estimate.
  • Object-tracking modules in surveillance and sports analytics — persist identities across frames in which a subject is hidden behind another.
  • Stateful network firewalls and intrusion detectors — classify a packet using remembered connection state rather than the packet alone.

In short, the model-based reflex agent is the smallest architectural step that makes reactive behaviour viable in the real world. It keeps the speed and simplicity of condition–action rules, but replaces the naive assumption "what I see is what there is" with a maintained, model-driven best estimate of what there actually is. Everything above it in the hierarchy — goal-based, utility-based, and learning agents — builds on this same state-estimation core, and merely changes how the action is chosen once the state is known.

Goal-Based Agents

1

Goal-Based Agents

▶

A goal-based agent is the first architecture in the standard progression of agent designs that explicitly represents what it is trying to achieve, separately from what it currently knows and what it can do. Simple reflex agents map a percept directly to an action; model-based reflex agents add an internal state so that the mapping can account for unobservable parts of the world. Both, however, bury the agent's purpose inside the condition–action rules themselves. If you want a model-based reflex taxi to drive to the airport instead of the railway station, you must rewrite the rules. A goal-based agent instead keeps the world model and bolts on a declarative statement of the desired outcome, then reasons about which sequence of actions will transform the current state into one that satisfies that statement. This shift — from lookup to deliberation — is what gives goal-based agents their characteristic flexibility and their characteristic cost.

What makes an agent goal-based. Three features, taken together, define the architecture. First, it retains the internal world model inherited from the model-based reflex design. Because real environments are usually partially observable, the agent cannot read the full state off its sensors; it maintains a belief about the current state and updates that belief as new percepts arrive. A warehouse robot whose camera sees only the aisle in front of it still "knows" that a pallet was left in aisle 7 two minutes ago, because its internal state records it. Without this model, lookahead would be impossible — you cannot predict the consequences of an action if you do not know what state you are acting in.

Second, the agent holds a goal: a description of a state or, more usually, a set of states that count as success. Goals are normally written as a predicate over states rather than as a single enumerated state. "The robot is at the loading dock" is satisfied by any state in which the robot's position is the dock, regardless of battery level, gripper contents, or time of day. A goal test such as IsGoal(s) = (s.location == DOCK and s.carrying == nothing) partitions the state space into goal states and non-goal states. This is a binary notion — a state either satisfies the goal or it does not — and that binary character is both the source of the architecture's simplicity and, as we will see, the source of its main limitation.

Third, action selection becomes a reasoning problem rather than a lookup problem. A reflex agent answers "what rule matches this percept?" A goal-based agent answers "which of the action sequences available to me leads from my current believed state to a goal state?" That question is answered by simulating the future: what will happen if I do this? The agent applies its transition model to candidate actions, obtains predicted successor states, applies the model again to those, and so on, building a tree of hypothetical futures until one of them satisfies the goal test. Consider a taxi at a junction deciding whether to turn left. A reflex rule might say "if the light is green, go." A goal-based agent asks: if I turn left, I end up on Elm Street; from Elm Street can I still reach the airport, and at what cost? The decision at the junction is justified by events several steps downstream.

Finally, goals make the agent's purpose explicit and inspectable. You can print the goal, log it, change it at run time, and explain a decision by reference to it ("I turned left because the only route to the airport that avoids the closed bridge goes through Elm Street"). In a reflex agent the purpose is implicit — smeared across hundreds of rules — and therefore cannot be audited, swapped, or reasoned about by the agent itself or by a human reviewer.

Core components of the agent program. The goal-based agent program is conventionally decomposed into five cooperating parts, executed in a perceive–deliberate–act loop.

  • Sensors and state estimation. Each cycle, the sensors supply a percept. The agent combines the percept with its previous belief and its knowledge of what it just did to produce an updated belief about the current state. This is the same UPDATE-STATE step used by the model-based reflex agent, and its quality bounds everything downstream: plan on a wrong state and you will execute a wrong plan.
  • A model of how the world evolves. This captures change that happens independently of the agent — traffic builds up at rush hour, a conveyor belt advances, an opponent moves a piece. In deterministic, static domains this component may be trivial; in dynamic domains it is essential for predicting the state at the moment the agent's action will actually take effect.
  • A model of what my actions do. The transition function RESULT(s, a) = s' predicts the successor state. In a planning formalism this is usually written as preconditions and effects rather than as a lookup table, because the state space is far too large to enumerate.
  • An explicit goal representation. A goal test, a conjunction of literals, a target state, or a set of acceptable states. This is the component that is swapped when the task changes.
  • A search or planning procedure. This is the deliberation engine: it chains predicted states together to find a sequence [a1, a2, ..., an] such that applying the transition model to the current state in order yields a goal state.
  • Actuators. The agent executes the first action of the chosen sequence, then the loop repeats. Re-planning (or at least re-checking the plan) after each action is what keeps the agent robust when the world does not behave exactly as the model predicted.
function GOAL-BASED-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: state, the agent's current belief about the world
                model, how the world evolves and what my actions do
                goal,  a description of the desired set of states
                plan,  an action sequence, initially empty

    state <- UPDATE-STATE(state, last_action, percept, model)
    if plan is empty or not VALID(plan, state, model) then
        plan <- SEARCH(state, goal, model)      // deliberation
    if plan is empty then
        return NoOp                              // goal unreachable
    last_action <- FIRST(plan)
    plan <- REST(plan)
    return last_action

Notice two design details in this skeleton. The agent only re-plans when the stored plan is empty or has been invalidated — this amortises the cost of deliberation over many cycles, an important optimisation given how expensive search is. And the agent commits to only one action at a time, so a surprise in the next percept can trigger a fresh plan before much damage is done. An architecture that computed a 200-step plan and then executed it blindly would be brittle in any environment that is not perfectly deterministic and static.

Search and planning as decision making. Deliberation can be organised in two broad styles, which differ in how richly they represent actions.

Search treats states as atomic, opaque objects. The agent knows only that it has a start state, a successor function, and a goal test; it explores the space of action sequences by repeatedly expanding states. Breadth-first search, uniform-cost search, and A* are all instances. For route finding this is natural: a state is "the taxi is at junction 42," the successors are the junctions reachable by one road, and the goal test is "is this junction the airport?"

Planning keeps a structured, factored representation of states and actions. A state is a set of true facts, and an action is described by its preconditions and effects, for example:

Action(Load(crate, truck, loc),
  PRECOND: At(crate, loc) & At(truck, loc) & Empty(truck)
  EFFECT:  ~At(crate, loc) & In(crate, truck) & ~Empty(truck))

Goal: In(crate7, truckB) & At(truckB, depot)

Because the planner can see inside states and actions, it can reason about which action achieves which sub-goal, ignore irrelevant facts, and decompose the problem — things an atomic-state search cannot do. For large, structured domains such as logistics, spacecraft operations, or manufacturing scheduling, planning is dramatically more efficient than blind search over the same space.

The depth of lookahead required depends entirely on the task. A single-step lookahead suffices only when one action achieves the goal directly: a vacuum agent in a dirty square need only check that Suck produces a clean square. This degenerate case looks almost like a reflex agent, and indeed a goal-based agent in such a domain buys you little. The interesting cases need multi-step lookahead, and here the cost bites. If each state has branching factor b and the shallowest solution lies at depth d, an uninformed search may generate on the order of bd nodes. With b = 10 and d = 12 — a modest route-planning problem — that is of the order of 1012 nodes, which is already infeasible to enumerate one by one.

This is why heuristics matter. A heuristic h(s) estimates the remaining cost from state s to the nearest goal state, letting the search order its frontier so that promising branches are expanded first and hopeless ones are effectively pruned. Straight-line distance is the classic heuristic for route finding; the number of misplaced tiles or the sum of Manhattan distances serves the 8-puzzle; the count of unsatisfied goal literals, or the cost of a relaxed plan that ignores delete effects, serves classical planning. A heuristic that never overestimates the true remaining cost is called admissible, and A* with an admissible heuristic is guaranteed to return an optimal solution while typically expanding orders of magnitude fewer nodes than uninformed search. The heuristic is where domain knowledge re-enters a design that otherwise prides itself on being general.

Flexibility compared to reflex agents. The practical payoff of separating goal from model is modularity, and it shows up in four distinct ways.

  • New goals yield new behaviour for free. Tell the delivery robot At(dock) today and At(bay3) tomorrow and it produces entirely different action sequences from the same map and the same transition model. No code is rewritten; only data changes. A reflex agent would need a fresh rule set per destination.
  • Model improvements propagate to every goal. Add a newly opened corridor to the map, or correct the model of how long the lift takes, and every future plan — for every destination — benefits immediately. In a reflex design, the same correction would have to be applied by hand to every rule that implicitly encoded the old route.
  • Novel situations are handled without anticipation. Suppose a bridge closes. A goal-based taxi, told the edge no longer exists, simply searches again and finds a detour the designer never thought about. A reflex taxi has no rule for "bridge closed on the route to the airport" unless someone wrote one, and no mechanism for inventing a replacement.
  • Behaviour is explainable. Because the goal and the model are explicit objects, a decision can be justified: "I chose TurnLeft because, given my belief that the bridge is closed, the cheapest path satisfying At(airport) starts with TurnLeft." This transparency matters enormously for debugging, for certification in safety-critical domains, and for user trust.

Contrast this with the combinatorics facing a reflex designer. If there are S distinguishable situations and D possible destinations, condition–action rules must in principle cover S × D combinations. The goal-based agent needs one model of size roughly S plus a goal of size roughly 1, and it computes the cross-product on demand. The cost is paid in CPU time at run time rather than in designer effort at design time — which is precisely the trade-off the next section examines.

Computational cost and limitations. Deliberation is not free, and goal-based agents have several well-understood weaknesses.

  • Exponential blow-up. As noted, both time and memory can grow as bd. Breadth-first search stores the entire frontier, so memory usually fails before time does. Real systems mitigate this with heuristics, iterative deepening, pattern databases, hierarchical decomposition, or plan caching — but the worst case remains intractable, and classical planning is PSPACE-complete in general.
  • Latency versus reflexes. A reflex agent responds in effectively constant time: match a rule, emit an action. In a real-time domain — collision avoidance, a control loop running at 100 Hz, an industrial safety interlock — a planner that takes 300 ms to think is simply the wrong tool. Practical architectures therefore often layer a fast reflex "safety" level beneath a slower deliberative level, letting reflexes pre-empt the plan when something urgent happens.
  • Binary goals cannot rank successes. If two routes both reach the airport, one taking 20 minutes and one taking 90, the goal test says "success" to both. You can patch this by adding path costs and using uniform-cost search or A*, which optimises a single cost measure, but the goal itself still carries no notion of degree of satisfaction.
  • No trade-offs between competing goals. A taxi should be fast, safe, cheap, legal, and comfortable. These conflict: faster means less safe, cheaper means slower. A binary goal conjunction can only say "achieve all of them" — and if the conjunction is unsatisfiable, the agent simply reports failure rather than finding the best available compromise. Partially achievable goals ("deliver 8 of the 10 parcels") are likewise outside its natural vocabulary.
  • Model uncertainty breaks confident plans. Search assumes the transition model is correct. If actions are stochastic, if other agents interfere, or if the world model is stale, a plan that looked guaranteed can fail mid-execution. Partial remedies include re-planning every cycle, contingency planning that branches on observations, and conformant planning that works for all possible outcomes — all of which increase cost further.

These limitations are exactly the motivation for the utility-based agent, which replaces the binary goal test with a utility function mapping each state (or each sequence) to a real number. Utility lets the agent say "this outcome is somewhat better than that one," supports principled trade-offs between conflicting objectives, and — combined with probabilities from an uncertain model — supports maximising expected utility rather than hoping a plan succeeds. Goal-based agents can be seen as the special case in which utility is 1 at goal states and 0 elsewhere.

Choosing a goal-based architecture. Architecture selection should be argued from the properties of the task environment and the operational constraints, not from fashion. Four questions settle most cases.

  • Do the goals change often while the dynamics stay stable? This is the sweet spot. A warehouse robot's map and motion model are fixed for months, but its destination changes every few minutes. Encoding the stable part once (the model) and varying the volatile part (the goal) minimises maintenance effort — one of the strongest practical arguments for the design.
  • Does the correct action depend on consequences several steps ahead? If a locally attractive action can lead into a dead end — turning onto a one-way street, spending a resource that is needed later, taking a cul-de-sac in a maze — then lookahead is not optional. Reflex rules, which see only the present, systematically fall into such traps.
  • Are responses required immediately, over a small set of situations? Then prefer a reflex design, or a hybrid in which reflexes handle time-critical events and a planner handles the longer horizon. A thermostat, an anti-lock braking controller, and a packet-dropping firewall rule need microseconds, not a search tree.
  • Is a reliable transition model actually available? This is the precondition everyone forgets. Planning is only as good as RESULT(s, a). If the domain is poorly understood, highly stochastic, or populated by adversaries, a nominally brilliant plan will fail on contact with reality; you may need stochastic planning, online re-planning, or a learning agent that acquires the model from experience.

When you do commit to the architecture, make the justification concrete by naming three things: the goal representation ("goal test At(robot, bay3) & Carrying(nothing)"), the search method ("A* over the grid graph with a Manhattan-distance heuristic, re-planned on each obstacle detection"), and the expected cost ("branching factor 4, typical depth 60, under 5,000 node expansions, well inside the 100 ms control budget"). Stating the rejected alternatives strengthens the case: a simple reflex wall-follower was rejected because it cannot guarantee reaching an arbitrary bay; a utility-based design was rejected because all successful routes are acceptable and no trade-off between competing objectives is required, so the extra modelling effort would buy nothing. That pattern — goal, method, cost, rejected alternatives — is what turns an architectural preference into a defensible engineering decision.

Worked illustration. Consider a hospital delivery robot that must take a sample from Ward C to the Lab. Its sensors report corridor occupancy and its own position; its internal state records the floor plan plus the fact that the east lift has been out of service since 09:00 — information no current percept provides, which is exactly why the world model is needed. Its goal is At(robot, Lab) & Holding(nothing). Its transition model says a Move action succeeds unless a corridor is blocked, that UseLift requires the lift to be in service, and that Deposit requires being at the Lab counter. A* with a floor-aware distance heuristic returns the sequence [Move(C, junction1), Move(junction1, westLift), UseLift(3, 1), Move(liftLobby, Lab), Deposit]. The robot executes Move(C, junction1) only, then re-perceives. If a trolley now blocks junction1, the stored plan is invalidated and a new search finds a route via the south corridor — a response the designer never explicitly programmed. Had the task instead been "deliver all six samples in whatever order minimises total time while never letting any sample exceed its 30-minute stability window," the binary goal test would be inadequate: that problem needs a continuous measure over outcomes, and the appropriate architecture would be utility-based.

A goal-based agent goes beyond reacting to what it currently senses. It keeps an internal model of how the world works and how actions change it, and it also holds explicit information about the state it is trying to reach. Utility-Based Agents

Utility-Based Agents

▶

A goal-based agent is a substantial advance over a reflex agent: it can look ahead, simulate the consequences of its actions, and select a sequence that reaches a desired state. But a goal is a binary predicate. It partitions the universe of possible states into exactly two classes — acceptable and unacceptable — and says nothing whatsoever about how the members of each class compare with one another. A delivery robot whose goal is parcel_at(destination) is perfectly satisfied by a route that takes four minutes and by a route that takes forty, by a route through an empty corridor and by a route that weaves between a crowd of people and a flight of stairs. A utility-based agent closes exactly this gap. It replaces the question "Is this state a goal?" with the far richer question "How desirable is this state, compared with the alternatives available to me?" This single change in the internal representation of preference is what allows an agent to be rational in the demanding sense required by messy, costly, risky, real-world task environments.

From goals to preferences: why utility is needed. Consider a goal test written as a boolean function and a utility function written as a scalar function of the same state:

def is_goal(state):            # goal-based agent
    return state.parcel_location == state.destination

def utility(state):            # utility-based agent
    return (100 * state.parcel_delivered
            -  1.0 * state.minutes_elapsed
            -  5.0 * state.collision_count
            -  0.2 * state.battery_used_percent)

The goal test answers "did I succeed?"; the utility function answers "how good was this success?" The practical consequences of that difference are large:

  • Many plans satisfy a goal, but they are not equivalent. A search over a city road network will typically return dozens of loop-free routes from A to B. All of them satisfy at(B). They differ in distance, travel time, fuel burn, toll cost, number of unprotected left turns, and exposure to accident risk. A goal test cannot see any of these differences because they are all collapsed into "true".
  • Goals give no principled tie-breaking rule. When two plans both work, a goal-based agent must fall back on something arbitrary — whichever plan the search happened to find first, or whichever had the fewest actions. "Fewest actions" is in fact a crude, implicit utility function, and it is frequently the wrong one: a three-step plan that flies a drone through a thunderstorm is worse than an eight-step plan that routes around it.
  • Utility separates achieving from achieving well. Two robots can both deliver the parcel; only one of them does so quickly, quietly, without startling pedestrians and with 60% battery to spare. Only utility can express that distinction.
  • Utility degrades gracefully when the goal is unreachable. This is perhaps the most underappreciated benefit. If the destination corridor is flooded and no plan achieves parcel_at(destination), a goal-based agent's search returns failure and it has nothing sensible to do. A utility-based agent simply notices that every reachable state scores poorly, and then picks the least-bad one: deliver the parcel to the reception desk, notify the recipient, and return to the charging dock. Partial credit is representable; a boolean goal has no notion of partial credit.

The utility function as an internalized performance measure. In the standard formulation, the agent designer specifies an external performance measure that an outside observer uses to judge the agent's behaviour over a sequence of environment states. A utility function U(s) → ℝ is the agent's own internal copy of that yardstick: a scalar score attached to a state (or to a history, or to an outcome), where higher means more preferred. Rationality then has a crisp operational definition: choose the action whose outcome maximizes utility.

  • The designer encodes what matters. Speed, safety, energy, comfort, monetary cost, fairness, noise — each becomes a term in U. The act of writing U forces the designer to state, in numbers, what the system is actually for.
  • Alignment is the critical correctness condition. If the external performance measure rewards clean floors but U rewards "dirt collected", a vacuum agent can maximize its utility by dumping collected dirt back onto the floor and sweeping it up again. The agent is behaving perfectly rationally with respect to U; the fault is entirely in the misalignment between U and the performance measure. Specification gaming of this kind is a design bug, not an agent malfunction.
  • Utilities are comparative, not absolute. Only the ordering (and, once probabilities are involved, the ratios of differences) carries meaning. Any positive affine transformation U'(s) = aU(s) + b with a > 0 leaves every decision unchanged. So "this state has utility 73" is meaningless in isolation; "this state has higher utility than that one, and the gap is twice as large as the other gap" is meaningful. A common engineering convention is to normalize each criterion onto [0, 1] so weights can be compared sensibly.
  • Consistency buys you a utility function for free. The von Neumann–Morgenstern result shows that if an agent's preferences over uncertain outcomes obey a few constraints (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence), then there exists a utility function whose expectation the agent is implicitly maximizing. An agent with intransitive preferences (A > B > C > A) can be exploited by a "money pump" that charges it to cycle round forever — so the axioms are not arbitrary mathematical tidiness, they are a defence against self-defeating behaviour.

Trading off conflicting goals. Real tasks pull in incompatible directions: fast versus safe, cheap versus comfortable, thorough versus timely. Utility resolves this by scalarization — aggregating multiple criteria into one number that can be ranked. The commonest aggregation is a weighted linear sum:

U(s) = w1*f1(s) + w2*f2(s) + ... + wn*fn(s)

# Autonomous taxi, criteria normalized to [0,1], higher = better
U(s) = 0.45*safety(s) + 0.25*speed(s) + 0.15*comfort(s)
     + 0.10*fuel_economy(s) + 0.05*legality_margin(s)

Other aggregations suit other intentions. A multiplicative form U = Π fi^wi refuses to let a strong criterion compensate for a near-zero one (useful when every criterion is essential). A min or "maximin" form U = min(f1..fn) optimizes the worst-performing criterion, which is appropriate for safety-critical or fairness-sensitive systems. A lexicographic ordering compares on safety first and only consults speed when safety ties, which approximates a hard constraint. Hard constraints can also be encoded as very large negative terms (a "penalty barrier"), e.g. -10^6 * collides(s).

Notice three things. First, the goal test cannot separate A, B and C at all. Second, the ranking produced by U is explicit and auditable: anyone can read the weights and see that the designer values avoiding risk about 75 times more per unit than avoiding a minute of travel. Third, the trade-off lives in data rather than in control flow. When a city tightens its school-zone policy, an engineer retunes one weight; nobody rewrites the planner. Compare that to a goal-based agent with hand-coded rules such as if near_school and hour < 16: avoid(), where every policy change is a code change with a regression-test tail.

The flip side is that bad weights produce behaviour that is technically optimal and practically unacceptable. If speed is weighted far above comfort, the taxi will brake and accelerate like a rally car; passengers will be miserable while the agent reports maximal utility. If a cleaning robot's utility counts only "square metres covered per hour", it will race over surfaces without actually cleaning them. Weight elicitation — interviewing stakeholders, running pairwise comparisons, simulating edge cases, inspecting the resulting behaviour, and iterating — is a genuine engineering activity, not a formality.

Handling uncertainty with expected utility. In deterministic, fully observable worlds the agent knows precisely which state each action produces, and it can simply compare U(result(a, s)) across actions. Most interesting environments are not like that. Actuators slip, sensors are noisy, other agents behave unpredictably, and the agent may not even know which state it is currently in. The outcome of an action is therefore a probability distribution over states, and the agent needs a transition model P(s' | s, a) alongside its utility function. The decision rule becomes the Maximum Expected Utility (MEU) principle:

EU(a | e) = ∑_s'  P(s' | a, e) * U(s')

action* = argmax_a  EU(a | e)        # e = the evidence/percepts so far

A worked example makes the mechanics concrete. A delivery drone must choose between three actions with the following outcome distributions, where utility is measured in "net value of the delivery":

A goal-based agent asking only "does this plan achieve delivered?" would regard all three as acceptable and might well pick the fastest, flying the drone into the lake once every five trips. Expected utility lets the agent compare a certain small gain against a risky large gain on a single common scale, which is impossible with a boolean goal.

The shape of the utility function encodes the agent's attitude to risk, and this is a design decision, not an accident:

  • Concave U (diminishing returns) → risk aversion. If U(money) = √money, then a certain €100 has utility 10 while a 50/50 gamble between €0 and €200 has expected utility 0.5(0) + 0.5(14.1) = 7.07. The agent prefers the sure thing even though the gamble has the same expected monetary value. The gap, called the insurance premium, is what the agent would pay to avoid the risk. Medical, aviation and financial-compliance agents should normally be risk-averse.
  • Linear U → risk neutrality. The agent cares only about expected value. Appropriate when the same decision will be repeated thousands of times and losses average out — e.g. a high-frequency ad-bidding agent.
  • Convex U (increasing returns) → risk seeking. An agent that must reach a threshold or lose everything — a game agent trailing badly in the final seconds, or a rover with just enough battery for one gamble — rationally prefers the high-variance option.

Expected utility also supplies the graceful-degradation behaviour mentioned earlier. When no action guarantees success, every action has an expected utility that is merely low rather than undefined, so the agent still has a well-defined best move. It additionally supports information-gathering actions: the value of a sensing action is the difference between the expected utility of acting after the observation and the expected utility of acting now (the "value of information"), which explains why a rational agent will pause to take a sensor reading even though looking achieves no goal directly.

Architecture and decision cycle. Structurally, a utility-based agent is a model-based agent with the goal test replaced by a scoring function. It retains every component needed for partial observability and adds the evaluation machinery:

  • Internal state / belief state. Updated each cycle from the previous state, the chosen action, the transition model ("how the world evolves" and "what my actions do"), and the new percept. In a stochastic, partially observable world this is a probability distribution over states rather than a single state.
  • Predictive model. Answers "what will the world be like if I do action A?" — in general producing a distribution P(s' | s, a) rather than one successor.
  • Utility function. Answers "how happy will I be in that state?" instead of "is that state the goal?".
  • Decision step. Compute EU for each candidate action (or each candidate plan, if the agent looks several steps ahead) and send the argmax to the actuators.
function UTILITY-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: belief     # distribution over states
                T, O       # transition and sensor models
                U          # utility function
                a_prev     # previous action

    belief ← UPDATE-BELIEF(belief, a_prev, percept, T, O)

    best_a, best_eu ← null, −∞
    for each a in ACTIONS(belief):
        eu ← 0
        for each s' in PREDICT(belief, a, T):
            eu ← eu + P(s' | belief, a) * U(s')
        if eu > best_eu:
            best_a, best_eu ← a, eu

    a_prev ← best_a
    return best_a

The price of this expressiveness is computation. Where a goal-based agent stops searching the instant the goal test returns true, a utility-based agent must in principle enumerate and score many candidate outcomes, and for multi-step lookahead the branching over actions and stochastic outcomes grows explosively (an expectimax tree of depth d with b actions and k outcomes per action has roughly (bk)^d leaves). Practical systems therefore bound the cost: depth-limited lookahead with a learned or hand-crafted evaluation function at the horizon, Monte Carlo sampling of outcomes instead of exhaustive enumeration, pruning of dominated actions, caching of utility values, and offline policy computation (value iteration over an MDP) so that online behaviour reduces to a table lookup. Richer forms — representing utility over whole histories rather than single states, or discounting future reward with a factor γ — are the foundation of MDP and reinforcement-learning methods.

When to choose a utility-based architecture. The choice is an engineering trade-off between decision quality and design/runtime cost. The table below summarizes where each architecture is appropriate:

Two caveats are worth stating plainly. First, the main design burden shifts rather than disappears: instead of writing elaborate conditional rules, the engineer must elicit, calibrate and validate a utility function — gathering stakeholder preferences, choosing a sensible functional form, normalizing criteria, and testing for perverse maxima. Second, utility-based reasoning is only as good as the probability model behind it; confident probabilities derived from a bad world model produce confidently bad decisions. In practice, teams validate utility designs by running the agent in simulation over adversarial scenarios, performing sensitivity analysis on the weights (does a 10% change in one weight flip the chosen action?), and sanity-checking the ranking it induces against expert human judgement.

Goal-based agents ask a single yes-or-no question: does this action sequence reach the goal? That is often not enough. There are usually many ways to reach a goal, and they differ in cost, time, risk, comfort, and side effects. A utility-based agent replaces the binary goal test with a utility function — a mapping from states (or outcomes) to a number that expresses how desirable each one is. This lets the agent rank competing outcomes rather than merely accept or reject them, and it is how we build agents that not only achieve a goal but achieve it well.

Two ideas do most of the work in this topic. First, the utility function acts as an internalized version of the external performance measure: the designer's standard of success is written into the agent so the agent can evaluate its own options. Second, when the environment is stochastic or partially observable, the agent cannot know which outcome an action will produce, so it chooses the action with the highest expected utility — probability-weighted desirability averaged over possible outcomes. This is what makes utility-based agents the natural architecture for uncertain, multi-objective settings, and the direct ancestor of decision-theoretic and reinforcement-learning methods later in the course.

Learning Agents

Every agent architecture examined so far — the simple reflex agent with its condition–action rules, the model-based reflex agent with its internal state and transition model, the goal-based agent with its explicit description of desirable world states, and the utility-based agent with its numerical preference function — shares a single structural dependency: somebody had to supply the knowledge in advance. A designer wrote the rules, encoded the model of how the world evolves, enumerated the goals, or calibrated the utility function. The agent is then only as good as the designer's foresight. A learning agent removes that ceiling. It is not a sixth, rival architecture so much as a wrapper that can be placed around any of the others, adding machinery that modifies the agent's own decision-making components in the light of experience.

The classic statement of the idea is Turing's: rather than attempting to hand-program an intelligent machine, build a simpler machine and then teach it. The learning agent framework makes that proposal concrete by splitting an agent into four interacting parts — a performance element, a critic, a learning element, and a problem generator — and specifying precisely what information flows between them.

Why learning agents are needed. Three distinct pressures push a designer toward learning, and it is worth separating them because they imply different designs.

  • Unanticipated situations. Real environments are combinatorially vast. A delivery robot's designer cannot enumerate every floor surface, every lighting condition, every arrangement of furniture, every unusual way a human might block a corridor. A rule set written in advance will inevitably encounter states it does not cover, and will then either fail silently or fall back on a default that may be badly wrong. Learning allows coverage to be grown from the environment itself rather than imagined ahead of time.
  • Initially unknown environments. Sometimes the designer genuinely has no information to give. A Mars rover's designers do not know the traction properties of a particular regolith patch; an agent dropped into a new e-commerce marketplace does not know the demand curve. Here learning is not an optimisation — it is the only way the agent can acquire a usable policy at all. The agent must become more competent than its initial program, which is a stronger claim than merely becoming faster.
  • Repairing faulty assumptions. Even where the designer supplied knowledge, that knowledge may be wrong or may go stale. A transition model that says "braking reduces speed by 5 m/s²" is wrong on wet tarmac. A utility function that weights fuel cost against delivery speed with a fixed exchange rate becomes miscalibrated when fuel prices triple. Experience provides the corrective signal: the agent observes the discrepancy between predicted and actual outcomes and adjusts. Environments also change — a non-stationary environment guarantees that a fixed program degrades over time.

A useful way to see the generality of the idea: the thing being improved is always some component of a conventional agent. In a reflex agent, learning edits condition–action rules. In a model-based agent, learning refines the transition and sensor models. In a goal-based agent, learning improves knowledge about which action sequences achieve goals. In a utility-based agent, learning adjusts the utility estimates themselves. The learning wrapper is architecture-agnostic.

The four components. The following table summarises what each part does, what it consumes, and what it produces.

Component Role Input Output
Performance element Selects actions — this is the "whole agent" of the previous architectures Percepts (and internal state, goals, utilities) Actions
Critic Judges how well the agent is doing against a fixed external performance standard Percepts / percept history, plus the performance standard Feedback: an evaluation of recent behaviour
Learning element Makes improvements to the performance element Critic's feedback, plus knowledge of how the performance element is built Changes to rules, models, goals, or utility estimates
Problem generator Proposes exploratory actions that yield new, informative experience Current knowledge and its gaps, learning goals Suggested actions (possibly suboptimal in the short term)

The loop is continuous and can be stated in four words: act, evaluate, modify, explore. The performance element acts; the critic evaluates; the learning element modifies the performance element; the problem generator occasionally overrides the performance element's preferred action to generate experience the agent would otherwise never obtain. Note the direction of the arrows: the learning element writes into the performance element. The performance element does not know it is being taught; from its own perspective it is simply an agent whose rules happen to change.

A concrete illustration. Consider an automated taxi.

  • The performance element contains everything needed to drive: perception, route planning, steering and braking rules.
  • The taxi makes a quick left turn across three lanes of traffic. The critic observes the ensuing percepts — horns sounding, a passenger's shocked expression, a near-miss registered by the proximity sensors — and, against a performance standard that includes "avoid alarming passengers and other drivers", reports this as poor performance.
  • The learning element formulates a new or modified rule: a condition–action rule that forbids three-lane turns, or a downward revision of the utility estimate for that manoeuvre.
  • The problem generator might later propose trying braking on a wet road surface in a safe, low-speed setting, because the agent's model of stopping distance under those conditions is poorly determined. This action is not what the current policy would choose, but it produces information.

The critic and the performance standard. The critic exists because the agent's own percepts do not come labelled with "good" or "bad". A sequence of percepts is just data; converting it into an evaluation requires an interpretive layer, and that layer requires a yardstick.

The crucial design principle is that the performance standard is external to the agent and must not be modifiable by the agent. The reason is a degenerate-solution problem. If the agent could rewrite its own standard, the easiest route to "high performance" would be to lower the bar — the agent would learn to declare itself successful rather than to behave well. This is sometimes called reward hacking or specification gaming, and the external, fixed standard is the structural defence against the most blatant form of it. Humans face an analogue of this problem: it is far easier to redefine success than to achieve it.

It is worth being precise about a distinction that students frequently blur:

Aspect Performance measure (performance standard) Utility function
Whose it is The designer's / the outside world's The agent's own internal representation
Purpose To judge the agent's behaviour from outside To guide the agent's choice among available actions
Where it lives In the critic, fixed In the performance element, learnable
Scope Evaluates environment state sequences over time Evaluates states or state–action pairs for decision-making
Can the agent change it? No Yes — this is precisely what learning adjusts

The ideal case is that the agent's utility function converges on something that maximises the external performance measure. The two are related — the utility function is, in effect, the agent's internal hypothesis about what the performance standard rewards — but they are never identical, and learning is the process of narrowing the gap. In a vacuum-world example, the performance standard might be "one point per square cleaned per time step, minus one point per unit of electricity consumed." The agent's utility function may begin as a crude proxy ("prefer moving toward visible dirt") and be refined until it approximates the true standard, including the energy penalty it initially ignored.

The learning element: what can actually be learned. Because the learning element modifies the performance element, the design of the learning element depends entirely on the design of the performance element. There is no generic learner; the learner must know what kind of thing it is editing.

Performance element architecture Learnable component Example of an improvement
Simple reflex Condition–action rules: add, strengthen, weaken, delete A spam filter adds the rule "if subject contains 'wire transfer urgent' then quarantine" after repeated user corrections
Model-based reflex Transition model ("how the world evolves") and sensor model ("how percepts relate to state") A robot learns that its wheel odometry over-reports distance by 4% on carpet, and recalibrates
Goal-based Action outcome knowledge, operator preconditions and effects, heuristics for goal achievement A planner learns that "order part X" has a 10-day latency, so plans that assume 2 days fail
Utility-based Utility estimates for states or state–action pairs; weights on competing objectives A trading agent lowers the estimated utility of holding a position overnight after repeated gap-down losses

Cutting across this is a second classification — the kind of feedback available, which determines what family of learning algorithm is applicable:

  • Supervised learning. The agent is given input–output pairs: for each situation, the correct answer. A medical diagnosis agent shown thousands of labelled images learns a mapping from image to diagnosis. In the agent framework, this arises naturally when the agent can observe the actual outcome of a situation and compare it with its own prediction — the environment effectively labels the agent's percepts after the fact. A taxi that predicts "the car ahead will brake" and then perceives it braking has generated its own supervised training example.
  • Unsupervised learning. No correct outputs are supplied; the agent discovers structure. A taxi driving agent, without being told, clusters driving conditions into categories resembling "good traffic day" and "bad traffic day", which can then be used as internal state features. Unsupervised learning alone cannot tell the agent what to do, because no preference information is present — but the representations it discovers make other learning easier.
  • Reinforcement learning. The agent receives a scalar reward or penalty rather than a correct answer. The taxi receives no tips from passengers; it must work out for itself which aspects of its behaviour caused that. Reinforcement learning is the most general and the most difficult, because the feedback says "that was bad" without saying "here is what you should have done instead." It is also the natural fit for the learning agent architecture, because rewards are simply another percept that the critic interprets.

The problem generator, and exploration versus exploitation. If the performance element always chose the action it currently believed best, the agent would be trapped by its own ignorance. Actions it has never tried have no evidence in their favour, so they are never tried, so they never acquire evidence. The problem generator breaks this cycle by deliberately proposing actions whose informational value exceeds their immediate expected payoff.

  • Exploitation is choosing the action with the highest expected value under current knowledge. It maximises reward now, assuming current beliefs are correct.
  • Exploration is choosing an action with uncertain outcome in order to reduce that uncertainty and improve future decisions. It sacrifices expected reward now for better decisions later.

The canonical illustration is the multi-armed bandit. Imagine a row of slot machines with unknown and different payout rates. After a few pulls, one machine has paid out twice out of three. Pure exploitation says: pull that lever forever. But three samples is nearly no evidence; the apparently best machine may in fact be the worst, and the agent will never find out. Pure exploration — pulling levers uniformly at random forever — gathers excellent statistics but never cashes in on them, so average reward stays mediocre permanently.

The failure modes are symmetric and both serious:

  • Pure exploitation locks the agent into a local optimum. A delivery robot that finds one workable route will keep using it even if a corridor twice as fast exists one floor down. The agent is not aware it is missing anything, because evidence of the alternative never arrives.
  • Pure exploration never consolidates. Performance remains at the level of random behaviour, and the knowledge gained is never converted into value. In some domains it is also dangerous.

Practical schemes therefore blend the two, typically with a bias toward exploration early and exploitation later, since early knowledge is poor and the remaining time over which to profit from new knowledge is long. Common mechanisms include:

  • ε-greedy: with probability ε choose a random action, otherwise choose the best known. Often ε is annealed — decayed from, say, 0.3 toward 0.01 — so exploration fades as knowledge matures.
  • Optimism in the face of uncertainty: initialise unexplored options with high estimated value, so the agent is naturally drawn to try them, and the estimates fall to reality as evidence accumulates.
  • Confidence-bound methods: choose the action with the highest upper plausible value, which automatically favours options that are either genuinely good or genuinely under-sampled.
  • Offline or simulated exploration: perform the risky experiments in a simulator or sandbox, then deploy what was learned. This decouples the cost of exploration from the real environment.

Feedback signals and learning in unknown environments. The quality and timing of feedback shape what is learnable.

  • Immediate reward and penalty. The simplest case: an action is followed directly by a scalar signal. Credit assignment is trivial — the action just taken is clearly responsible. Pain and hunger in biological agents work like this; so do a chess engine's immediate material gains.
  • Delayed reward and the credit-assignment problem. Far harder. A chess agent receives a single signal — win, lose, draw — after perhaps sixty moves. Which move caused the loss? The blunder may have been move 14, with the final collapse merely its consequence. The agent must propagate credit backward through time. Techniques such as temporal-difference learning address this by using the agent's own later estimates to update earlier ones, in effect passing the evaluation signal back along the chain of states. A business analogue: a customer churns after eight months; was it the pricing change in month two, or the support interaction in month seven?
  • Noisy and partial feedback. If the environment is stochastic, a good action can produce a bad outcome by chance. An agent that updates strongly on single episodes will over-fit — it will conclude the action is bad on one unlucky sample. The remedies are statistical: average over many episodes, use small learning rates so no single observation dominates, and hold out data to check that an apparent pattern generalises. Partial feedback compounds this: a recommender system learns only about items users actually clicked, never about the counterfactual performance of items it chose not to show, producing a self-reinforcing bias in the data it collects.
  • Simultaneous model and policy learning. In a genuinely unknown environment, the agent does not have the luxury of learning the world first and then optimising within it. It must build the transition model and the policy concurrently, each informing the other — a chicken-and-egg problem, because a poor model produces poor exploration, which produces a poor model. This is why the problem generator is not an optional extra in unknown environments: it is the mechanism that keeps the model-building process fed with data from regions the current (poor) policy would avoid.
  • Safety and irreversibility. The decisive practical constraint. Exploration is defensible when mistakes are recoverable; it is indefensible when they are not. A self-driving car must not "explore" whether a pedestrian crossing can be taken at 60 km/h. A surgical robot must not experiment on a patient. A trading agent must not explore position sizes that could bankrupt the fund. The standard response is to encode hard constraints outside the learning loop: a set of actions that the problem generator may never propose and the performance element may never select, regardless of estimated value. These constraints are a form of designer-supplied knowledge that learning is explicitly forbidden to override — the same logic that makes the performance standard immutable. Where possible, irreversible decisions are also wrapped in human approval, or rehearsed in simulation first.

Designing and justifying a learning agent. Adding learning is a design decision with costs, and should be argued for rather than assumed. A workable design procedure runs as follows.

  • Identify the component that most needs improvement. Learning should be targeted, not diffuse. Ask: where does this agent's knowledge come from, and which part of it is most likely to be wrong or incomplete? If the physics of the domain are well understood but user preferences are not, learn the utility function and leave the transition model fixed. If the actuators are uncertain but the objective is crisp, learn the transition model. Learning everything at once multiplies data requirements and makes failures impossible to diagnose.
  • Specify the external performance standard. Write down explicitly what the critic will measure, in terms of environment states and sequences, not in terms of the agent's internal variables. Scrutinise it for gaming opportunities: a cleaning agent rewarded for "dirt collected" may learn to dump dirt on the floor and re-collect it. If the standard omits something you care about — energy, safety, user annoyance — the agent will treat that thing as free and will exploit it.
  • Choose a feedback mechanism suited to the environment. If ground-truth labels are obtainable cheaply (historical outcomes, human annotation), supervised learning is the efficient choice. If only a quality signal is available and the agent must discover its own actions, reinforcement learning is required. If the environment is partially observable, the agent must learn under uncertainty about its own state, which rules out the simplest methods. Match the technique to observability and to the reward structure, including its delay.
  • Decide how much exploration is affordable. This is a function of the cost of mistakes. In a simulator or a recommender system, mistakes cost almost nothing and aggressive exploration is correct. In aviation, medicine, or finance, the exploration budget is close to zero in the live environment and must be spent in simulation, in shadow mode (the learner proposes but a trusted system acts), or on a small controlled fraction of traffic.
  • Compare against a non-learning baseline. Learning adds real costs: data pipelines, training infrastructure, non-deterministic behaviour that complicates testing, drift that requires monitoring, and the risk that the agent gets worse on a bad batch of data. A fixed rule-based agent is auditable, reproducible, and cheap to maintain. Learning is justified when (a) the environment is genuinely unknown at design time, (b) the environment changes faster than you can push updates, or (c) the required behaviour is too complex or too individualised to specify by hand. If none of these holds, the rule-based design is the better engineering choice.

A worked comparison makes the trade-off concrete. Consider a warehouse pick-path agent. A non-learning version uses a fixed shortest-path algorithm over a static map: it is predictable, easy to verify, and requires no data infrastructure — but it cannot exploit the fact that aisle 7 is congested every weekday at 10:00, or that the map is stale after a re-racking. A learning version wraps the same planner in a loop where the critic measures actual pick times against a standard of "items per hour, with zero collisions", the learning element updates per-aisle traversal-cost estimates, and the problem generator occasionally routes through under-sampled aisles to keep the cost estimates fresh. Exploration is cheap here (a slightly longer route costs seconds), feedback is immediate and quantitative, and the environment changes weekly. Learning is clearly justified. Change the domain to a drug-dosing agent and every one of those judgements reverses: exploration is dangerous, feedback is delayed and noisy, and a non-learning protocol with human oversight becomes the defensible design.

A learning agent is any agent architecture — simple reflex, model-based, goal-based, or utility-based — wrapped inside a loop that lets it improve with experience. Russell and Norvig describe this loop in four parts: the performance element (the agent you already know, which chooses actions), the critic (which judges incoming percepts against a fixed performance standard), the learning element (which uses the critic's feedback to modify the performance element), and the problem generator (which deliberately suggests exploratory actions that may be suboptimal now but informative later). The key insight is that the performance standard must sit outside the performance element; otherwise the agent could simply learn to lower its own bar.

This topic matters in CAI4002 because the earlier architectures all assume a designer who already knows enough about the environment to hand-code the right rules, model, goals, or utility function. In unknown, partially observable, or changing environments, that assumption fails. Learning agents are the engineering answer: start with an incomplete agent, give it feedback signals, and let experience fill the gaps. This is also where the exploration versus exploitation tension appears — an agent that only exploits its current best guess never discovers anything better.

Selecting and Justifying an Architecture

▶

Choosing an agent architecture is an engineering decision, not a matter of taste. Every architecture — simple reflex, model-based reflex, goal-based, utility-based, and learning — buys a particular capability at a particular cost. The capability is only worth buying if something in the task environment demands it. The discipline of architecture selection therefore runs in one direction: observe the environment, state the task requirements, derive the minimum architecture that satisfies the performance measure, and then justify why you stopped where you did. A justification that begins with the architecture ("we used a learning agent because machine learning is powerful") is not a justification at all; it is a preference dressed up as an argument. The sections below set out a repeatable procedure: profile the environment, map requirements to architectures, weigh performance against complexity, check the data and maintenance constraints, write a defensible argument, and test the procedure against worked scenarios.

Start with the environment profile. The task environment, not the designer's ambition, determines which capabilities an agent must possess. Before any design decision is made, the environment should be characterised along the standard dimensions — observability, determinism, episodic versus sequential structure, static versus dynamic, discrete versus continuous, single-agent versus multi-agent, and known versus unknown dynamics — and each judgement should be recorded with the evidence that supports it.

  • Observability drives the need for internal state. If the agent's sensors reveal every aspect of the environment that is relevant to the current decision, then the current percept alone is a sufficient basis for action, and a simple reflex agent with condition–action rules can be optimal. The qualifier "relevant" matters: an environment may be technically partially observable yet fully observable with respect to the decision at hand. A thermostat cannot see the room's occupancy, furniture, or weather forecast, but the only variable its performance measure depends on is temperature, which it senses directly — so it is effectively fully observable and a reflex rule suffices. Conversely, if any part of the relevant state is hidden — a room the robot cannot currently see, a card an opponent holds, a machine component whose wear is not directly measurable — the agent must infer that state from the percept sequence. That inference requires memory plus a model of how the world evolves and how actions affect it, which is precisely the definition of a model-based reflex agent. Partial observability does not merely suggest internal state; it forces it, because without state the agent cannot distinguish two situations that produce identical percepts but demand different actions.
  • Dynamism requires tracking change that the agent did not cause. In a static environment the world only changes when the agent acts, so a snapshot taken at decision time remains valid while the agent deliberates. In a dynamic environment — pedestrians moving, prices fluctuating, other robots repositioning stock — the world evolves independently. The agent's internal model must therefore contain two distinct kinds of knowledge: a transition model of how its own actions change the world ("if I move forward 1 m, my position advances"), and a world-evolution model of how the world changes on its own ("vehicles ahead continue at roughly their current velocity"). Semi-dynamic environments, where the world is static but the performance score degrades with elapsed time (as in chess with a clock), impose a related constraint: deliberation itself has a cost, which pushes back against expensive architectures.
  • Competing objectives and probabilistic outcomes signal utility. A goal is a binary predicate: a state either satisfies it or it does not. That representation is adequate when there is exactly one notion of success and all ways of achieving it are equally acceptable. It breaks down in two common situations. First, when outcomes are uncertain, the agent must compare a plan that reaches the goal with probability 0.95 and mild cost against one that reaches it with probability 0.6 and no cost — a comparison that binary goal satisfaction cannot express. Second, when several objectives conflict (speed versus fuel, throughput versus fairness, profit versus risk), there is no single goal predicate; there is a preference ordering over bundles of attributes. Both cases require a utility function that maps states or outcome distributions to a scalar, and a decision rule of maximising expected utility.
  • Unknown or shifting dynamics point to learning. If the designer cannot write down the transition model, the rule set, or the utility weights in advance — because the dynamics are unknown, because users' preferences drift, or because an adversary adapts — then the correct behaviour has to be discovered from experience. This requires a learning agent: a performance element that selects actions, a critic that evaluates outcomes against a fixed external performance standard, a learning element that modifies the performance element in response to the critic's feedback, and a problem generator that proposes exploratory actions so the agent does not stagnate on locally adequate behaviour. Note the precondition hidden in this: learning is only available if a usable feedback signal exists.
  • Document the profile explicitly. Record the property, the verdict, and the evidence, so the later architecture argument can be traced back to observations rather than assertions. A compact table is the most usable form:

Map task requirements to architectures. Once the environment is profiled, the mapping from requirement to architecture is largely mechanical. The guiding question for each candidate is: what can this architecture represent that the one below it cannot?

  • Condition–action responses with no history requirement → simple reflex agent. The agent program is a set of rules of the form if percept matches condition then do action. It is fast, trivially testable, and has no state to corrupt. It is appropriate when the correct action is a pure function of the current percept: an airbag controller, a thermostat, a spam filter applied to a single message in isolation, or the classic vacuum agent that senses both its location and the dirt status of the square it occupies.
  • Memory of unobserved state or action effects → model-based reflex agent. The same condition–action rules operate, but their conditions are evaluated against an estimated world state rather than the raw percept. The agent maintains state ← UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept, transition_model, sensor_model). This is the right choice when the agent must remember where it has been, what it has already done, or what is probably true but currently unseen — for example a robot tracking which rooms it has cleaned, or a fault-monitoring agent tracking a machine's hidden wear level.
  • Desired end state reached via search or planning → goal-based agent. Here the agent is given an explicit goal predicate and uses the transition model to search for an action sequence that reaches a goal state. The architecture's defining capability is lookahead: considering the future consequences of candidate action sequences before committing. It is required whenever the right immediate action depends on a destination that is not encoded in any local rule — route finding, puzzle solving, assembly sequencing. It also brings flexibility: change the goal and the behaviour changes without rewriting any rules, whereas a reflex agent would need a new rule set.
  • Trade-offs, degrees of success, or risk → utility-based agent. The goal predicate is replaced (or supplemented) by a utility function U(state) or U(outcome), and the agent chooses the action maximising expected utility: a* = argmax_a Σ_s' P(s' | s, a) · U(s'). This is the only architecture in the family that can rationally resolve conflicts between objectives, compare partial successes, or trade probability of success against cost.
  • Behaviour that must be discovered or refined over time → learning agent. Learning is a wrapper, not a rival: a learning agent's performance element can itself be reflex, model-based, goal-based, or utility-based. The decision to learn is therefore orthogonal to the decision about what structure the performance element has, and both decisions must be justified separately.

Weighing performance against complexity. The mapping table tells you what each architecture can do; it does not tell you what the task needs. That determination comes from the performance measure, which must be stated first and stated precisely. "Clean the floor" is not a performance measure; "percentage of floor area with dirt level below threshold, averaged over an eight-hour shift, minus 0.1 per unit of energy consumed, minus 50 per collision with a human" is. Once written, it functions as a test: start from the simplest architecture and ask whether it can attain an acceptable score. Escalate only when you can name the specific deficiency that causes failure.

  • Per-decision latency is a real constraint. Reflex rule evaluation is effectively constant time. State estimation adds a per-cycle update whose cost grows with the state representation; particle filters and Bayesian updates can be expensive. Goal-based search is worse: uninformed search over a branching factor b to depth d costs on the order of bd node expansions, and even with good heuristics the cost is many orders of magnitude above a rule lookup. Expected-utility calculation adds a summation over outcomes for every candidate action, and when combined with lookahead it multiplies the search cost. In a semi-dynamic or safety-critical setting, an architecture that produces a better decision too late produces a worse outcome. An obstacle-avoidance response computed in 300 ms by a planner is inferior to a cruder one computed in 5 ms by a reflex rule.
  • Models and goal representations are artefacts that must be built and maintained. Choosing a model-based or goal-based design commits the team to authoring a transition model, keeping it synchronised with the real world (new aisle layout, new product categories, changed road network), and validating it. Model drift is a silent failure mode: the agent behaves confidently and wrongly because its model no longer matches reality. The total cost of a model-based design is therefore not the implementation cost but the lifetime maintenance cost.
  • Hybrid layering is usually the honest answer for physical systems. Rather than picking one architecture, layer them: a fast reflex layer handles safety-critical, short-horizon responses with hard latency guarantees and simple, auditable rules; above it a model-based layer maintains the world estimate; above that a goal- or utility-based deliberative layer plans over a longer horizon. The reflex layer is given override authority, so that a planner failure or a late plan cannot cause a collision. This is the standard structure of robotic control stacks and of the subsumption-style architectures that preceded them.
  • Do not choose learning for novelty. If the correct behaviour is known, writable as rules, and verifiable by inspection or testing, a deterministic rule set is superior on almost every axis that matters: it is cheaper, it is predictable, it can be formally checked, it needs no training data, and it fails in ways engineers can diagnose. A learned policy introduces data dependencies, distributional-shift risk, opacity, and a monitoring obligation. Learning earns its place when the correct behaviour is genuinely unknown or genuinely non-stationary — not when it merely sounds more sophisticated.

Data, feedback, and maintainability constraints. Architectures impose obligations on the organisation that operates them, and these obligations often decide the question when the technical analysis leaves two candidates close together.

  • Learning needs a critic and experience. The critic's job is to convert raw percepts into an evaluation against a fixed performance standard, and it can only do so if some observable consequence correlates with success. A recommendation agent has an excellent feedback signal (clicks, dwell time, purchases, returns). A medical triage agent may have a signal available only after weeks, for a small number of cases, and with severe consequences for exploratory actions. When feedback is absent, delayed beyond usefulness, biased by the agent's own past choices, or when exploration is ethically or physically unsafe, the learning architecture is simply unavailable regardless of its theoretical appeal. Quantify this before committing: how many labelled or rewarded episodes per day, with what delay, and what is the cost of a bad exploratory action?
  • Reflex rule sets scale badly. Rules are cheap at ten and brittle at a thousand. As conditions multiply, rules begin to overlap and conflict, priority schemes become opaque, and each new requirement risks regressions in unrelated behaviour. Combinatorial growth in the number of distinguishable percepts is the usual symptom that a declarative model plus a search or inference procedure would be cheaper to maintain than an ever-expanding rule table.
  • Utility functions must be elicited and calibrated, and are politically contested. Writing U = 0.5·speed − 0.3·fuel + 0.2·priority looks like engineering, but each coefficient encodes a value judgement: how many minutes of delay is one unit of fuel worth? How much risk of a late delivery is acceptable to save cost? Different stakeholders — operations, finance, safety, customers — will answer differently, and the weights may need periodic renegotiation. Plan for elicitation workshops, sensitivity analysis (does the chosen policy change if a weight moves by 20%?), and a documented owner for the weights.
  • Explainability and auditability can override raw performance. In regulated domains — lending, hiring, clinical decision support, public-sector eligibility — the ability to state why an action was taken may be a legal requirement. An explicit rule or an explicit goal-and-plan representation yields a natural explanation ("rule 17 fired because the account was 90 days overdue"; "this route was chosen because it was the shortest path satisfying the delivery-window constraint"). A learned policy may outperform both and still be unacceptable if its decisions cannot be justified to an auditor or appealed by an affected individual.
  • Ask who maintains it. An architecture that only its original author can modify is a liability. If the operating team are logistics supervisors, a goal-based agent whose goals they can edit through an interface is more maintainable than a utility-based agent whose weights interact in non-obvious ways, which is in turn far more maintainable than a learned policy requiring retraining pipelines, data versioning, and drift monitoring. Match the architecture to the competence and staffing of the people who will own it for the next five years.

Building a defensible justification. A justification is a short, structured argument that another engineer could check. It has five parts, in order.

  • Evidence first: PEAS and environment properties. State the Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, and Sensors, then the environment property table. These are the premises of the argument. For a delivery robot: P = parcels delivered on time, energy used, collisions, customer rating; E = campus pathways with pedestrians, lifts, weather; A = drive wheels, steering, lid actuator, speaker; S = GPS, lidar, cameras, wheel encoders, lid sensor, network link.
  • Chosen architecture and the necessary capability. Name the architecture and name the one capability that makes it necessary — the thing the next simpler design cannot represent. "Utility-based, because the performance measure combines punctuality, energy, and collision risk with explicit exchange rates, and no binary goal predicate can rank a plan that is two minutes slower but halves collision probability."
  • Rejected alternatives, both simpler and more complex. Naming a simpler alternative proves you did not over-engineer; naming a more complex one proves you did not under-engineer out of convenience. "A goal-based agent was rejected because it cannot trade punctuality against risk. A learning agent for route policy was rejected because the campus network is known and stable, feedback on route quality arrives only once per delivery, and an exploratory route near a stairwell carries unacceptable risk."
  • Residual risks and failure modes. Every choice leaves something uncovered. State it: "The utility weights are fixed and will not adapt to seasonal pedestrian density; the world-evolution model assumes constant pedestrian velocity, which fails in crowd surges; and the agent has no mechanism to detect that its map is stale."
  • Migration triggers, stated measurably. Specify in advance what observation would justify a different architecture, so the decision can be revisited on evidence rather than opinion. "Migrate to a learning agent for pedestrian prediction if constant-velocity prediction error exceeds 1.5 m at a 2 s horizon in more than 10% of encounters over a four-week window. Add a reflex override layer if emergency-stop latency exceeds 150 ms at the 99th percentile. Revisit utility weights if on-time delivery falls below 92% for two consecutive months."

Comparative worked scenarios. The procedure is best understood by watching the same analysis applied to variations of a task, where small changes in the environment profile flip the architectural verdict.

  • Vacuum cleaner with full dirt sensing. PEAS: P = squares cleaned per time step, penalty for movement; E = two squares A and B; A = Left, Right, Suck; S = location sensor, dirt sensor. The dirt sensor reports the status of the current square and the location sensor reports position, so the decision-relevant state is fully observable, the environment is static and deterministic, and each decision is effectively episodic. Three rules suffice: if dirty then Suck; if at A then Right; if at B then Left. A simple reflex agent is adequate and cheapest; a model-based design would add a state variable that provably never changes the chosen action, buying nothing. Rejected more complex alternative: goal-based planning, rejected because the plan it would produce is identical to the rule output at a far higher cost per decision.
  • Vacuum cleaner with limited sensing across many rooms. Change one premise: the robot has only a local dirt sensor, no global position sensor, and operates across twenty rooms. Now the environment is partially observable. A reflex agent cannot tell "room already cleaned" from "room not yet visited" — the percepts are identical — so it will wander, re-clean, and leave areas untouched; worse, a deterministic reflex agent can enter an infinite loop. The agent must maintain an occupancy and coverage map updated from odometry and percepts, i.e. state ← UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept). A model-based reflex agent is required, and the necessary capability is coverage tracking. Residual risk: odometry drift corrupts the map, so a localisation correction mechanism (or periodic docking reset) is needed.
  • Route-finding delivery robot with a defined destination. PEAS: P = parcels delivered on time; E = a known campus path network; A = drive and steer; S = GPS, encoders, lidar. The environment is sequential: the correct turn at the current junction depends entirely on where the robot is ultimately going, and no local condition–action rule can encode that dependency for arbitrary destinations. The agent needs a goal predicate (at(destination)), a transition model of the path graph, and a search algorithm such as A* with a straight-line-distance heuristic. A goal-based agent is required, and the necessary capability is lookahead over action sequences. A model-based reflex agent was rejected because it would require a separate hand-written rule set per destination — an unmaintainable combinatorial explosion. A utility-based agent was rejected at this stage because the performance measure contains a single objective with no trade-offs to resolve.
  • Delivery robot balancing speed, fuel use, and parcel priority. Now the performance measure becomes multi-objective: minimise lateness, minimise energy, honour parcel priority, and avoid risky corridors. Two candidate routes can no longer be compared by a goal predicate, because both reach the destination — they differ in degree. The agent must score outcomes, for example U = −1.0·expected_lateness_minutes − 0.4·energy_kWh + 2.5·priority_served − 8.0·P(near-miss), and select the plan maximising expected utility over the path distribution. A utility-based agent is required; the necessary capability is commensurating conflicting objectives and handling stochastic travel times. The organisational cost is real: the four coefficients must be elicited from operations and safety stakeholders, documented, owned, and re-examined by sensitivity analysis.
  • Recommendation agent facing shifting user preferences. PEAS: P = long-run engagement and satisfaction, penalised for irrelevant or harmful suggestions; E = a catalogue and a population of users whose tastes drift seasonally and individually; A = present a ranked list; S = clicks, dwell time, purchases, ratings, returns. The environment's dynamics are unknown at design time and non-stationary thereafter: no fixed rule set or hand-written utility function stays accurate, because the preference model it encodes decays. The feedback signal, however, is abundant, fast, cheap, and low-risk to explore against. This is the textbook case for a learning agent: the performance element ranks items (often utility-based, scoring predicted relevance), the critic converts behavioural signals into an evaluation against the fixed standard of long-run satisfaction, the learning element updates the scoring model, and the problem generator deliberately injects exploratory recommendations so the agent discovers new preferences instead of exploiting a stale profile. Residual risks to acknowledge: feedback loops that narrow the catalogue, popularity bias, and the explainability gap — which is why many deployments wrap the learned scorer in explicit business rules for exclusions and compliance.
  • Autonomous vehicle: a hybrid stack. No single architecture satisfies this environment, which is partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, continuous, multi-agent, and safety-critical with hard real-time deadlines. The resolution is layering. A reflex layer implements emergency braking and lane-keeping limits as simple, auditable, formally checkable rules with guaranteed millisecond-scale latency and override authority. A model-based layer fuses lidar, radar, camera, and map data into a tracked world state including occluded objects and other vehicles' predicted trajectories. A utility-based deliberative layer plans over a several-second horizon, trading progress, comfort, legality, and collision risk under uncertainty. Learning appears inside specific components — perception, intent prediction — where the dynamics genuinely cannot be hand-specified, while the safety layer deliberately remains non-learned so that its behaviour can be verified. The justification writes itself from the profile: each layer exists because a named environment property makes the layer below insufficient, and the reflex layer exists because latency and verifiability make the layer above untrustworthy as a sole safeguard.

Once you can name the agent architectures — simple reflex, model-based reflex, goal-based, utility-based, and learning — the harder question becomes: which one should you actually build? This topic gives you a decision framework. You start from the environment profile (observable or partial, deterministic or stochastic, episodic or sequential, static or dynamic, discrete or continuous, single- or multi-agent), translate the task requirements into capabilities the agent must have, and then select the simplest architecture that meets those requirements.

The framework matters because the "most sophisticated" architecture is rarely the right answer. A learning agent needs training data, a feedback signal, and ongoing maintenance; a utility-based agent needs a defensible utility function; a model-based agent needs an internal state model somebody has to keep correct. Every added capability buys performance at the cost of complexity, data dependence, and long-term upkeep. A good justification names the alternatives you rejected and states, in terms of the environment profile, why they were rejected.

Designing Agent Programs for Sample Environments

Designing Agent Programs for Sample Environments

▶

Designing an agent program is not an act of inspiration; it is an act of specification followed by elimination. You first pin down exactly what the agent is being judged on and what world it inhabits, and only then do you choose the simplest internal organisation that can plausibly score well on that judgement. The sequence matters: a design that begins with "let's use reinforcement learning" or "let's write a few if-then rules" has already committed to an architecture before anyone has checked whether the environment supplies the percepts that architecture needs. The material below walks through the design discipline — PEAS first, then architecture selection, then critique — and applies it to four progressively harder sample environments: a vacuum world, an autonomous navigation task, a trading agent, and a game-playing agent.

Starting with a PEAS Specification

PEAS stands for Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. It is the design contract. Each of the four fields does a distinct job, and the most common beginner errors come from blurring them.

The performance measure must be a scalar or a ranked set of criteria, not a description of behaviour. A statement like "the agent should move efficiently and clean up dirt" is a behaviour sketch, not a measure: two designs can both satisfy it while producing different outcomes, and nothing in the sentence tells you which one won. A measure must be computable by an external observer from the sequence of environment states the agent produced. Compare:

  • Not a performance measure: "Vacuums the room thoroughly and does not waste time."
  • A performance measure: "Score = (1 point for each square that is clean at each of the 1000 time steps) − (1 point per movement action) − (50 points if the agent falls down the stairs)."

The second version is a scalar. It is also falsifiable: you can run two agent programs and read off which one is better. Where a single scalar is genuinely impossible — as is usually the case in navigation or trading — you state a ranked (lexicographic) set instead: "First, never violate a traffic law or collide; subject to that, minimise expected journey time; subject to that, minimise fuel use; subject to that, maximise comfort." Ranking is weaker than a scalar because it refuses to trade a tiny amount of safety for a large amount of speed, but it is honest about priorities and it is still objective.

Note also that the performance measure is defined over environment states, not over the agent's own beliefs. An agent that believes the floor is clean scores nothing; an agent whose actions made the floor clean scores. This distinction is what stops designers from accidentally rewarding self-deception.

Environment properties must be listed explicitly, because each property is the reason a particular architecture is needed or not needed. The six standard dimensions are:

Enumerating actuators bounds the action space. The agent program is a function that returns an action; if you have not listed the legal actions, you have not defined the function's codomain. Writing "Actuators: wheels, brakes, steering" is too vague to code against. Write the action set: {Accelerate(a) | a ∈ [−4, +2] m/s²} × {Steer(δ) | δ ∈ [−30°, +30°]} ∪ {SignalLeft, SignalRight, Horn}. This immediately exposes design problems: if the performance measure penalises discomfort but the actuator list has no way to limit jerk, the measure cannot be optimised.

Enumerating sensors determines the percept delivered on each cycle — and, crucially, exposes the gap between what the agent needs and what it can observe. This is the single most productive step in the whole PEAS process. For each item of information the decision rule requires, ask: which sensor supplies it, and at what noise and latency? Anything required but unsupplied must be inferred, and inference needs internal state and a transition model. The sensor list is therefore the formal proof that a reflex agent is or is not sufficient.

Vacuum World: Reflex versus Model-Based Design

The two-square vacuum world is deliberately trivial, which is exactly why it is a good instrument: it lets you see architecture choice forced by a single environment property rather than by complexity.

The fully observable case. The percept is a pair (Location, Status) with Location ∈ {A, B} and Status ∈ {Clean, Dirty}. There are exactly four possible percepts, and therefore the complete percept-to-action table has four rows:

PerceptAction
[A, Dirty]Suck
[A, Clean]Right
[B, Dirty]Suck
[B, Clean]Left

Four rules — and in fact only two distinct decisions ("if dirty, suck; otherwise move to the other square"). The agent program is a two-line lookup:

function REFLEX-VACUUM-AGENT(percept) returns action
    (location, status) <- percept
    if status = Dirty then return Suck
    else if location = A then return Right
    else return Left

This is a simple reflex agent, and it is provably adequate here because the percept uniquely identifies everything the decision depends on. Note the honest caveat: with the "+1 per clean square per step, −1 per move" measure, this agent oscillates forever between clean squares and bleeds points. Under the simpler "+1 per clean square per step" measure with no movement penalty, it is optimal. The architecture is adequate; the policy is only optimal relative to the measure you wrote. This is already an argument for evaluating designs against the stated measure rather than against intuition.

Removing the location sensor. Now the percept is just Status. There are two possible percepts, so a reflex agent can implement only four possible policies (one action choice for each of two percepts). Examine them:

  • Always Suck when Dirty, always Right when Clean: the agent reaches square B, finds it clean, and keeps executing Right against the wall forever. If square A later becomes dirty, it is never revisited. Permanent failure.
  • Always Suck when Dirty, always Left when Clean: symmetric failure at square A.
  • Suck regardless of status: never moves; the other square is never cleaned.
  • Move regardless of status: never cleans anything.

There is no deterministic reflex policy that cleans both squares and stops wasting moves, because two different world states produce the identical percept [Clean] while demanding different actions ("go right" from A, "stay or stop" at B). This is the textbook demonstration that partial observability breaks simple reflex agents. A randomised reflex agent (flip a coin between Left and Right when Clean) does eventually clean everything, which is worth noting — randomisation can rescue reflex agents from infinite loops — but it is inefficient and its expected score is poor.

Restoring correct behaviour with internal state. A model-based reflex agent keeps a small amount of memory and a transition model, and uses the percept to update it:

persistent: belief = {A: Unknown, B: Unknown}   # believed cleanliness
            pos = A                              # believed position
            last_action = None

function MODEL-BASED-VACUUM-AGENT(status) returns action
    # 1. UPDATE-STATE: fold last_action and new percept into the model
    if last_action = Right and pos = A then pos <- B
    if last_action = Left  and pos = B then pos <- A
    belief[pos] <- status
    if last_action = Suck then belief[pos] <- Clean

    # 2. RULE-MATCH on the internal state, not on the raw percept
    if belief[pos] = Dirty then action <- Suck
    else if belief[other(pos)] ≠ Clean then action <- move-toward(other(pos))
    else action <- NoOp

    last_action <- action
    return action

Two things are doing the work here. The transition model ("Right from A lands me in B") lets the agent infer its position without a location sensor, by dead-reckoning from its own action history. The sensor model ("the dirt sensor reports the square I am standing on") lets it attribute the percept to the right square. Together they convert a percept sequence into a belief state, and the rules then fire on the belief state. Notice the NoOp branch: once both squares are believed clean, the agent stops moving, which is impossible for the sensorless reflex agent and which directly earns points under a movement-penalising measure. (If dirt can reappear, the model must decay Clean beliefs back to Unknown after some time — an assumption worth documenting.)

Scaling to an n-square grid. Now the asymmetry becomes decisive. If the agent is fully observable over the whole grid, the world state is "which of the n squares are dirty" × "where the agent is", i.e. n · 2ⁿ states, and a complete lookup table needs one row per state:

Squares (n)World states (n · 2ⁿ)Rows in a full percept-to-action tableSize of the model-based program

The table grows exponentially in the number of squares; the model-based program does not grow at all, because it stores a compressed representation (an n-bit dirt map plus a position) and computes the action from it rather than looking it up. This is the general lesson: a reflex table enumerates the state space, while a model-based program factors it. Exponential blow-up in rule tables is the structural reason real agents carry models.

Motivating a utility-based variant. Introduce the movement penalty: "+1 per clean square per step, −2 per move, −0 for NoOp." Now actions are no longer simply right or wrong; they trade off. Suppose the agent sits on a clean square and believes square 7 (eight moves away) is dirty with probability 0.6, and square 2 (two moves away) is dirty with probability 0.3. A goal-based formulation ("make every square clean") cannot choose between them — both satisfy the goal eventually. A utility-based agent computes expected net gain: travelling to square 2 costs 4 points and yields, with probability 0.3, a stream of +1 per remaining step; travelling to square 7 costs 16 points for a 0.6 chance of the same stream. With 20 steps remaining, the near square wins; with 400 steps remaining, the far square wins. The utility function makes the horizon and the probabilities first-class inputs to the decision, which is precisely what the goal formulation discards.

Autonomous Navigation: Goal-Based Design

Navigation is the canonical case where the right action now depends on a destination that is not in the percept. That single fact rules out reflex architectures.

The goal as an explicit state. Specify the goal as a predicate over states: GOAL(s) ≡ (s.position ∈ dropoff_zone) ∧ (s.parcel_delivered = true). The agent then searches a state space whose nodes are (position, heading, time) and whose edges are actions with costs, using A* over a road graph with an admissible heuristic such as straight-line distance divided by maximum legal speed. The output is an action sequence — a plan — rather than a single action:

function GOAL-BASED-NAVIGATOR(percept) returns action
    state <- UPDATE-STATE(state, last_action, percept, transition_model, sensor_model)
    if plan is empty or not VALID(plan, state) then
        plan <- SEARCH(state, goal, model)          # e.g. A* over the road graph
    action <- FIRST(plan); plan <- REST(plan)
    last_action <- action
    return action

The key architectural feature is the goal variable. Change the destination and the behaviour changes completely with no edit to the program — which is the practical benefit of goal-based design over compiled-in rules.

Partial observability and dynamism force replanning. A vehicle sees perhaps 100 m of road; it cannot observe a lane closure two kilometres ahead, a pedestrian behind a parked van, or the intentions of the car in the next lane. The world also changes while the agent deliberates: a plan computed for the state 400 ms ago may already be invalid. Hence the VALID(plan, state) test and the continual UPDATE-STATE step: the agent maintains a world model (map + tracked obstacles + its own pose, typically via a Kalman or particle filter) and treats its plan as a hypothesis to be revised, not a commitment. In practice this appears as a layered design — a slow global planner (route over the road graph, replanned every few seconds), a medium local planner (trajectory over the next 5 seconds, replanned at 10 Hz), and a fast reactive safety layer (emergency braking, running at 100 Hz). The reactive layer is a genuine reflex agent, retained precisely because reflexes are fast; it is subordinated to, not replaced by, the deliberative layers.

Contrast with pure obstacle avoidance. A reflex rule set such as "if obstacle ahead, turn right; else go forward" produces behaviour that looks purposeful and is utterly unreliable for a destination task. It has no representation of where it is going, so it cannot fail to reach the goal — it was never trying. Worse, it exhibits classic reflex pathologies: limit cycles around convex obstacles, oscillation in corridors, and the inability to deliberately back out of a dead end (backing out is locally "wrong" and globally necessary). Reaching a specified address reliably requires a goal representation and lookahead; this is an architecture-level limitation, not a tuning problem.

Competing objectives expose the limits of goals. Many plans reach the destination. Which one should the agent execute? Consider three candidate routes:

RouteTime (min)Fuel (L)Comfort (jerk events)Legal riskGoal satisfied?
Motorway242.13noneYes

All three satisfy GOAL(s). Goal satisfaction is a binary predicate and therefore cannot rank them. The designer either smuggles the ranking into the search cost function (which is already a partial move to utility) or admits it openly.

Upgrading to a utility function. Define U = −(w_t · time + w_f · fuel + w_c · discomfort + w_l · E[legal penalty] + w_s · E[collision cost]), with weights expressed in a common currency (money, or "equivalent minutes"). The planner then minimises expected cost rather than testing a predicate, and the ranked route table above resolves automatically once the weights are set — and, importantly, resolves differently for an ambulance (high w_t) than for a passenger shuttle (high w_c), without changing the program. The added design cost is real and should be acknowledged: you must elicit and calibrate weights, you must estimate probabilitiesfor rare events from sparse data, and you must defend the resulting trade-offs to regulators and passengers. The justification is that the environment is stochastic and the objectives genuinely conflict: there is no ordering of actions available to a goal-based agent that is correct for all stakeholders, so the trade-off has to live somewhere explicit. A utility function puts it in one auditable place rather than scattering it through ad-hoc tie-breaking rules.

One honest caveat: lexicographic safety constraints are usually kept outside the utility sum. Collapsing "never run a red light" into a weighted term means that a sufficiently large time saving can buy a red light, which is unacceptable. The practical pattern is a constrained utility maximisation: hard constraints filter the action set, and the utility function ranks whatever survives.

Trading Agents: Utility and Uncertainty

A trading agent is the cleanest example of an environment in which no action guarantees anything. Every architecture below utility-based is structurally unable to express the problem.

Utility over outcomes, not over a binary goal. The outcome of a trading decision is not "succeeded/failed" but a distribution over portfolio states. A workable utility function scores an outcome on several axes at once:

  • Profit and loss — the realised and unrealised change in portfolio value, net of commissions, spread, slippage and financing costs.
  • Risk exposure — position size relative to limits, concentration in a single name or sector, leverage, and sensitivity (delta, duration) to a market factor.
  • Variance / drawdown — the dispersion of outcomes, not just the mean. Two strategies with identical expected profit are not equally good if one has twice the volatility.

A standard formulation is mean–variance utility: U(w) = E[r(w)] − (λ/2) · Var[r(w)], where w is the portfolio weight vector, r the return, and λ > 0 the risk-aversion coefficient. Equivalently one can use a concave utility of wealth such as U(W) = log W, whose concavity is risk aversion: the pain of losing £10,000 exceeds the pleasure of gaining £10,000, so the agent declines fair gambles. This is not a detail — it is the formal reason a utility-based trader behaves sensibly where an expected-profit maximiser would bet the firm.

Environment properties. Written out explicitly:

PropertyValueJustification
ObservabilityPartially observableThe agent sees prices and visible order-book depth, not other participants' intentions, hidden/iceberg orders, or pending news.
DeterminismStochasticThe same order placed in the same visible state yields different fills and different subsequent prices.
EpisodesSequentialA position taken now constrains capital, risk limits and tax treatment later; today's trade moves tomorrow's price.

AgentsMulti-agent, partly adversarialOther traders — including those detecting and front-running your order flow — optimise measures that depend on your actions.

The multi-agent property has a specific consequence that single-agent reasoning misses: your own actions change the environment's response function. A large market order moves the price against you (market impact), and a predictable execution pattern invites exploitation. Hence real execution agents randomise order sizes and timing — a randomised policy is a rational response to an adversary, exactly as in game theory.

Expected utility maximisation as the decision rule. When no action guarantees the goal, the agent ranks actions by expected utility:

function UTILITY-TRADER(percept) returns action
    state  <- UPDATE-STATE(state, last_action, percept)      # prices, book, position, cash
    model  <- CURRENT-PROBABILITY-MODEL()                     # P(outcome | state, action)
    best   <- Hold; best_eu <- EU(Hold)
    for each action a in LEGAL-ACTIONS(state):                # includes size and limit price
        eu <- SUM over outcomes o of  P(o | state, a) * U(o)  # U includes risk/variance terms
        eu <- eu − TRANSACTION-COST(a) − EXPECTED-IMPACT(a)
        if eu > best_eu then best <- a; best_eu <- eu
    if VIOLATES-RISK-LIMITS(best, state) then best <- Hold
    last_action <- best
    return best

A concrete illustration. The model says a stock currently at £100 will, over the next hour, be at £102 with probability 0.55 and £97 with probability 0.45. Expected profit per share is 0.55(+2) + 0.45(−3) = +1.10 − 1.35 = −0.25 — negative, so even a risk-neutral agent holds. Change the probabilities to 0.65/0.35 and expected profit is +1.30 − 1.05 = +0.25 per share: a risk-neutral agent buys the maximum size. A mean–variance agent, however, also computes the variance of that payoff (≈ 5.7 per share) and sizes the position so that E[r] − (λ/2)Var[r] is maximised, which yields a small position. Notice that the architecture produces position sizing automatically; nowhere did anyone write a rule saying "trade smaller when uncertain."

Adding a learning element. The probabilities above were asserted; in reality they must be estimated, and the estimates decay as markets change (regime shift, new participants, altered microstructure). The agent therefore becomes a learning agent with the four standard components:

  • Performance element — the expected-utility trader above, which chooses orders.
  • Critic — compares realised outcomes against the model's predictions and against the performance measure: realised P&L net of costs, calibration error (did events predicted at 65% happen about 65% of the time?), and realised volatility versus forecast volatility.
  • Learning element — updates P(outcome | state, action), e.g. by Bayesian updating of a predictive model, refitting a return forecaster on a rolling window, or gradient updates to a neural estimator; it may also recalibrate the market-impact model from the agent's own fills.
  • Problem generator — deliberately places small exploratory orders in instruments or venues the current model rates poorly, so that the agent keeps collecting data outside its current comfort zone. Without it the agent suffers the classic feedback trap: it only ever observes fills for trades it chose to make, so it never learns that its pessimistic estimate for a neglected market was wrong.

The exploration cost here is literal money, which is why the problem generator must be budgeted: "no more than 0.5% of capital, and no more than 2% of expected daily loss budget, allocated to exploratory positions."

Critiquing the goal-based alternative. Suppose someone proposes a goal-based trader: "Goal = achieve a 15% return this year; act so as to reach that state." Three failures follow directly from the architecture:

  • It is indifferent among all plans that might reach the goal. Buying a diversified index on margin and putting the entire account on a single biotech binary event both "achieve 15%" in some reachable future. Goal predicates cannot rank by risk because risk is a property of the distribution, and a goal test looks only at individual states.
  • It induces perverse risk-seeking when behind. If the agent is down 10% in November, the only remaining plans that reach +15% are extremely high-variance ones, so goal satisfaction requires gambling. This is the well-documented "doubling down" pathology, and it is produced by the architecture, not by a bug.
  • It is indifferent to overshoot and to path. A +60% return scores the same as +15%, so profitable opportunities are declined once the target is met; and a path that dips to −40% before recovering is scored identically to a smooth one, even though the fund would have been liquidated by its investors en route.

The repair in every case is the same: replace the binary predicate with a utility function that is sensitive to the whole outcome distribution. That is precisely the definition of upgrading from a goal-based to a utility-based architecture.

Game-Playing Agents: Learning Architectures

Games are the standard laboratory for learning agents because the performance measure is unambiguous, the environment is cheap to simulate, and feedback is plentiful.

Mapping onto the four components. Take a self-play chess or Go program:

ComponentRole in generalInstantiation in a game-playing agent
Performance elementSelects actions given percepts; this is "the agent" in the non-learning senseSearch (alpha–beta or Monte Carlo tree search) guided by the current evaluation function and move-ordering policy
CriticJudges the agent's behaviour against a fixed external performance standard and emits a feedback signalScores positions and games: final result (win/draw/loss), or the difference between the predicted value of a position and the value backed up by deeper search
Learning elementModifies the performance element using the critic's feedbackGradient updates to evaluation-function weights or network parameters; tuning of search heuristics and move priors
Problem generatorProposes actions/experiences that are informative even if currently suboptimalExploration: temperature-based or ε-greedy move selection, Dirichlet noise at the root, randomised opening books, self-play against earlier/weaker versions

The division of labour is worth stating precisely: the performance element tries to win now; the problem generator deliberately sacrifices some of that to learn more; the critic supplies the standard; the learning element is the only component that writes to the performance element. Conflating critic and performance element is a common design error — if the agent grades itself using the same evaluation function it is trying to improve, it will happily confirm its own mistakes.

Defining the critic's feedback signal. Two sources, with different properties:

  • Terminal outcomes (+1 win, 0 draw, −1 loss) are unambiguous and perfectly aligned with the performance measure, but extremely sparse: one scalar per game of perhaps 80 moves. This creates the credit assignment problem — which of the 80 moves deserves blame? Monte Carlo methods solve it statistically by averaging over very many games.
  • Intermediate evaluation signals are dense and available every move. The classic device is temporal-difference learning, as in Samuel's checkers player and TD-Gammon: the critic's error signal is δ_t = V(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t) (with the true outcome substituting for V at the terminal state), i.e. the agent learns to make each position's value consistent with the value of the position that actually followed. Alternatively, the critic can compare the shallow static evaluation of a position with the value returned by a deep search from it, and train the evaluation to predict the deeper, more reliable number.

In both cases note what makes this a critic and not merely a reward: the standard is external and fixed (the rules of the game decide wins), and it is applied to the agent's own behaviour to produce a correction.

Why the problem generator must force suboptimal moves. A performance element that always plays its current best move is on-policy and self-confirming. Suppose the evaluation function wrongly assigns a low value to positions with doubled pawns. The agent will then never enter such positions, will never see their true outcomes, and will never receive the data needed to correct the error. The bad estimate is protected by the very fact that it is bad. The problem generator breaks the loop by occasionally choosing a move the performance element rates second- or third-best — sampling proportionally to visit counts with a temperature parameter, adding Dirichlet noise to root priors so that every legal move has some chance of being tried, or starting games from randomly chosen opening positions. The short-term cost is real (some games are lost that would have been won); the long-term payoff is an evaluation function that is accurate over a much wider region of the state space. This is the exploration–exploitation trade-off in its architectural form, and it is why the problem generator is drawn as a separate box rather than folded into the performance element.

Why hand-coded evaluation is brittle. A classical chess evaluation is a weighted sum of human-identified features: material (♙=1, ♘=♗=3, ♖=5, ♕=9), mobility, king safety, pawn structure, control of the centre. Writing one requires expert knowledge; tuning the weights by hand is slow; and the feature set itself encodes the designer's blind spots. In a state space of roughly 10⁴⁴ legal chess positions or 10¹⁷⁰ Go positions, no enumeration is possible and the evaluation function must generalise — it must give sensible values to positions no one has ever seen. Hand-coded functions generalise poorly at the boundaries: they misjudge long-term positional compensation for material, they are exploitable once an opponent discovers which feature is mis-weighted, and every rule added to patch one case tends to break another. Go was the decisive demonstration: decades of hand-crafted evaluation produced only amateur-level play, while learned value networks trained by self-play surpassed the best humans. The architectural lesson generalises beyond games — whenever the state space is too large to enumerate and too subtle to summarise in a few dozen hand-chosen features, learn the evaluation from outcomes.

The architectural consequence is sharp. Chess needs a model-based, utility-guided search agent with a learned evaluation; the internal state it must track is small. Also note the critic becomes harder to build: in poker a single hand's result is a terrible feedback signal because luck dominates, so the critic must be redesigned to measure decision quality rather than outcome.

Critiquing and Justifying Architecture Choices

A design is not finished when the architecture is chosen; it is finished when the choice is defended from both sides. The discipline below produces a short, reviewable justification that survives contact with a sceptical colleague.

1. State why the next-simpler architecture fails, citing a concrete environment property. Vague claims ("a reflex agent isn't smart enough") are not arguments. The correct form names the property and exhibits the failure. Examples:

  • Simple reflex → model-based: "Partially observable: with the location sensor removed, the world states (at A, both clean) and (at B, both clean) generate the identical percept [Clean] but require different actions, so no deterministic function from percept to action is correct. Internal state is mathematically necessary, not merely convenient."
  • Model-based → goal-based: "The destination is not a function of the current percept, and the correct first action at a junction depends on where the vehicle is ultimately going. Without an explicit goal variable, a new destination requires rewriting rules."
  • Goal-based → utility-based: "Multiple plans satisfy the goal and the environment is stochastic, so the goal predicate cannot rank a 21-minute bus-lane route with a likely fine against a 24-minute legal route. A binary test has no expressive capacity for trade-offs."
  • Utility-based → learning: "The probability model is non-stationary — market microstructure and participants change — so any fixed P(outcome | state, action) degrades. Feedback is abundant and cheap (every fill and every closing price), so the conditions for learning are met."

2. State why the next-more-complex architecture is unnecessary, citing avoided cost. This half is skipped far more often and is where most over-engineering originates. The claim takes the form "the extra machinery would buy nothing measurable on the stated performance measure, and here is what it would cost":

  • "A learning element for the two-square vacuum is unnecessary: the environment is fully specified, stationary, and tiny, and the optimal policy is already known in closed form. Learning would add a training regime, an exploration budget that loses points, and a non-deterministic component that is harder to certify — for zero attainable score improvement, since the hand-written policy is optimal."
  • "A utility-based vacuum is unnecessary when the performance measure has no movement penalty, because every non-wasteful action is equally good; the trade-off the utility function would express does not exist in the measure."
  • "A full POMDP solver for the delivery vehicle is unnecessary: the receding-horizon replanner achieves the measure, while exact POMDP solution is intractable at this state-space size and would blow the 100 ms control deadline."

Costs worth naming explicitly are computation (per-cycle latency, memory, energy), data (labelled examples, simulator fidelity, exploration losses), design and maintenance effort (who tunes the weights, who retrains the model, who debugs non-deterministic behaviour), and verifiability (a 4-row table can be exhaustively tested; a learned network cannot).

3. Identify the percept or state information each upgrade requires, and confirm it is obtainable. Every architectural step has a data precondition. If the precondition is not met, the upgrade is a fantasy:

ArchitectureInformation it requiresCheck to perform

Goal-basedAn explicit goal predicate and a searchable model of action consequencesIs the goal supplied externally and is the state space searchable within the time budget?
Utility-basedNumeric utilities on a common scale, plus outcome probabilitiesWho sets the weights, and can the probabilities be estimated with enough data?
LearningA feedback signal from a critic, and a safe way to exploreIs feedback observable, timely, and attributable? Is exploration affordable and safe?

Two examples of the check failing usefully. Autility-based medical triage agent requires utilities on a common scale across patients; if the institution cannot or will not state how it trades a 1% mortality increase against a four-hour wait, the weights do not exist and the "upgrade" would merely hide an arbitrary choice inside a constant. A learning agent for a rare-fault industrial inspector requires attributable feedback; if the ground truth about a missed fault only arrives eighteen months later as a warranty claim with no record of which inspection passed it, the critic cannot assign credit and the learning element has nothing to consume. In both cases the correct response is either to acquire the missing information (commission a clinical-preference study; instrument the inspection pipeline to log part IDs) or to stay at the simpler architecture and document why.

4. Evaluate the design against the stated performance measure, not against intuitive notions of intelligence. The temptation is to judge an agent by whether its behaviour looks clever. This is unreliable in both directions. The two-square reflex vacuum looks mindless and is optimal under the no-movement-penalty measure. A sophisticated planner that deliberates for 900 ms looks intelligent and scores terribly in a dynamic world with a 100 ms deadline, because by the time it acts the world has moved. Conversely, an agent that discovers an odd-looking but high-scoring behaviour — a trading agent that mostly holds, a navigation agent that takes a longer route to avoid an unprotected right turn — is performing correctly even though a human observer might call it timid.

The practical procedure is:

  • Instantiate the measure as an executable scoring function.
  • Define an environment generator that samples instances from the specified environment class (dirt distributions, traffic densities, market regimes, opponent pools), including adversarial and edge-case instances.
  • Run each candidate architecture over many instances and report the score distribution — mean and worst case, since a design with a good average and a catastrophic tail is usually unacceptable.
  • Compare against baselines: a random agent, the simplest reflex agent, and (where computable) an omniscient optimal agent, which bounds how much score is actually available.

This last point deserves emphasis. If the optimal policy scores 940 and the simple reflex agent scores 905, then the entire budget for model-based, goal-based, utility-based and learning machinery is 35 points. Measuring the headroom before building is the cheapest design decision available.

If the measure itself produces behaviour you find unacceptable — the agent exploits a loophole, or optimises a proxy at the expense of what you meant — the correct fix is to repair the performance measure, not to bolt a patch onto the agent program. An agent that scores well on a badly written measure has done its job; the specification is the defect.

5. Document assumptions so the design can be revisited. Every architecture choice rests on claims about the environment that may later become false. Record them as an explicit, dated list with the design decision each one supports, so that when the environment specification changes you can tell immediately which decisions are now unsupported. Assumptions should also be made testable at runtime wherever possible. An agent that monitors its own preconditions — "my predicted percept disagrees with my actual percept more often than the sensor model allows", "my realised variance is three times my forecast" — can detect that its design envelope has been exceeded and degrade safely (hand over to a human, revert to a conservative fallback policy, raise an alert) rather than continuing to act confidently on an invalid model. That self-monitoring is itself a small critic, and it is the cheapest form of robustness available to a non-learning agent.

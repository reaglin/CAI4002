Introduction

Once you know what admissibility and consistency mean, the practical question becomes: where do good heuristics come from, and how do you tell one apart from another? This topic answers both. A heuristic is only useful if it is both cheap to compute and close to the true remaining cost h*(n); the art lies in finding estimates that are as informed as possible while never overestimating. The standard recipe is relaxation — drop a constraint from the original problem, solve the easier version exactly, and use that exact cost as an estimate for the hard problem. Misplaced tiles and Manhattan distance both arise this way from the 8-puzzle, and the same idea generalizes to pattern databases, which precompute exact solution costs for a subproblem (say, the positions of tiles 1 through 4) and look them up during search.

Comparison matters just as much as construction. If h2(n) is greater than or equal to h1(n) at every node and both are admissible, we say h2 dominates h1, and A* using h2 never expands more nodes than A* using h1 — a strong theoretical reason to prefer more informed heuristics. When dominance does not hold, or when you want a quantitative measure, the effective branching factor b* summarizes how bushy the search tree effectively was, and careful empirical comparison over many random instances tells you what actually happens in practice. You will also see how to combine several admissible heuristics safely by taking their maximum, and when subproblem costs can be added rather than maxed.

As you watch, focus on the mechanics of relaxation: what constraint is being dropped, why the relaxed problem is easy to solve, and why its optimal cost can never exceed the original's. Try to restate each example in the formal language of admissibility you learned earlier, and keep asking whether a proposed heuristic is dominated by, or dominates, one you already know.

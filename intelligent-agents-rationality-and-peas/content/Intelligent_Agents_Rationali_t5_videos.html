Introduction

So far this module has given you vocabulary: sensors and actuators, percept sequences, PEAS, and the six or seven dimensions along which any task environment can be described — fully vs. partially observable, deterministic vs. stochastic, episodic vs. sequential, static vs. dynamic, discrete vs. continuous, and single- vs. multi-agent. This topic is where that vocabulary gets exercised. You will work through a checklist on familiar domains: chess (fully observable, deterministic, sequential, multi-agent), poker (partially observable because of hidden cards, stochastic, strategic), automated driving (partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, continuous, multi-agent — the hard case), image classification (episodic and static), and indoor robot navigation (partial observability plus continuous space).

The reason this matters is practical rather than academic. The environment profile tells you what kind of agent you need. A static, episodic, fully observable task may be solved by a simple reflex agent or a single-pass classifier; a partially observable, sequential task forces you to maintain internal state (a model-based agent); stochastic environments with competing outcomes push you toward utility-based reasoning; and unknown or changing environments argue for a learning agent. The second half of the topic walks that bridge from profile to architecture, and then warns you about the classification pitfalls students most often hit — calling chess stochastic because you cannot predict the opponent, calling driving discrete because the control signals are digitized, or forgetting that the agent's own uncertainty is not the same thing as environmental nondeterminism.

The videos below come from IBM Technology and are industry-facing rather than textbook-exact. Watch them to collect concrete deployed examples — drones, IoT monitoring, disaster response, assistants — and then practice running each example through the classification checklist yourself. When a video describes an agent type, ask which environment properties would justify that choice; when it shows an application, ask what the PEAS specification would look like. The videos supply the raw material; the classification work is yours.

Introduction

Once you can describe an agent in terms of sensors, actuators, and a PEAS specification, the next step is to characterize the world the agent operates in. AI researchers use six standard dimensions for this: fully vs. partially observable, deterministic vs. stochastic, episodic vs. sequential, static vs. dynamic, discrete vs. continuous, and single-agent vs. multi-agent. Each dimension is a question you ask about a task before you design anything: Can the agent see everything it needs to? Does the same action always produce the same result? Do earlier decisions constrain later ones? Is the world changing while the agent deliberates? Are there finitely many states and actions, or a continuum? Is anything else in the environment pursuing its own goals?

This classification matters because it is the single best predictor of how hard a task is and which agent architecture you will need. A fully observable, deterministic, episodic, static, discrete, single-agent environment can often be handled by a simple lookup or reflex rule. Weaken any one of those properties and you start needing internal state, probabilistic reasoning, search over sequences, or models of other agents. The vocabulary also lets you compare very different tasks — chess, a vacuum robot, a taxi, a medical diagnosis system — on the same scale.

The videos below come from IBM Technology and approach agents from an industry perspective rather than the textbook taxonomy. As you watch, do the translation work yourself: for every example system mentioned (a delivery drone, a chatbot, a planning agent), pause and ask where it falls on each of the six dimensions, and notice how the more capable agent types described in these videos are precisely the ones designed for harder environments — partially observable, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, and multi-agent.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos chiefly support MO4 and MO5. By presenting a ladder of agent types and contrasting one-shot generative systems with multi-step agentic ones, they give you concrete systems to classify along the observability, determinism, episodicity, dynamism, discreteness, and agent-count dimensions (MO4), and they make visible why a simple reflex rule suffices in easy environments while model-, goal-, utility-, and learning-based architectures are required in harder ones (MO5). They also reinforce the agent-environment picture from MO1, since every example is framed in terms of what the system perceives and what actions it takes.

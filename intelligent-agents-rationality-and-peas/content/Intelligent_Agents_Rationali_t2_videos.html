Introduction

A rational agent is one that, for every possible percept sequence, selects the action expected to maximize its performance measure, given the evidence from the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge the agent has. Those four ingredients — the performance measure, the agent's prior knowledge of the environment, the actions available, and the percept sequence to date — are the whole definition, and most confusion about rationality comes from quietly dropping one of them.

The single most important design move is where the performance measure lives: it evaluates outcomes in the environment, not the agent's internal sense of how well it is doing. A vacuum agent rewarded for dirt collected can cheat by dumping and re-sucking; one rewarded for a clean floor over time cannot. Rationality is also not omniscience — an agent cannot be blamed for outcomes it had no way to foresee — and it is not perfection or guaranteed success, since it maximizes *expected* performance, not actual performance in hindsight. What rationality does demand is information gathering (exploration, looking before crossing), learning from percepts over time, and autonomy from a designer's fixed assumptions. And because the performance measure and the environment are part of the definition, rationality is always relative to a specific task environment.

The videos below approach these ideas from the applied side. As you watch, keep translating what you see back into the formal definition: ask what the performance measure actually is, what the agent can perceive, what it is allowed to do, and whether a behavior being described as "smart" is rational by the definition or merely lucky. The third video is the most directly relevant to the measurement question — treat it as a warning about how badly chosen measures mislead us.

Introduction

So far you have seen problems that arrive pre-packaged: the 8-puzzle, the vacuum world, route finding on a tidy map. Real scenarios do not arrive that way. Someone hands you a messy description — scheduling deliveries, assembling a product, routing a robot through a cluttered room — and your job is to decide what counts as a state, what an action is, when you are done, and what a step costs. This topic gives you a repeatable procedure for doing that translation and for checking your answer before you spend hours implementing a search that was doomed by its formulation.

Two ideas carry most of the weight. The first is abstraction: a good state records exactly the detail that affects which actions are legal and whether the goal is reached, and nothing more. Pack in extra attributes and the state space explodes; strip out too much and the solution you find no longer maps back to a real plan. The second is validation: before you trust a formulation, you check that the goal test is a crisp predicate over states rather than a vague wish, that the cost function is consistent and non-negative in a single unit, and that the initial state is actually reachable toward some goal. The three named errors in this topic — over-rich states, ill-defined goal tests, inconsistent cost functions — are the failures these checks are designed to catch.

The videos below approach the topic from two angles. The first revisits problem space and search as a framework, which is the vocabulary you apply when formulating. The second is about root cause analysis, a structured diagnostic procedure from IT operations; watch it as a model of disciplined problem decomposition and transfer the habit of asking "what is the actual problem here?" to your own formulations rather than looking for AI content in it.

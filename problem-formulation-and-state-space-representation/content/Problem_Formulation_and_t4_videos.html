Introduction

Once you know the five components of a search problem — state space, initial state, actions, transition model, goal test, and path cost — the next step is to practise writing them down for real tasks. This topic works through the classic examples that recur throughout AI: the 8-puzzle, the vacuum world, 8-queens, and route finding or touring problems. Each one is small enough to reason about by hand, but each illustrates a different formulation issue, from the size of a permutation state space to the difference between incremental and complete-state formulations.

The deeper lesson is that the same task can be formulated in more than one way, and the choice matters enormously. For 8-queens, an incremental formulation that places any queen on any square yields roughly 1.8 x 10^14 sequences, while restricting placements to one queen per column with no attacks cuts the space to around 2,057 states. Abstraction is the tool behind this: we drop detail that does not affect solvability (the colour of the tiles, the weather on the route) and keep what does.

As you watch the videos below, keep a notepad with five headings and try to fill them in for whatever problem is on screen, even when the presenter does not use that vocabulary. Ask yourself: what exactly counts as a state here, how many are there, what does one action change, and how would I know a goal when I see one? Also notice when a video is discussing the state space itself versus the search tree built over it — the two are easy to conflate and the distinction is tested later in the module.

About this video

Part of an optimization algorithms series, this episode walks through the 8-puzzle and solves it with A* search. From the title and description you can expect a statement of the puzzle, a representation of board configurations and moves of the blank tile, a heuristic such as misplaced tiles or Manhattan distance, and a traced expansion of nodes toward the goal configuration. The roughly fourteen-minute length suggests a worked example rather than a quick overview.

Why this video

The 8-puzzle is the canonical sliding-tile formulation in this topic, and seeing it driven by an actual search algorithm makes the abstract components — states as tile arrangements, actions as blank moves, unit path cost — concrete and checkable.

Video 2: The 4 Queens Problem, AI & CSPs

a bit of intelligence · 5:51 · 15,056 views · 5y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

This short explainer introduces the n-queens puzzle, noting its origin with Max Bezzel in 1848, and uses the smaller 4-queens version to make the reasoning easier to follow. It frames the puzzle in the language of constraint satisfaction problems, so expect discussion of variables, domains, and the constraints that forbid queens from sharing a row, column, or diagonal.

Why this video

It directly supports the 8-queens subtopic and the comparison of formulations, since the CSP framing is exactly the kind of alternative representation that shrinks the state space relative to a naive incremental placement.

About this video

From a computer science teaching channel, this video presents the classic monkey-and-banana problem and its encoding in Prolog, alongside a series of other Prolog programs. Expect the problem to be described as a set of states (monkey position, box position, whether the monkey is on the box, whether the banana is held) with actions such as walking, pushing, climbing, and grasping, and rules that define legal transitions.

Why this video

It extends the worked-example set beyond the textbook four and shows a state represented as a tuple of relevant facts, which is a clear illustration of abstraction: only the attributes that affect reaching the banana are kept in the state.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos give you repeated practice at the core skill of MO3 — turning an informal puzzle description into a formal search problem — across sliding tiles, queens, and a planning-style scenario. Each presentation implicitly commits to a state representation, an action set, and a goal condition, so you can reverse-engineer the five components of MO1 and judge whether anything irrelevant has been carried into the state, which is the abstraction question in MO2. The A* treatment of the 8-puzzle lets you watch nodes being generated and expanded, supporting the search-tree versus state-space-graph distinction in MO4, while contrasting the CSP framing of queens with a generate-and-test approach gives you material for the critique of formulations and their complexity demanded by MO5.

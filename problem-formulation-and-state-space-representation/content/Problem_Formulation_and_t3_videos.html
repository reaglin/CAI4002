Introduction

Before an AI agent can search for a solution, someone has to decide what a "state" actually is. A state must capture exactly the information needed to decide what happens next — no more, no less. Include too little and the agent cannot tell legal moves apart; include too much (the color of the puzzle tiles, the time of day, the exact pixel position of a robot) and the state space explodes with distinctions that never affect the outcome. Abstraction is the disciplined act of throwing away detail that does not change solvability.

Once states are defined, the actions connect them, and the result is a state space graph: nodes are states, edges are actions. This graph is a mathematical object, not the physical world — the same physical room can map to a handful of abstract states, and a single abstract action can stand for a long sequence of real-world motions. The size of that graph, its branching factor (how many actions are available on average) and its depth (how far the goal lies from the start) are what determine whether search is feasible at all.

As you watch the video below, keep asking two questions. First: what information is being packed into one node, and what has been deliberately left out? Second: how fast does the number of reachable states grow as the puzzle gets bigger? Notice especially that a state is often more than just "where the player is" — in many puzzles the positions of several objects must be bundled together into one state.

About this video

Using the Theseus and the Minotaur puzzle as a running example, this video builds up the idea of a state space and then walks through algorithms that explore it — breadth-first search, Dijkstra's algorithm, and A*. Because the puzzle involves two moving pieces, it naturally illustrates why a state must bundle together several facts (both Theseus's and the Minotaur's positions) rather than just one. The presentation is visual, showing the maze alongside the graph of states that the maze induces.

Why this video

It is a clear, concrete demonstration of the distinction at the heart of this topic: the physical maze you see versus the abstract state space graph that search actually operates on. The visual treatment makes branching factor, depth, and the growth of reachable states tangible rather than abstract.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

The video supports MO1 by making the components of a search problem concrete — an initial configuration, legal moves, a transition to a new state, and a goal condition — within a single worked puzzle. It directly serves MO4 by separating the maze (the physical world) from the state space graph that algorithms traverse, which is the same distinction you will later apply to search trees versus state space graphs. Its multi-object state also reinforces MO2 and MO5: you can ask which details of the puzzle were kept because they affect future decisions and which were safely abstracted away, and judge whether the resulting representation is too rich or too sparse. For MO3, treat it as a template — the reasoning used here transfers directly to formulating the 8-puzzle, vacuum world, and route finding.

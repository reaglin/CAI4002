Introduction

Propositional logic lets us reason about fixed, atomic facts, but first-order logic adds variables, predicates, functions, and quantifiers. That extra expressive power creates a new problem: before we can apply an inference rule, we have to decide whether two expressions that look different can be made identical. A substitution binds variables to terms, and unification is the procedure that finds a substitution making two expressions match. When such a substitution exists, the most general unifier (MGU) is the one that commits to as little as possible, leaving the result as flexible as it can be for later inference steps.

Unification matters in this course because it is the mechanism that "lifts" propositional inference rules to first-order logic. Instead of grounding a knowledge base by substituting every constant into every quantified sentence, which can blow up or even be infinite, generalized Modus Ponens applies a rule directly to matching facts using a unifier. The same idea turns propositional resolution into first-order resolution: two clauses resolve when a literal in one unifies with the negation of a literal in the other, and the MGU is applied to the resolvent. This is the engine behind logic programming languages like Prolog and behind practical first-order theorem provers.

As you watch, look for three things. First, how a substitution is written and applied to an expression. Second, how matching is carried out term by term, including the case where unification fails because of conflicting bindings or a constant clashing with a different constant. Third, how the resulting unifier gets applied to the conclusion of a rule so that the inference produces a correctly instantiated new fact.

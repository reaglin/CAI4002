First-Order Logic: Objects, Relations, and Quantifiers

▶

Propositional logic gives us a clean, well-understood framework: a vocabulary of atomic symbols, five connectives, a truth-functional semantics, and complete inference procedures such as resolution refutation. What it does not give us is a way to talk about things. A propositional symbol like KingJohnIsGreedy is atomic — the logic cannot see inside it to notice that it concerns a particular individual (John) and a particular property (greed), and therefore it cannot relate that symbol to KingRichardIsGreedy in any systematic way. First-order logic (FOL), sometimes called first-order predicate calculus, repairs this by committing to a richer ontology: the world is assumed to contain objects, relations that hold among those objects, and functions that map objects to other objects. This single change in what the logic "sees" produces an enormous gain in expressive power while leaving the familiar machinery of connectives, models, and entailment intact.

Why first-order logic extends propositional logic

The central weakness of propositional logic is that it has no way to state a general rule about a class of objects. Consider the classic statement "All humans are mortal." In propositional logic, the only way to capture its content is to enumerate instances:

HumanSocrates ⇒ MortalSocrates
HumanPlato   ⇒ MortalPlato
HumanAristotle ⇒ MortalAristotle
...

Every human in the domain needs its own sentence. If the domain has ten thousand humans, the knowledge base needs ten thousand sentences; if a new human appears, the knowledge base must be extended by hand; and if the domain is infinite (say, the natural numbers), no finite propositional knowledge base can express the rule at all. In first-order logic the same content is one sentence:

∀x Human(x) ⇒ Mortal(x)

This sentence quantifies over the domain: it asserts something about every object at once, without naming any of them. The saving is not merely one of typing. It means the knowledge base states the rule once and the inference procedure instantiates it on demand for whichever objects turn out to be relevant.

The three ontological commitments of FOL map directly onto three kinds of symbol:

  • Objects — people, numbers, times, wars, colours, countries, legs, centuries. Anything with an individual identity can be an object. Objects are denoted by constant symbols, variables, and function terms.
  • Relations — properties and relationships that may hold among objects: is a king, is the brother of, is between. Relations are denoted by predicate symbols and evaluate to true or false.
  • Functions — mappings in which each object (or tuple of objects) is associated with exactly one other object: the father of, the left leg of, the successor of. Functions are denoted by function symbols and evaluate to objects, not to truth values.

Crucially, FOL retains everything propositional logic had. The connectives ¬, ∧, ∨, ⇒ and ⇔ behave exactly as before, with the same truth tables; the notions of model, entailment, validity and satisfiability are generalised rather than replaced. What is added is the layer of terms (expressions that denote objects) and quantifiers (operators that range over the domain).

FeaturePropositional logicFirst-order logic
Ontological commitmentFacts hold or do not holdObjects, relations among them, functions on them
Atomic unitProposition symbol (P, W1,3)Predicate applied to terms (Brother(John, Richard))
GeneralisationImpossible — must enumerateQuantifiers ∀ and ∃

InferenceDecidable (model checking, resolution always terminates)Semi-decidable: entailment is provable, non-entailment may loop forever

The last row is the price of admission. Because a first-order knowledge base may have infinitely many models over infinitely large domains, model checking by enumeration is no longer an option, and no algorithm can in general decide non-entailment. Church and Turing showed that first-order validity is undecidable; what remains is semi-decidability (a property sometimes called refutation completeness): if KB ⊨ α, a complete procedure such as first-order resolution will eventually find a proof, but if KB ⊭ α the procedure may never halt. Practical systems therefore restrict the language — for example to definite clauses, where forward and backward chaining are well behaved — or impose depth and time limits.

Syntax: constants, variables, predicates, and functions

A first-order vocabulary (or signature) consists of three classes of non-logical symbol, plus the logical apparatus shared by all first-order languages.

  • Constant symbols name particular objects: Richard, John, Crown, 2, UK. By convention they are capitalised. A constant always denotes exactly one object, though two different constants may denote the same object unless we say otherwise.
  • Variable symbols — conventionally lower case: x, y, z, p — do not name any fixed object. They are placeholders that range over the domain and acquire meaning only when bound by a quantifier.
  • Predicate symbols denote relations: King(x) (unary, so really a property), Brother(x, y) (binary), Between(x, y, z) (ternary). Each predicate has a fixed arity. A predicate applied to the right number of terms yields something that is true or false.
  • Function symbols denote functions: FatherOf(x), LeftLeg(x), Plus(x, y), Capital(c). A function symbol applied to terms yields another term — it denotes an object, and so it is not truth-valued. Writing LeftLeg(John) ∧ King(John) is a syntax error, because LeftLeg(John) is a leg, not a truth value.

Functions are the right choice whenever the relationship is total and single-valued: every person has exactly one biological father, so FatherOf can be a function; a person may have zero, one or many brothers, so Brother must be a predicate. One can always convert a function into a predicate with an extra argument (Father(x, y) meaning "y is the father of x") at the cost of needing extra axioms to say that such a y always exists and is unique.

These pieces compose according to a simple recursive grammar. A term is an expression that denotes an object:

Term → Constant | Variable | Function(Term, ..., Term)

So John, x, LeftLeg(John) and FatherOf(FatherOf(John)) are all terms; the last shows that function application nests arbitrarily deeply, which is how FOL can refer to infinitely many objects using a finite vocabulary. An atomic sentence (or atom) is a predicate symbol applied to the correct number of terms, or an equality between two terms:

AtomicSentence → Predicate(Term, ..., Term) | Term = Term

Examples: King(John), Brother(John, Richard), Married(FatherOf(Richard), MotherOf(John)), FatherOf(John) = Henry. An atomic sentence is true in a model exactly when the relation named by the predicate holds among the objects named by the terms.

Complex sentences are built from atoms using the propositional connectives, together with the quantifiers:

Sentence → AtomicSentence
         | ¬ Sentence
         | Sentence ∧ Sentence
         | Sentence ∨ Sentence
         | Sentence ⇒ Sentence
         | Sentence ⇔ Sentence
         | Quantifier Variable, ... Sentence
         | ( Sentence )

Quantifier → ∀ | ∃

Operator precedence follows the usual convention — ¬ binds tightest, then ∧, ∨, ⇒, ⇔ — and a quantifier extends as far to the right as possible, so ∀x P(x) ⇒ Q(x) parses as ∀x (P(x) ⇒ Q(x)), not (∀x P(x)) ⇒ Q(x). When in doubt, parenthesise. Some illustrative well-formed sentences:

¬Brother(LeftLeg(Richard), John)
King(John) ∧ ¬King(Richard)
Brother(Richard, John) ⇒ Brother(John, Richard)
∀x ∀y Brother(x, y) ⇒ Sibling(x, y)
∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John)

The universal quantifier (∀)

The sentence ∀x P(x) — read "for all x, P of x" — is true in a model precisely when P(x) comes out true under every possible substitution of a domain object for the variable x. Conceptually, if the domain were the finite set {Richard, John, Crown, LeftLeg(Richard)}, then

∀x King(x) ⇒ Person(x)

would be equivalent to the conjunction of its four instances:

(King(Richard) ⇒ Person(Richard))
∧ (King(John) ⇒ Person(John))
∧ (King(Crown) ⇒ Person(Crown))
∧ (King(LeftLeg(Richard)) ⇒ Person(LeftLeg(Richard)))

This "conjunction over the domain" reading is the best intuition pump for ∀, and it immediately explains the single most important stylistic rule in first-order logic: ∀ goes with ⇒. The quantifier ranges over everything in the domain, including crowns and legs, so a universal sentence needs an antecedent to filter down to the objects we actually mean. In the example above, the instances about the crown and the leg are vacuously true because King(Crown) is false, and an implication with a false antecedent is true. The implication acts as a guard.

Replacing ⇒ with ∧ is the classic beginner's error:

∀x King(x) ∧ Person(x)        ← WRONG

Expanded over the same domain, this says (King(Richard) ∧ Person(Richard)) ∧ (King(John) ∧ Person(John)) ∧ (King(Crown) ∧ Person(Crown)) ∧ ... — that is, everything in the universe is a king and a person, including the crown and Richard's left leg. It is a far stronger claim than intended and will be false in essentially every realistic model, poisoning the knowledge base.

Universals nest freely. To say that the sibling relation is symmetric:

∀x ∀y Sibling(x, y) ⇒ Sibling(y, x)

and transitivity of ancestry, with three nested universals:

∀x ∀y ∀z Ancestor(x, y) ∧ Ancestor(y, z) ⇒ Ancestor(x, z)

A single quantifier symbol with a list of variables, as in ∀x, y, z, is a common abbreviation for a run of quantifiers of the same type. Note also that the guard need not be a single predicate: ∀x Person(x) ∧ Adult(x) ⇒ CanVote(x) uses a conjunctive antecedent, which is perfectly correct because the ∧ is inside the implication, not replacing it.

The existential quantifier (∃)

The sentence ∃x P(x) — "there exists an x such that P of x" — is true in a model when there is at least one object which, substituted for x, makes P(x) true. The mirror-image intuition applies: ∃ behaves like a disjunction over the domain. For the four-object domain above,

∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John)

is equivalent to

(Crown(Richard) ∧ OnHead(Richard, John))
∨ (Crown(John) ∧ OnHead(John, John))
∨ (Crown(Crown) ∧ OnHead(Crown, John))
∨ (Crown(LeftLeg(Richard)) ∧ OnHead(LeftLeg(Richard), John))

and exactly one disjunct — the third — needs to be true for the whole sentence to hold. Note also that ∃ asserts "at least one", not "exactly one"; the sentence remains true if John is wearing two crowns at once.

Because ∃ is a disjunction, the natural partner connective is ∧: we want to assert that a single object has several properties simultaneously. Using ⇒ with ∃ is the complementary classic error:

∃x Crown(x) ⇒ OnHead(x, John)    ← WRONG

Expanded, this is a disjunction of implications, and it is satisfied as soon as any object in the domain fails the antecedent. Richard is not a crown, so Crown(Richard) ⇒ OnHead(Richard, John) is vacuously true, so the whole sentence is true — even in a world with no crowns at all, or where John is bare-headed. The sentence is almost a tautology and asserts essentially nothing. The symmetry is worth memorising:

QuantifierReading over the domainNatural partner connectiveTypical error and why it fails
∀xConjunction of all instances⇒ (guards the class)∀ with ∧ claims every object in the universe has the property
∃xDisjunction of all instances∧ (bundles properties on one object)∃ with ⇒ is trivially satisfied by any object failing the antecedent

When ∀ and ∃ are mixed, order matters enormously. Compare:

∀x ∃y Loves(x, y)     "Everybody loves somebody (possibly a different somebody each time)."
∃y ∀x Loves(x, y)     "There is one particular person whom everybody loves."

The second entails the first — if a universally beloved individual exists, then certainly everyone loves someone — but not conversely. In a world with Alice loving Bob, Bob loving Carol, and Carol loving Alice, the first sentence is true and the second is false. The reason is scoping: in ∀x ∃y ... the choice of y occurs inside the scope of x, so y may depend on x; in ∃y ∀x ... the choice of y is made first and must work for all x. (This dependency is exactly what Skolemisation makes explicit when converting to clausal form: the inner existential becomes a Skolem function of the outer universally quantified variable, Loves(x, F(x)), whereas an outer existential becomes a Skolem constant, Loves(x, B).)

Quantifier equivalences and nesting

Because ∀ is a generalised conjunction and ∃ a generalised disjunction, De Morgan's laws lift straight from propositional logic to the quantifiers. The two quantifiers are interdefinable through negation:

EquivalenceReading
∀x P(x) ≡ ¬∃x ¬P(x)"Everyone likes ice cream" = "There is no one who does not like ice cream"
∃x P(x) ≡ ¬∀x ¬P(x)"Someone likes broccoli" = "Not everyone dislikes broccoli"
¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x)"Not everyone likes broccoli" = "Someone does not like broccoli"
¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x)"No one likes broccoli" = "Everyone does not like broccoli"

These mirror ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ ¬A ∨ ¬B and ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ ¬A ∧ ¬B exactly. Strictly speaking only one quantifier is primitive; the other is definable. Practically, the equivalences are the tool for pushing negations inward when converting sentences to conjunctive normal form for resolution.

Regarding reordering: adjacent quantifiers of the same type commute freely, so ∀x ∀y S(x, y) ≡ ∀y ∀x S(x, y) and ∃x ∃y S(x, y) ≡ ∃y ∃x S(x, y). Mixed quantifiers do not commute, as the Loves example showed; the only valid one-way relationship is ∃y ∀x S(x, y) ⊨ ∀x ∃y S(x, y).

Scope and binding. The scope of a quantifier is the sentence immediately following it (after precedence is resolved). An occurrence of a variable inside the scope of a quantifier for that variable is bound; an occurrence outside any such scope is free. In

(∀x P(x, y)) ∧ Q(x)

the x in P is bound, but the y and the x in Q are free. A formula containing free variables is an open formula, not a sentence: it has no truth value in a model until the free variables are assigned objects. Only closed formulas (sentences) can be asserted into a knowledge base. Renaming a bound variable consistently throughout its scope changes nothing — ∀x King(x) and ∀z King(z) are the same sentence (alphabetic variants).

Shadowing. Reusing the same variable name in nested scopes is legal but confusing, exactly as in a programming language:

∀x [Person(x) ⇒ (∃x Dog(x) ∧ Owns(x, x))]

Here the inner ∃x re-binds x, so the outer x is invisible inside the brackets and Owns(x, x) says the dog owns itself — certainly not the intended "every person owns a dog". Rewriting with fresh names makes the intent unambiguous:

∀x Person(x) ⇒ (∃y Dog(y) ∧ Owns(x, y))

The practical rule is simple: give every quantifier its own variable name (standardising apart), which is also a mandatory preprocessing step in CNF conversion for first-order resolution.

Semantics: interpretations and models

In propositional logic a model is just a truth assignment. In first-order logic a model is a richer structure with two components:

  • A domain (or universe of discourse) D: a non-empty set of objects. Non-emptiness is required so that ∀x P(x) ⇒ ∃x P(x) remains valid and quantified sentences behave sensibly. Objects in the domain are mathematical abstractions — they need not be nameable by any constant in the vocabulary.
  • An interpretation I: a mapping that assigns a meaning to every non-logical symbol:
    • each constant symbol to exactly one object in D;
    • each predicate symbol of arity n to a set of n-tuples of domain objects — the tuples for which the relation holds (the relation's extension);
    • each function symbol of arity n to a total function from Dn to D, so that every input tuple has exactly one output object.

Consider a tiny model of the Richard-and-John world. Let the domain be D = {r, j, c, l}, where r is Richard the Lionheart, j is King John, c is a crown and l is Richard's left leg. An interpretation might be:

SymbolKindInterpretation in this model
RichardConstantr
JohnConstantj
KingUnary predicate{ ⟨j⟩ }
PersonUnary predicate{ ⟨r⟩, ⟨j⟩ }
CrownUnary predicate{ ⟨c⟩ }
BrotherBinary predicate{ ⟨r, j⟩, ⟨j, r⟩ }
OnHeadBinary predicate{ ⟨c, j⟩ }
LeftLegUnary functionr ↦ l, j ↦ l, c ↦ l, l ↦ l

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the interpretation of LeftLeg must be total: even though crowns have no legs, the function symbol must return something for every argument, so the model simply maps the awkward cases somewhere (here, to l). Modellers often introduce a dummy "invisible object" for this purpose, or avoid the issue by using a predicate instead. Second, nothing forces distinct constants to denote distinct objects, and nothing forces every object to have a name — l is unnamed by any constant and is reachable only through the term LeftLeg(Richard).

Truth is then defined compositionally. A term denotes an object: a constant denotes whatever I assigns it, a bound variable denotes the object currently substituted for it, and f(t₁,…,tₙ) denotes the result of applying I(f) to the objects denoted by t₁,…,tₙ. An atomic sentence P(t₁,…,tₙ) is true exactly when the tuple of denoted objects lies in I(P). Complex sentences are evaluated by the usual truth tables, and quantified sentences by the conjunction/disjunction-over-the-domain rules described earlier. So in our model:

  • Brother(Richard, John) is true, because ⟨r, j⟩ ∈ I(Brother).
  • King(Richard) is false, because ⟨r⟩ ∉ I(King).
  • ∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John) is true, witnessed by c.
  • ∀x King(x) ⇒ Person(x) is true: the only king is j, and ⟨j⟩ ∈ I(Person); the other three objects satisfy the implication vacuously.
  • ∀x King(x) ∧ Person(x) is false, since c is neither.
  • Brother(LeftLeg(Richard), John) is false, because ⟨l, j⟩ ∉ I(Brother).

With truth-in-a-model defined, the remaining semantic notions carry over unchanged from propositional logic, which is one of the great economies of the first-order framework:

  • Entailment. KB ⊨ α when α is true in every model in which every sentence of KB is true. Equivalently, there is no model that satisfies KB ∪ {¬α} — the fact exploited by resolution refutation.
  • Validity. α is valid when it is true in every model under every interpretation: for example ∀x P(x) ⇒ P(A), or ∀x (P(x) ∨ ¬P(x)). Valid first-order sentences are the theorems of pure logic.
  • Satisfiability. α is satisfiable when some model makes it true; unsatisfiable sentences such as ∃x (P(x) ∧ ¬P(x)) are true in none. As before, KB ⊨ α if and only if KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable.

The difference from the propositional case is purely computational. There, "every model" meant one of 2n rows of a truth table and could be enumerated. Here, "every model" means every choice of domain — of every finite size and infinite ones too — and every interpretation over it. Enumeration is impossible, so first-order reasoning must proceed by proof (unification, resolution, chaining) rather than by model checking.

Translating natural language into first-order sentences

Formalisation is a design task, not a mechanical one, and it is where most practical errors originate. A workable discipline is:

  • Fix the ontology first. Decide what the objects are (people? courses? time intervals? events?), then which properties and relationships you need as predicates, then which single-valued mappings are better expressed as functions. Write down the intended meaning of every symbol in English before writing any sentences — an undocumented predicate is a future bug.
  • Watch the quantifier cues. "Every", "all", "any", "each", "whoever" and bare plurals ("dogs bark") signal ∀. "Some", "a", "there is", "at least one", "someone" signal ∃.
  • Apply the connective rule. ∀ with ⇒, ∃ with ∧ — the overwhelmingly common pattern.
  • Test against small models. Invent a two- or three-object world, evaluate the candidate sentence, and ask whether its truth value matches your intuition. This catches both over-commitment (the sentence says more than the English, e.g. ∀ with ∧) and under-commitment (the sentence is vacuously true, e.g. ∃ with ⇒).

Worked examples, with a domain of people and animals:

EnglishFirst-order sentenceComment
Every student passed the exam.∀x Student(x) ⇒ Passed(x, Exam1)Universal guarded by implication.
Some student passed the exam.∃x Student(x) ∧ Passed(x, Exam1)Existential bundled with conjunction.
Every person owns a dog.∀x Person(x) ⇒ (∃y Dog(y) ∧ Owns(x, y))Mixed quantifiers; the dog may differ per person.
There is a dog that everyone owns.∃y Dog(y) ∧ (∀x Person(x) ⇒ Owns(x, y))Stronger claim: one shared dog.
Everyone's father is a person.∀x Person(x) ⇒ Person(FatherOf(x))Function term used in the consequent.
Brothers are siblings.∀x ∀y Brother(x, y) ⇒ Sibling(x, y)Bare plural reads as a universal.

Negative quantification. "No", "none", "nobody" and "never" have two equally correct renderings, related by the equivalences above. "No student failed" becomes either

¬∃x Student(x) ∧ Failed(x)           (negated existential)
∀x Student(x) ⇒ ¬Failed(x)           (universal with negated consequent)

Both are logically equivalent; the second is usually preferred in knowledge bases because it is already close to clause form. A sanity check: do not write ¬∀x Student(x) ⇒ Failed(x), which merely denies that all students failed — a far weaker claim.

Equality. The equality symbol is a built-in binary predicate whose interpretation is fixed in every model: t₁ = t₂ is true exactly when t₁ and t₂ denote the same object. It is indispensable for several idioms:

  • Asserting identity: FatherOf(John) = Henry.
  • Asserting distinctness: ¬(Richard = John), often written Richard ≠ John. Without such axioms a model is free to make two constants denote the same object — a "unique names" assumption must be stated explicitly in pure FOL.
  • Defining relations: ∀x ∀y Sibling(x, y) ⇔ (¬(x = y) ∧ ∃m ∃f ¬(m = f) ∧ Parent(m, x) ∧ Parent(f, x) ∧ Parent(m, y) ∧ Parent(f, y)) — note the explicit inequalities, without which a person would count as their own sibling.
  • Counting: "John has at least two brothers" is ∃x ∃y Brother(x, John) ∧ Brother(y, John) ∧ ¬(x = y); "exactly one" is ∃x P(x) ∧ (∀y P(y) ⇒ x = y).

Finally, a note on over- and under-commitment in practice. Under-commitment leaves out information the English conveys: translating "Spouses are of legal age" as ∀x ∀y Spouse(x, y) ⇒ Adult(x) forgets to say anything about y. Over-commitment asserts more than was meant: translating "Students take courses" as ∀x ∀y Student(x) ∧ Course(y) ⇒ Takes(x, y) claims every student takes every course, when the intended reading is ∀x Student(x) ⇒ (∃y Course(y) ∧ Takes(x, y)). Both failures are invisible on inspection and obvious the moment you evaluate the sentence in a three-object test model — which is exactly why the testing step belongs in the workflow.

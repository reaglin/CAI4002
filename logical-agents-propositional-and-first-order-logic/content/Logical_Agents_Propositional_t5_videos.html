Introduction

Propositional logic treats each statement as an indivisible atom: "Socrates is mortal" is just a symbol P, with no internal structure. That makes it impossible to express the fact that whatever holds for every human also holds for Socrates. First-order logic (also called predicate logic) fixes this by looking inside sentences, introducing constants that name objects, predicates that assert properties and relations, functions that map objects to objects, variables that stand for unspecified objects, and the two quantifiers — the universal quantifier for "for all" and the existential quantifier for "there exists."

This added expressive power is what lets a knowledge-based agent say general things about a world rather than enumerating every case. In this course it underpins everything that follows: once you can write first-order sentences, you can describe domains compactly, reason about them with inference procedures, and connect logical representation to the agent architectures introduced earlier in the module. The semantic side matters just as much as the syntax — an interpretation fixes a domain of objects and assigns meaning to each symbol, and a model is an interpretation in which your sentences come out true.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, the grammar: which symbols can legally appear where, and why a predicate applied to a term is a sentence while a function applied to a term is not. Second, the pairing of quantifier and connective — universal quantifiers normally go with implication, existential quantifiers with conjunction — and what goes wrong if you swap them. Third, quantifier order in nested sentences, since reversing two quantifiers usually changes the meaning entirely. Try translating a few English sentences yourself before the video gives its answer.

Propositional Logic: Syntax and Semantics

▶

Propositional logic — sometimes called Boolean logic or sentential logic — is the simplest formal language that is still powerful enough to support genuine automated reasoning. It is the natural starting point for the study of knowledge representation because it separates, with unusual clarity, two things that are easy to confuse in ordinary language: syntax, which governs which strings of symbols count as legal sentences, and semantics, which governs what those sentences mean and when they are true. A propositional sentence is a piece of notation; its meaning is supplied entirely by a model, a complete assignment of truth values to the symbols from which the sentence is built. Everything else in propositional logic — entailment, validity, satisfiability, equivalence, and the correctness of inference procedures — is defined in terms of these two ideas. This topic develops both in detail: the grammar that generates well-formed formulas, the models that interpret them, the truth tables that define the connectives, the recursive procedure that evaluates an arbitrary sentence in an arbitrary model, and the three semantic properties (validity, satisfiability, equivalence) that classify sentences by the sets of models that satisfy them.

Atomic sentences and proposition symbols. The indivisible building blocks of the language are proposition symbols, each of which stands for a single fact about the world that is either true or false. Conventionally they are written beginning with an uppercase letter: Rain, Umbrella, W1,3 (usually typeset W1,3 and read "there is a wumpus in square [1,3]"), B2,1 ("square [2,1] is breezy"), P, Q, R. A sentence consisting of nothing but a single proposition symbol is called an atomic sentence. The word atomic is meant literally: the logic cannot look inside the symbol. From the point of view of propositional logic, P1,2 is a single opaque token; the subscripts "1,2" are part of its name, not a structure that any rule of inference can decompose, index over, or quantify across. This is exactly the limitation that first-order logic later removes by introducing objects, relations, and quantifiers.

Two atomic sentences are special constants with fixed, model-independent meanings. True is the always-true proposition and False is the always-false proposition. They are grammatically atomic sentences, so they can appear anywhere a symbol can, but no model is free to reassign them. They are convenient in simplification: α ∧ True is equivalent to α, α ∨ True is equivalent to True, and α ∧ False is equivalent to False.

It is worth dwelling on the fact that symbol names are arbitrary labels. If a knowledge base uses the symbol Rain, the logic does not know anything about precipitation; it knows only that there is a symbol which, in any given model, is either true or false. The connection between the symbol and the world is held in the head of the knowledge engineer (and documented, one hopes, in a comment). This is a strength as well as a weakness: the inference machinery is completely general because it is completely blind to intended meaning. Consistently renaming every occurrence of Rain to X7 throughout a knowledge base changes nothing whatsoever about which conclusions follow.

Finally, a knowledge base is always written over a fixed vocabulary of proposition symbols, and that vocabulary alone determines the entire space of possible worlds under consideration. If the vocabulary is {P, Q, R}, then there are exactly eight possible worlds, no more and no fewer, and every semantic question about the knowledge base reduces to a question about which of those eight worlds are compatible with it. Enlarging the vocabulary doubles the space of worlds for each new symbol — a fact with major computational consequences.

Logical connectives. From atomic sentences, complex sentences are built using five standard connectives.

  • Negation (¬). The only unary connective. ¬P is read "not P" and is true exactly when P is false. An atomic sentence or a negated atomic sentence is called a literal: P is a positive literal and ¬P is a negative literal. Literals matter later because the normal forms used by resolution are built entirely out of them.
  • Conjunction (∧). P ∧ Q is read "P and Q"; the two parts P and Q are its conjuncts. It is true only when both conjuncts are true — it is the most demanding of the binary connectives, ruling out three of the four possible combinations.
  • Disjunction (∨). P ∨ Q is read "P or Q"; the parts are its disjuncts. It is inclusive or: true when at least one disjunct is true, including the case where both are true. English "or" is often exclusive ("you may have soup or salad"), so translation requires care; exclusive or must be written out, for example as (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬(P ∧ Q).
  • Implication (⇒). P ⇒ Q is read "P implies Q" or "if P then Q"; P is the premise or antecedent and Q is the conclusion or consequent. It is false in exactly one case: premise true, conclusion false. Everywhere else it is true. This material reading surprises newcomers, because it makes Rain ⇒ 2 + 2 = 4 true and makes any implication with a false premise vacuously true. The justification is that P ⇒ Q is not a claim of causation or relevance; it is simply a claim that the case "P true and Q false" does not arise.
  • Biconditional (⇔). P ⇔ Q is read "P if and only if Q" and asserts that the two sides always share a truth value in the model at hand. It is equivalent to (P ⇒ Q) ∧ (Q ⇒ P). Biconditionals are heavily used in knowledge engineering to state definitions, as in B1,1 ⇔ (P1,2 ∨ P2,1): a square is breezy exactly when a neighbouring square holds a pit. Writing only (P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ⇒ B1,1 would permit breezes with no cause, so the stronger biconditional is usually what is intended.

When parentheses are omitted, a standard precedence order resolves the ambiguity, from tightest to loosest binding: ¬, then ∧, then ∨, then ⇒, then ⇔. Thus ¬P ∧ Q ⇒ R ∨ S abbreviates ((¬P) ∧ Q) ⇒ (R ∨ S). Because precedence conventions vary between textbooks and tools, explicit parentheses are preferable in anything that must be read by other people.

Well-formed formulas and the formal grammar. The set of legal sentences is defined by a recursive grammar, usually presented in Backus–Naur Form (BNF). A string is a well-formed formula (wff) if and only if the grammar can generate it.

The grammar has a base case — every atomic sentence, including the constants True and False, is already a well-formed formula — and a recursive case: if α and β are well-formed formulas, then so are ¬α, (α ∧ β), (α ∨ β), (α ⇒ β), and (α ⇔ β). Here α and β are metavariables ranging over sentences, not symbols of the language itself. Because the recursive case can be applied to its own output without limit, sentences of arbitrary nesting depth are legal: from P and Q we may build (P ⇒ Q), then ¬(P ⇒ Q), then (¬(P ⇒ Q) ∨ R), then ((¬(P ⇒ Q) ∨ R) ⇔ (S ∧ ¬T)), and so on indefinitely. The language is therefore infinite even over a finite vocabulary.

An ungrammatical string such as P ∧ ∨ Q is not false — it is meaningless. Truth values are assigned only to well-formed formulas, so asking whether P ∧ ∨ Q is true in a model is like asking whether the string "the the of" is true. This is the key point about syntax: syntax alone says nothing about truth. The grammar is a filter that determines which expressions are legal and, just as importantly, imposes a unique parse structure on each legal expression so that the semantics has something unambiguous to work on. (P ∧ Q) and (P ⇒ Q) are equally well formed; the grammar does not prefer one, and it certainly does not tell us that one of them is true. Meaning arrives only with models.

Models and truth assignments. A model in propositional logic is a complete assignment of a truth value to every proposition symbol in the vocabulary. The word "complete" matters: a model leaves nothing undetermined. Over the vocabulary {P1,2, P2,2, P3,1}, the assignment

m1 = { P1,2 = false,  P2,2 = false,  P3,1 = true }

is one model; a partial assignment that fixes only P1,2 is not a model (it is a partial assignment, useful inside algorithms such as DPLL but not a possible world). Each model can be thought of as one possible world: a fully specified way things might be, as far as the chosen vocabulary can express.

Because each of the n symbols may independently take either of two values, a vocabulary of n symbols yields exactly 2n distinct models. Three symbols give 8 models, ten symbols give 1024, and sixty-four symbols give over 1019. This exponential blow-up is the reason that model-by-model enumeration, although conceptually perfect, is impractical for large knowledge bases, and it is the reason that syntactic inference procedures exist at all.

A model m satisfies a sentence α — equivalently, α is true in m, or m is a model of α — if α evaluates to true under the assignment m. The standard notation for the set of all models in which α is true is M(α). This set-of-models view is extremely useful because it converts logical relationships into set relationships:

  • α is valid exactly when M(α) is the set of all models.
  • α is unsatisfiable exactly when M(α) is empty.
  • α and β are equivalent exactly when M(α) = M(β).
  • A knowledge base KB entails α (KB ⊨ α) exactly when M(KB) ⊆ M(α) — every world in which the knowledge base holds is a world in which α holds. Adding sentences to a knowledge base shrinks M(KB), which is why more knowledge yields more conclusions.

Models are therefore the bridge between syntax and the world. Strings on a page have no inherent truth; models supply the interpretation that makes "true", "false", "entails", and "sound inference" precise, checkable notions rather than matters of opinion.

Reading the columns individually makes the character of each connective clear. ¬P is true in exactly the rows where P is false — it simply flips the value. P ∧ Q is true in only one of the four rows, the last. P ∨ Q is true in three of the four rows and false only when both disjuncts are false; this single false row is what makes disjunction inclusive. P ⇒ Q is false in exactly one row, the third, where the premise is true and the conclusion false. Comparing the P ⇒ Q column with the ¬P and P ∨ Q columns shows immediately that P ⇒ Q is logically equivalent to ¬P ∨ Q — both have the pattern true, true, false, true. This equivalence is the standard way of eliminating implications when converting sentences to conjunctive normal form. Finally, P ⇔ Q is true in the two rows where P and Q agree (both false, or both true) and false in the two rows where they differ; its negation is exactly exclusive or.

A worked example of translation into these connectives: "If it is raining and I have no umbrella, I get wet" becomes (Rain ∧ ¬Umbrella) ⇒ Wet. Note that in a world with no rain, the sentence is true regardless of Wet — the knowledge base simply makes no commitment about whether I am wet for other reasons. If we wanted to claim that rain-without-umbrella is the only way to get wet, we would strengthen the arrow to a biconditional.

Evaluating complex sentences recursively. Because every well-formed formula has a unique parse tree, and because every connective is defined by a truth table that depends only on the truth values of its arguments, there is a simple recursive procedure that computes the value of any sentence in any model:

  • Base case: if the sentence is a proposition symbol, look up its value in the model. If it is True or False, return that fixed value.
  • Recursive case: evaluate the immediate subsentences first, then apply the truth table of the top connective to the resulting values.

Equivalently: work bottom-up through the parse tree, from the leaves (symbols) outward, until the top-level connective has been evaluated. Consider the sentence

α = ((P ∨ ¬Q) ⇒ (R ∧ P))

and the model   m = { P = true,  Q = true,  R = false }

Parse tree:
            ⇒
          /   \
         ∨     ∧
        / \   / \
       P   ¬ R   P
           |
           Q

Evaluation, leaves outward:
   P        = true          (lookup)
   Q        = true          (lookup)
   R        = false         (lookup)
   ¬Q       = false         (negation of true)
   P ∨ ¬Q   = true          (true ∨ false)
   R ∧ P    = false         (false ∧ true)
   whole    = false         (true ⇒ false  is the one false row of ⇒)

Changing the model changes only the leaf lookups; the structure of the computation is fixed by the parse tree. In the model {P = true, Q = true, R = true} the same sentence evaluates to true ⇒ true = true, and in {P = false, Q = true, R = false} it evaluates to (false ∨ false) ⇒ … = false ⇒ false = true. A short nested example involving the constants: (¬False ∧ (P ∨ False)) reduces to (True ∧ P), and so has the same value as P in every model.

Two properties of this procedure deserve emphasis. First, every well-formed sentence receives exactly one truth value in every model: the recursion always terminates (each step moves to strictly smaller subsentences), it is defined for every case of the grammar, and the parse is unique, so there is no possibility of a sentence being "both true and false" or "neither". The logic is unambiguous and total. Second, the semantics is truth-functional, also called compositional: the value of a compound depends only on the truth values of its parts, never on their internal form, subject matter, or how they were derived. Consequently, if two subsentences happen to have the same truth value in a model, swapping one for the other cannot change the value of the whole in that model. This is precisely the property that licenses substitution of logical equivalents anywhere inside a sentence — the engine behind normal-form conversion and behind most simplification steps performed by reasoning systems. (Natural language is not truth-functional in this way; "Alice believes that P" can change value when P is replaced by an equally true sentence, which is one reason that modelling belief requires richer logics.)

Validity, satisfiability, and logical equivalence. Once truth in a model is defined, sentences can be classified by the shape of M(α).

A valid sentence, or tautology, is true under every possible assignment of truth values. P ∨ ¬P (the law of the excluded middle) is the classic example: whichever value P takes, one disjunct is true. Validity can always be tested by building the full truth table and checking that the final column contains no false. Valid sentences carry no information about the world — they rule out no possible world — but they are enormously useful, because every sound inference rule corresponds to a valid implication. Modus ponens, for instance, corresponds to the validity of (P ∧ (P ⇒ Q)) ⇒ Q.

A satisfiable sentence is true in at least one model; that model is a witness to its satisfiability. Deciding whether an arbitrary propositional sentence is satisfiable is the SAT problem, the first problem shown to be NP-complete (Cook, 1971). Naïve truth-table checking takes time proportional to 2n for n symbols, but modern SAT solvers based on the DPLL backtracking framework, augmented with unit propagation, clause learning, and good branching heuristics, routinely solve industrial instances with hundreds of thousands of variables. This makes SAT a practical target language: planning, circuit verification, scheduling, and diagnosis problems are often solved by encoding them as propositional sentences and calling a solver.

An unsatisfiable sentence, or contradiction, is true in no model at all; P ∧ ¬P is the stock example, and (P ⇒ Q) ∧ P ∧ ¬Q is a slightly less obvious one. The three notions are tightly linked by negation:

  • α is valid if and only if ¬α is unsatisfiable.
  • α is satisfiable if and only if ¬α is not valid.

These equivalences underwrite proof by refutation: to show that a knowledge base entails a query, one shows that KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable. Combined with the deduction theorem — KB ⊨ α if and only if (KB ⇒ α) is valid — they let a single satisfiability-testing engine answer every entailment question that can be posed in the logic.

Logical equivalence is the last of the three properties. Sentences α and β are logically equivalent, written α ≡ β, when they are true in exactly the same set of models: M(α) = M(β). Equivalently (and this is the usual test), α ≡ β precisely when the biconditional α ⇔ β is valid. Note the distinction in level: ⇔ is a connective inside the language, producing a sentence that may be true in some models and false in others, whereas ≡ is a claim about two sentences, asserting that their ⇔ is a tautology. Because the semantics is truth-functional, equivalents may be substituted for one another inside any larger sentence without changing its meaning — the basis of all algebraic manipulation of formulas.

¬(P ⇒ (Q ∧ R))

Propositional logic is the simplest formal language that a knowledge-based agent can use to represent what it believes about the world. Its syntax tells you which strings of symbols count as sentences: atomic propositional symbols such as P or Rain, combined with the connectives for negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, and biconditional, assembled according to a formal grammar (often written in BNF). Its semantics tells you what those sentences mean: a model is simply an assignment of true or false to every propositional symbol, and truth tables define how the truth value of a complex sentence is computed recursively from the truth values of its parts.

This topic matters because everything later in the module depends on it. Entailment, model checking, conversion to conjunctive normal form, and resolution are all defined in terms of the syntax and semantics introduced here, and first-order logic extends this same machinery with predicates, functions, and quantifiers. The key definitions to internalize are validity (true in every model), satisfiability (true in at least one model), and logical equivalence (the same truth value in every model).

While watching, keep two questions separate in your mind: is this string a well-formed sentence (syntax), and in which models is it true (semantics)? Pay particular attention to the truth table for implication, which surprises most students, and to how a truth table enumerates 2^n rows for n propositional symbols — a fact that will matter when we discuss the cost of model checking.

Entailment, Validity, and Satisfiability

▶

Logic gives us a way to say precisely what it means for one piece of knowledge to follow from another. When a knowledge-based agent is told a collection of facts about its world and then asked a question, we need a standard — independent of any program, data structure, or search strategy — that says which answers are correct. That standard is logical entailment. Everything else in this topic (validity, satisfiability, equivalence, soundness, completeness) is either a reformulation of entailment or a way of judging whether a particular algorithm lives up to it. The central idea is deceptively simple: an agent is entitled to conclude α from its knowledge base only if there is no possible way the world could be that makes the knowledge base true and α false.

Models and the semantics of entailment. A model is a mathematical abstraction of a possible world. In propositional logic, a model is nothing more exotic than an assignment of true or false to every proposition symbol in the language. If the language contains the symbols P, Q, and R, then one model is {P = true, Q = false, R = true}; there are 2³ = 8 models in total. The semantics of the logic — the truth tables for the connectives — then fix the truth value of every sentence in the language once the model is given. We say that model m satisfies α, or that m is a model of α, when α is true in m. The notation M(α) denotes the set of all models in which α is true.

Thinking of sentences as the set of worlds they permit is the key move. A sentence is a constraint on how the world might be: asserting it rules out every model in which it is false. The more you assert, the fewer worlds remain. With this picture, entailment has a crisp set-theoretic definition:

KB ⊨ α   if and only if   M(KB) ⊆ M(α)

In words: every world in which the knowledge base is true is also a world in which α is true. The knowledge base has already narrowed the possibilities down so far that α cannot fail. Note the direction of the subset relation, which students often reverse: the stronger sentence (the KB) has the smaller model set and sits on the left.

Consider a tiny concrete example drawn from the Wumpus world. Suppose an agent has visited squares [1,1] and [2,1], felt no breeze in [1,1] but a breeze in [2,1]. Let P12, P22, and P31 mean "there is a pit in [1,2]", "…in [2,2]", "…in [3,1]". The physics of the world says a square is breezy exactly when an adjacent square has a pit, so the agent's knowledge base entails ¬P12 (because [1,1] is breeze-free) and also the disjunction P22 ∨ P31 (because [2,1] is breezy). Enumerating the eight assignments to these three symbols, exactly three survive as models of the KB:

Entailment is a three-way outcome at the level of a query: α entailed, ¬α entailed, or neither (the KB is simply silent).

Two structural consequences of the model-set view are worth drawing out. First, entailment is a relation between sentences, defined purely by the semantics. It exists whether or not anybody ever computes it, and it is not defined in terms of any algorithm, proof system, or inference rule. An algorithm can be evaluated against entailment, but it does not define it. Second, classical logic is monotonic: adding sentences to a KB can only shrink M(KB), because each new sentence is an extra constraint that eliminates models. If M(KB) ⊆ M(α) and M(KB ∧ β) ⊆ M(KB), then M(KB ∧ β) ⊆ M(α), so anything previously entailed stays entailed:

if  KB ⊨ α   then   KB ∧ β ⊨ α      (for any β)

Monotonicity is computationally convenient — a conclusion once derived never has to be retracted as new premises arrive — but it is also a genuine limitation. Everyday "default" reasoning is non-monotonic: learning that Tweety is a bird licenses the conclusion that Tweety flies, but learning additionally that Tweety is a penguin retracts it. Classical propositional and first-order logic cannot behave that way, which is precisely why extensions such as default logic, circumscription, and probabilistic reasoning exist.

Entailment as formal correct reasoning. Saying KB ⊨ α is saying that α is true whenever the KB is true — that no counterexample world exists. A counterexample is simply a model that satisfies the KB but falsifies α; entailment holds exactly when the search for such a model comes up empty. This is why entailment captures necessity rather than plausibility, typicality, or statistical likelihood. If a KB says "99% of swans are white" and "Odette is a swan", it does not entail "Odette is white", because a model exists in which Odette is one of the exceptional black swans. Logical entailment licenses no leaps, however reasonable those leaps may be; grading degrees of belief is the job of probability theory, not of ⊨.

The most direct way to decide entailment is the procedure the table above illustrates: model checking. Enumerate every possible model; for each one, test whether the KB is true; if it is, check that α is also true. If some model satisfies the KB and falsifies α, report "not entailed"; if no such model turns up after exhaustive enumeration, report "entailed". In pseudocode form:

function TT-ENTAILS?(KB, α) returns true or false
    symbols ← a list of the proposition symbols in KB and α
    return TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, symbols, {})

function TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, symbols, model) returns true or false
    if symbols is empty then
        if PL-TRUE?(KB, model) then return PL-TRUE?(α, model)
        else return true                      // vacuously OK: KB is false here
    else
        P ← FIRST(symbols);  rest ← REST(symbols)
        return TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, rest, model ∪ {P = true})
           and TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, rest, model ∪ {P = false})

This is a depth-first enumeration of a complete binary tree of assignments. Its correctness is beyond dispute — it is a literal transcription of the definition of ⊨ — but its cost is brutal. With n proposition symbols there are 2n models, so time is O(2n) even though space is only O(n) for the recursion stack. Ten symbols means 1,024 rows, which is trivial; fifty symbols means over 1015 rows, which is not. A realistic Wumpus world of size 4×4 already needs dozens of symbols once you include pits, breezes, the wumpus, stench, and time-indexed agent locations; industrial circuit-verification or planning problems routinely involve hundreds of thousands. The exponential blow-up is the standing motivation for everything that follows: normal forms, resolution, Horn-clause chaining, and modern SAT solving all exist to avoid enumerating models one by one.

Because algorithms and the entailment relation are distinct things, we use distinct notation. KB ⊨ α is the semantic claim about models. KB ⊢ᵢ α — read "α is derived from KB by procedure i" — is the syntactic claim that running inference procedure i on KB actually produces α. The subscript names the procedure, so one may write KB ⊢RES α for a resolution derivation and KB ⊢FC α for forward chaining. Keeping ⊨ and ⊢ apart is what makes it even possible to ask the questions of soundness and completeness discussed at the end of this topic: are the things the program prints exactly the things that are true in all models?

Validity and the deduction theorem. A sentence is valid if it is true in all models — under every possible assignment of truth values to its proposition symbols. Valid sentences are also called tautologies. Examples include P ∨ ¬P (the law of the excluded middle), P ⇒ P, (P ∧ (P ⇒ Q)) ⇒ Q (modus ponens packaged as a sentence), and True itself. In model-set terms, α is valid exactly when M(α) is the set of all models.

Validity connects to entailment through the deduction theorem, one of the most useful bridges in logic:

KB ⊨ α   if and only if   the sentence (KB ⇒ α) is valid

The proof is a one-line consequence of the truth table for ⇒. The implication KB ⇒ α is false in a model precisely when KB is true and α is false there — that is, precisely at a counterexample to entailment. So "KB ⇒ α has no falsifying model" and "there is no counterexample to KB ⊨ α" say the same thing. Applying this to the Wumpus example, KB ⊨ ¬P12 is the same claim as "(KB ⇒ ¬P12) is a tautology", which you could verify by building an eight-row truth table for that single compound sentence and checking that the final column is all true.

The practical payoff is reduction: a question about the relationship between two objects (a KB and a query) becomes a question about a property of one object (a single sentence). Any machinery that tests one sentence for validity can now answer arbitrary entailment questions, and a KB consisting of many sentences can be folded into one by conjunction, since {α₁, α₂, …, αₙ} ⊨ β iff (α₁ ∧ α₂ ∧ … ∧ αₙ) ⇒ β is valid.

There is a philosophical corollary worth noticing: valid sentences carry no information about the world. Because a valid sentence excludes no model, telling an agent "P ∨ ¬P" does not shrink M(KB) at all and so cannot help it distinguish one state of affairs from another. "Either it is raining or it is not raining" is unimpeachably true and completely useless as a weather report. Information content, in this framework, is exactly the capacity to eliminate possible worlds — which is why tautologies are empty and contradictions, at the other extreme, are catastrophic.

Satisfiability and proof by refutation. A sentence α is satisfiable if it is true in some model, i.e. M(α) ≠ ∅; it is unsatisfiable (contradictory) if no model makes it true, i.e. M(α) = ∅. P ∧ ¬P is the canonical unsatisfiable sentence; (P ∨ Q) ∧ (¬P ∨ R) is satisfiable, as witnessed by {P = false, Q = true, R = anything}. Determining satisfiability of a propositional sentence is the SAT problem, the first problem shown to be NP-complete (Cook, 1971).

The three notions line up neatly along a spectrum of how many models a sentence has:

Validity and satisfiability are duals, linked by negation:

α is valid           iff   ¬α is unsatisfiable
α is satisfiable     iff   ¬α is not valid

Combining this duality with the deduction theorem produces the single most important identity in automated reasoning:

KB ⊨ α   iff   (KB ⇒ α) is valid
         iff   ¬(KB ⇒ α) is unsatisfiable
         iff   (KB ∧ ¬α) is unsatisfiable

This is the basis of proof by refutation (equivalently, reductio ad absurdum): to show that α follows, add the negation of α to the knowledge base and show that the enlarged set is contradictory. If assuming "not α" alongside everything you know leads to an impossibility, then α could not have been false, given the KB. Back in the Wumpus example, to prove KB ⊨ ¬P12 we would assert KB ∧ ¬¬P12, i.e. KB ∧ P12, and derive a contradiction from the breeze-free report at [1,1] together with the rule B11 ⇔ (P12 ∨ P21).

Refutation is attractive for several reasons. It converts an open-ended "is this true in all models?" question into a closed, goal-directed search for an inconsistency; it fits naturally with the resolution rule, which is refutation-complete for propositional logic (it can always derive the empty clause from an unsatisfiable set of clauses, though it is not designed to enumerate all entailed sentences directly); and it hands the whole problem to a SAT solver. Although SAT is NP-complete in the worst case, modern SAT solvers — conflict-driven clause learning, unit propagation with watched literals, activity-based variable ordering, restarts — routinely settle real-world instances with millions of variables and clauses. The practical consequence is that reducing an entailment question to an unsatisfiability question is not an academic manoeuvre but the standard engineering path, used in hardware verification, software model checking, planning as satisfiability, and configuration.

Logical equivalence. Two sentences α and β are logically equivalent, written α ≡ β, if they are true in exactly the same set of models: M(α) = M(β). Equivalently — and this is the definition worth memorising because it is stated purely in terms of entailment —

α ≡ β   if and only if   α ⊨ β  and  β ⊨ α

Mutual entailment forces mutual subset inclusion of the model sets, hence equality. This is why equivalence is strictly stronger than implication in one direction: (P ∧ Q) ⊨ P, but P ⊭ (P ∧ Q), so the two are not equivalent — the model {P = true, Q = false} separates them. Equivalence preserves meaning exactly; it is a licence to substitute one sentence for another anywhere, in any context, without changing what the knowledge base says about the world. One-directional implication offers no such licence.

Note also the related distinction: α ≡ β is a statement about sentences (metalanguage), whereas α ⇔ β is itself a sentence (object language). They are connected in the obvious way: α ≡ β holds exactly when α ⇔ β is valid.

Because each rewrite preserves the model set, a chain of rewrites preserves it too. That is exactly what justifies conversion to conjunctive normal form (CNF) — a conjunction of clauses, each clause a disjunction of literals — as a legitimate preprocessing step before resolution. The output sentence is not merely "close enough" to the input; it has identical models, so any entailment question answered about the CNF form is answered about the original. A worked conversion of the Wumpus biconditional:

B11 ⇔ (P12 ∨ P21)

  2. eliminate ⇒ :   (¬B11 ∨ P12 ∨ P21) ∧ (¬(P12 ∨ P21) ∨ B11)
  3. De Morgan   :   (¬B11 ∨ P12 ∨ P21) ∧ ((¬P12 ∧ ¬P21) ∨ B11)

                     (¬B11 ∨ P12 ∨ P21) ∧ (¬P12 ∨ B11) ∧ (¬P21 ∨ B11)

The result is three clauses, ready for resolution, and logically indistinguishable from the sentence we started with. Note that double-negation elimination is quietly doing work throughout: when a refutation proof adds ¬α and α itself contains a negation — as with ¬¬P12 above — the rule ¬(¬α) ≡ α is what restores a well-formed literal. The same rule tidies up after De Morgan's laws push negations through nested expressions. Associativity and commutativity, meanwhile, are what allow us to stop writing parentheses inside a clause and to treat a clause as an unordered set of literals, which is how resolution implementations actually store them. One caution: distributing ∨ over ∧ can in the worst case cause an exponential blow-up in the number of clauses, which is why industrial tools often use the Tseitin transformation — introducing fresh symbols to name subformulas — producing a sentence that is merely equisatisfiable rather than strictly equivalent, but only linearly larger.

Soundness and completeness of inference. With ⊨ fixed as the standard of correctness and ⊢ᵢ denoting what a particular algorithm actually produces, we can finally grade algorithms. Two properties matter.

  • Soundness (also called truth-preservation): an inference procedure i is sound if whenever KB ⊢ᵢ α, it is also the case that KB ⊨ α. Everything the algorithm derives really is entailed. Soundness means the set of derivable sentences is a subset of the set of entailed sentences.
  • Completeness: procedure i is complete if whenever KB ⊨ α, it is also the case that KB ⊢ᵢ α. Everything that is entailed can be derived. Completeness means the derivable set is a superset of the entailed set.

An algorithm that is both sound and complete derives exactly the entailed sentences — no more, no less — and can therefore be trusted as a faithful mechanisation of the entailment relation.

PropertyFormal statementWhat its failure costs you
Soundif KB ⊢ᵢ α then KB ⊨ αAn unsound procedure announces conclusions that do not follow — it invents facts. An agent might march into a square it has "proved" safe and be eaten.
Completeif KB ⊨ α then KB ⊢ᵢ αAn incomplete procedure may fail to find an answer that is genuinely there — it misses valid conclusions. The agent gets stuck, or falls back on guessing, even though the information was already in its KB.

Unsoundness is normally the worse defect, because its errors are silent and positively misleading, whereas incompleteness at least fails honestly. Still, both matter. A trivially sound procedure is one that derives nothing at all; a trivially complete procedure is one that derives every sentence in the language. Neither is useful, which is why the two criteria must be assessed together.

Model checking is both sound and complete for propositional logic. This follows immediately from its construction: TT-ENTAILS? returns true exactly when no model satisfies the KB while falsifying α, which is verbatim the definition of ⊨. Because the number of propositional models is finite (2n), the enumeration always terminates with a definite yes or no — propositional entailment is decidable. The price, as noted, is the exponential blow-up, so soundness and completeness are necessary but not sufficient virtues: an algorithm must also finish in a usable amount of time. This is the trade-off landscape in which other methods are placed. Resolution with a complete search strategy is sound and refutation-complete for propositional logic, but can generate enormous numbers of clauses. Forward and backward chaining over definite clauses are sound and complete for that restricted class of sentences and run in time linear in the size of the KB — a deliberate exchange of expressive power for tractability. In first-order logic the situation shifts again: resolution is sound and complete (Gödel's completeness theorem, realised procedurally), but entailment is only semi-decidable, so a procedure may run forever on a non-entailed query without ever reporting "no".

Finally, soundness and completeness are properties of the reasoning, not guarantees about reality. They certify that the algorithm correctly tracks the entailment relation among sentences; they say nothing about whether those sentences describe the agent's actual environment. Grounding is the extra requirement that the KB's sentences themselves be true of the world. If the agent's percepts are reliable and its model of the world's physics is accurate — if "there is a breeze in [2,1]" really was perceived and "breezy iff adjacent pit" really is how the cave works — then sound inference carries that truth forward, and every derived sentence is also true of the world. Sentences enter the KB either from perception (the sensor-to-sentence link, which can be faulty) or from general rules supplied by the designer or learned from experience (which may be only approximately correct, like "flames mean fire"). Garbage in, impeccably derived garbage out: a perfectly sound and complete reasoner on a false knowledge base yields confident falsehoods. The logical apparatus guarantees the validity of the step from premises to conclusion; responsibility for the truth of the premises lies elsewhere.

Logic gives us a precise way to say what an agent knows and what follows from that knowledge. The central idea is entailment: a knowledge base KB entails a sentence alpha (written KB |= alpha) when alpha is true in every model — every assignment of truth values to the symbols — in which KB is true. Entailment is a relation about meaning, not about symbol manipulation, and it is the standard against which we judge any inference procedure.

Propositional Inference: Model Checking and Resolution

▶

Logical inference is the process of deriving new sentences from old ones in a way that is guaranteed to preserve truth. A knowledge-based agent stores what it knows in a knowledge base (KB) of sentences and must be able to answer questions of the form "given everything I know, does it follow that α is true?" This topic develops the machinery that answers that question for propositional logic: the semantic notion of entailment, the brute-force method of model checking, the refinements that make satisfiability search practical, the normal form that makes proof mechanical, the resolution rule together with proof by refutation, and finally the restricted but highly efficient world of Horn clauses with forward and backward chaining.

Entailment and the inference problem. Entailment is a relationship between sentences that rests entirely on semantics, not on any particular algorithm. We write KB ⊨ α and read it as "KB entails α". The definition is: KB ⊨ α if and only if, in every model in which KB is true, α is also true. A model in propositional logic is simply a complete assignment of true or false to every proposition symbol under consideration. If M(KB) denotes the set of models satisfying the KB and M(α) the set satisfying the query, then entailment is set inclusion: KB ⊨ α exactly when M(KB) ⊆ M(α). The more a KB says, the fewer models it admits, and the more sentences it entails.

A worked illustration helps. Suppose the KB contains the two sentences P ∨ Q and ¬Q. With two symbols there are four models. The KB is true only in the single model {P = true, Q = false}. The sentence P is true in that model, so KB ⊨ P. The sentence Q is false there, so KB ⊭ Q; indeed KB ⊨ ¬Q trivially since ¬Q is already in the KB. Note carefully that KB ⊭ Q is not the same as KB ⊨ ¬Q in general — a KB may simply be silent about a symbol, entailing neither it nor its negation.

Against this semantic yardstick we measure algorithms. An inference procedure is written KB ⊢ᵢ α, meaning "procedure i derives α from KB".

  • Soundness (also called truth-preservation): whenever KB ⊢ᵢ α, it is also the case that KB ⊨ α. A sound procedure never invents conclusions that are not genuinely supported. An unsound procedure is worse than useless, because an agent acting on a fabricated conclusion may take catastrophic actions.
  • Completeness: whenever KB ⊨ α, the procedure can derive it, KB ⊢ᵢ α. A complete procedure never misses a consequence. Incompleteness is tolerable in practice (many fast reasoners are deliberately incomplete), but it means a "no" answer must be read as "I could not show it", not "it is false".

Both properties are relative to a semantics, and both are needed for an inference engine to be a faithful implementation of entailment. Truth-table enumeration and resolution are each sound and complete for propositional logic; forward and backward chaining are sound and complete for the Horn fragment only.

Decidability and complexity. Because a propositional KB mentions only finitely many symbols, there are only finitely many models, so entailment is decidable: an algorithm always terminates with a correct yes/no answer. That is a stronger guarantee than first-order logic offers, where entailment is only semi-decidable. Decidability, however, says nothing about cost. Deciding whether KB ⊨ α is co-NP-complete in general. The reason is the duality with satisfiability: KB ⊨ α holds exactly when KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable, and SAT is the canonical NP-complete problem, so its complement — proving unsatisfiability — sits in co-NP. A "no" answer to entailment has a short certificate (exhibit one model satisfying KB ∧ ¬α), but a "yes" answer in general does not; one must in effect rule out all models. This asymmetry explains why practical systems invest so heavily in search heuristics and proof techniques rather than hoping for a polynomial algorithm.

The deduction theorem. The bridge between entailment and the familiar notion of a valid (tautological) sentence is the deduction theorem: for any sentences KB and α, KB ⊨ α if and only if the implication (KB ⇒ α) is valid, i.e. true in every model. (Here KB is understood as the conjunction of all its sentences.) The proof is immediate from the semantics of ⇒: the implication can only fail in a model where KB is true and α false, and such a model exists precisely when entailment fails. A second, equally useful equivalence is proof by refutation (reductio ad absurdum): KB ⊨ α if and only if (KB ∧ ¬α) is unsatisfiable. These three formulations — entailment, validity of an implication, unsatisfiability of a conjunction — are interchangeable, and each suggests a different algorithm: enumerate models, test a tautology, or run a satisfiability solver.

Model checking by truth-table enumeration. The most direct algorithm simply implements the definition of entailment. Collect every proposition symbol occurring in KB or α; call them P₁ … Pₙ. Enumerate all 2ⁿ assignments of truth values to these symbols. For each assignment, evaluate the KB. If the KB evaluates to false, the model is irrelevant and is skipped. If the KB evaluates to true, evaluate the query: if the query is also true, continue; if the query is false, stop and report that entailment fails. If the enumeration completes without finding such a model, report that KB ⊨ α.

function TT-ENTAILS?(KB, α) returns true or false
    symbols ← a list of the proposition symbols in KB and α
    return TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, symbols, {})

function TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, symbols, model) returns true or false
    if EMPTY?(symbols) then
        if PL-TRUE?(KB, model) then return PL-TRUE?(α, model)
        else return true          // KB false here: model places no constraint
    else
        P ← FIRST(symbols);  rest ← REST(symbols)
        return TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, rest, model ∪ {P = true})
               and
               TT-CHECK-ALL(KB, α, rest, model ∪ {P = false})

Truth-table enumeration is sound and complete by construction, because it is a literal transcription of the semantic definition of entailment; there is no gap between what it derives and what is entailed. Its cost, however, is brutal. The time complexity is O(2ⁿ) for n symbols (strictly, O(2ⁿ · m) if evaluating the sentences costs m). The space complexity is only O(n), because the recursive formulation never materialises the whole table — it performs a depth-first traversal of a binary tree of partial models, holding just one root-to-leaf path at a time. Even so, a modest KB with 60 symbols yields roughly 1018 models, which is beyond any machine. Enumeration is therefore a definitional tool and a teaching device rather than a production algorithm; it is, however, an excellent way to verify small examples and to confirm the correctness of logical equivalences.

Satisfiability and DPLL-style search. Because entailment reduces to unsatisfiability of KB ∧ ¬α, any good satisfiability (SAT) solver is also a good entailment checker. The Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) algorithm is a backtracking search over partial models that takes the same recursive enumeration skeleton as above and adds three devastatingly effective improvements. Its input is a sentence in CNF, viewed as a set of clauses.

  • Early termination. A partial model often settles the outcome without assigning every symbol. A clause is already satisfied the moment any one of its literals is true, so the clause (A ∨ B) is satisfied by A = true regardless of B. If every clause is satisfied, the search can stop immediately and report satisfiability. Conversely, if any clause has all of its literals assigned false, that branch is dead and must be abandoned at once rather than extended through the remaining symbols. Truth-table enumeration, by contrast, stubbornly completes every assignment.
  • Unit propagation (the unit clause heuristic). A unit clause is one with exactly one literal still unassigned and all other literals already false. That literal is forced: there is no choice but to make it true. Assigning it may in turn reduce other clauses to unit clauses, producing a cascade that fixes many symbols with no search at all. For example, with clauses (¬A ∨ B), (¬B ∨ C), (¬C ∨ D) and the unit clause (A): asserting A makes the first clause a unit on B, forcing B; that forces C; that forces D. Unit propagation applied to a KB of definite clauses is exactly forward chaining. In modern solvers it typically consumes the majority of running time and is the single most important optimisation.
  • The pure symbol heuristic. A symbol is pure if, across all clauses not yet satisfied, it appears with only one polarity — always positive or always negative. Such a symbol can safely be assigned the value that makes its literals true, since doing so can only satisfy clauses and never falsify one; no backtracking over this choice is ever required. In the clause set (A ∨ ¬B), (¬B ∨ ¬C), (C ∨ A), the symbol A occurs only positively and B only negatively, so we may set A = true and B = false outright. Purity is relative to the remaining clauses: once early termination marks clauses as satisfied, further symbols may become pure.

Industrial solvers layer further techniques on this core: intelligent backtracking that jumps back to the decision actually responsible for a conflict, clause learning that records the cause of each conflict as a new clause so the same dead end is never revisited, activity-based branching heuristics such as VSIDS, random restarts, and lazy "watched literal" data structures that make unit propagation nearly free. The cumulative effect is startling: DPLL-derived solvers routinely dispatch industrial instances with millions of variables and tens of millions of clauses, despite the worst-case exponential bound that complexity theory guarantees must remain. This is why hardware verification, software model checking, and planning are now commonly compiled into SAT.

Local search and WALKSAT. An entirely different strategy abandons systematic search. Local search starts from a complete random assignment and repeatedly flips the value of a single symbol, guided by the number of clauses satisfied. WALKSAT picks a clause that is currently unsatisfied and then flips one of its symbols, choosing with some probability p a random symbol in the clause (a "random walk" move that escapes local optima) and otherwise the symbol that maximises the number of satisfied clauses (a greedy "min-conflicts" move). The balance between the two keeps the search from stagnating on plateaux.

function WALKSAT(clauses, p, max_flips) returns a model or failure
    model ← a random assignment of true/false to the symbols in clauses
    for i = 1 to max_flips do
        if model satisfies clauses then return model
        clause ← a randomly selected clause from clauses that is false in model
        with probability p
            flip the value in model of a randomly selected symbol from clause
        else
            flip whichever symbol in clause maximises the number of satisfied clauses
    return failure

WALKSAT is often dramatically faster than DPLL on large satisfiable instances, especially ones with many solutions. Its crucial limitation is that it is incomplete: returning failure after max_flips flips means only "no model found within the budget", never "no model exists". Since entailment requires demonstrating that KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable, local search cannot establish entailment. It is, however, perfectly suited to the opposite task: finding a counterexample that refutes a conjectured entailment, and to applications such as planning where any satisfying assignment is the answer sought.

Conjunctive normal form. Both DPLL and resolution require their input in a uniform syntactic shape. A literal is a proposition symbol or its negation; a clause is a disjunction of literals; a sentence is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) if it is a conjunction of clauses. Every propositional sentence has a logically equivalent CNF, obtained by a deterministic four-stage rewriting procedure.

  • Step 1 — eliminate biconditionals. Replace every α ⇔ β with (α ⇒ β) ∧ (β ⇒ α). This is the step most likely to be forgotten, and it doubles the size of the affected subformula.
  • Step 2 — eliminate implications. Replace every α ⇒ β with ¬α ∨ β. After this step the only connectives left are ¬, ∧, and ∨.
  • Step 3 — move negations inward (negation normal form). Apply De Morgan's laws, ¬(α ∧ β) ≡ (¬α ∨ ¬β) and ¬(α ∨ β) ≡ (¬α ∧ ¬β), together with double-negation elimination ¬¬α ≡ α, until every negation sign sits directly on a proposition symbol.
  • Step 4 — distribute ∨ over ∧. Apply α ∨ (β ∧ γ) ≡ (α ∨ β) ∧ (α ∨ γ) repeatedly until the sentence is a flat conjunction of disjunctions. Finally, flatten nested associative operators and optionally simplify by dropping duplicate literals and deleting tautological clauses that contain both P and ¬P.

A full worked conversion of the wumpus-world style sentence B₁,₁ ⇔ (P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁):

1. Eliminate ⇔ :
   (B₁,₁ ⇒ (P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁)) ∧ ((P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ⇒ B₁,₁)

2. Eliminate ⇒ :
   (¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ (¬(P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∨ B₁,₁)

3. Move ¬ inward (De Morgan):
   (¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ ((¬P₁,₂ ∧ ¬P₂,₁) ∨ B₁,₁)

4. Distribute ∨ over ∧ :
   (¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ (¬P₁,₂ ∨ B₁,₁) ∧ (¬P₂,₁ ∨ B₁,₁)

The result is three clauses, and it is logically equivalent to the original — equivalent, not merely equisatisfiable, because every rewrite rule used is an equivalence. That equivalence guarantee has a price. Step 4 can cause exponential blow-up: the sentence (A₁ ∧ B₁) ∨ (A₂ ∧ B₂) ∨ … ∨ (Aₙ ∧ Bₙ) distributes into 2ⁿ clauses, one for each way of choosing a literal from each disjunct. In practice this is avoided by the Tseitin transformation, which introduces a fresh symbol to name each compound subformula and adds clauses defining it; the result is linear in size and equisatisfiable (though not equivalent, since it mentions new symbols). For entailment checking, equisatisfiability is all that is needed, so Tseitin encoding is the standard industrial practice.

The resolution rule. Resolution is a single inference rule that, with CNF and refutation, is complete for propositional logic. In its general form, the full resolution rule takes two clauses containing complementary literals and produces the disjunction of all the remaining literals:

    ℓ₁ ∨ … ∨ ℓᵢ ∨ … ∨ ℓₖ ,     m₁ ∨ … ∨ mⱼ ∨ … ∨ mₙ
   ---------------------------------------------------------   where ℓᵢ and mⱼ are complementary
    ℓ₁ ∨ … ℓᵢ₋₁ ∨ ℓᵢ₊₁ … ∨ ℓₖ ∨ m₁ ∨ … mⱼ₋₁ ∨ mⱼ₊₁ … ∨ mₙ

The canonical example: from (A ∨ B) and (¬B ∨ C) infer (A ∨ C). The rule is sound, and the reason is easy to see by case analysis on B. If B is false, then for the first clause to hold A must be true. If B is true, then for the second clause to hold C must be true. Either way A ∨ C holds. Note that resolution must be applied to exactly one complementary pair at a time. If two clauses share two complementary pairs — for instance (A ∨ B) and (¬A ∨ ¬B) — then resolving on A gives (B ∨ ¬B) and resolving on B gives (A ∨ ¬A); both resolvents are tautologies and carry no information, which is correct, since those two clauses are jointly satisfiable and entail nothing new. Resolving on both pairs at once would illegitimately produce the empty clause, so the rule is deliberately restricted.

Factoring. A resolvent is a set of literals, not a list, so duplicate literals must be collapsed. This step is called factoring. Resolving (A ∨ B) with (A ∨ ¬B) yields the raw resolvent (A ∨ A), which factoring reduces to the single-literal clause (A). Keeping clauses in this canonical form matters for two reasons: it keeps clauses from growing without bound through repeated duplication, and it allows the algorithm to recognise when a newly generated clause is identical to one already present, which is what makes termination detection possible.

Proof by refutation. Resolution alone cannot derive every entailed sentence — from (A) it will never produce the entailed sentence (A ∨ B), because resolution only ever shortens or merges clauses. What resolution is guaranteed to do is detect unsatisfiability, a property known as refutation completeness: if a set of clauses is unsatisfiable, resolution applied exhaustively will derive the empty clause. Combined with the refutation equivalence established earlier, this gives a complete decision procedure for entailment. The algorithm is:

  • Form the sentence KB ∧ ¬α, i.e. add the negation of the query to the knowledge base.
  • Convert the whole thing to CNF and collect the clauses into a set.
  • Repeatedly select pairs of clauses containing complementary literals, compute the resolvent, factor it, and add it to the set if it is new.
  • If the empty clause { } (written □) is ever derived, stop and report KB ⊨ α. The empty clause is a disjunction of no literals, which is false in every model; deriving it from KB ∧ ¬α by sound rules proves that KB ∧ ¬α has no model, i.e. that it is unsatisfiable, i.e. that KB ⊨ α.
  • If the process saturates — no pair of clauses yields a resolvent that is not already present — stop and report that KB ⊭ α, because KB ∧ ¬α is satisfiable. Termination is guaranteed: with n symbols there are only finitely many distinct clauses (at most 3ⁿ, since each symbol appears positively, negatively, or not at all), so the clause set cannot grow forever.
function PL-RESOLUTION(KB, α) returns true or false
    clauses ← the set of clauses in the CNF representation of (KB ∧ ¬α)
    new ← { }
    loop do
        for each pair of clauses Cᵢ, Cⱼ in clauses do
            resolvents ← PL-RESOLVE(Cᵢ, Cⱼ)          // factored
            if resolvents contains the empty clause then return true
            new ← new ∪ resolvents
        if new ⊆ clauses then return false            // saturation
        clauses ← clauses ∪ new

The empty clause appears, so KB ⊨ R. Now contrast a non-entailment. Take KB = (P ∨ Q) and query α = P. The clause set is (P ∨ Q) and (¬P). The only available resolution is on P, giving (Q). Resolving (Q) against the others produces nothing new — there is no clause containing ¬Q. The set {(P ∨ Q), (¬P), (Q)} is saturated and contains no empty clause, so KB ⊭ P; and indeed the model {P = false, Q = true} satisfies the KB while falsifying the query.

The practical difficulty with naive resolution is the combinatorial explosion of generated clauses; real theorem provers therefore impose strategies such as unit preference (prefer resolving with short clauses, since the goal is the shortest clause of all), the set-of-support strategy (require every resolution step to involve a descendant of the negated query, keeping the proof goal-directed), and subsumption (discard any clause that is a superset of an existing clause, since it is weaker and therefore redundant).

Horn clauses and definite clauses. The expense of full resolution motivates a restricted language in which inference is cheap. A definite clause is a disjunction of literals of which exactly one is positive; a Horn clause is a disjunction with at most one positive literal. Horn clauses therefore comprise definite clauses together with goal clauses (no positive literal) and facts (a single positive literal, a definite clause with an empty premise). The crucial observation is that a definite clause can be rewritten as an implication whose premise is a conjunction of positive literals and whose conclusion is a single positive literal:

(¬L₁ ∨ ¬L₂ ∨ … ∨ ¬Lₖ ∨ H)   ≡   (L₁ ∧ L₂ ∧ … ∧ Lₖ) ⇒ H

This form is enormously natural for knowledge engineering — it reads as a rule, "if all of these conditions hold, then conclude this" — and it is the basis of logic programming (Prolog), production-rule systems, and deductive databases. For example:

Facts:  Mythical? — no;  we know:  Mammal
Rules:  Mammal ⇒ WarmBlooded
        WarmBlooded ∧ HasFeathers ⇒ Bird
        Mammal ∧ GivesMilk ⇒ Nurtures

Three properties make the Horn fragment attractive. First, entailment of an atom from a Horn KB can be decided in time linear in the size of the KB, a dramatic improvement on the co-NP-completeness of the general case. Second, inference can be carried out with forward or backward chaining, which produce proofs that humans can follow as chains of rule applications. Third, unit propagation in DPLL is precisely forward chaining, so the same machinery serves both worlds. The cost of these benefits is expressiveness: a Horn KB cannot state genuine disjunctions such as P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁ or negative conclusions, so the wumpus world's knowledge cannot be fully captured in Horn form.

Forward chaining. Forward chaining is data-driven: it begins with the atoms known to be true and repeatedly fires any rule all of whose premises are already known, adding the conclusion to the set of known facts. It stops when the query atom is derived, or when a complete pass adds no new fact (a fixed point has been reached), in which case the query is not entailed.

function PL-FC-ENTAILS?(KB, q) returns true or false
    count ← a table where count[c] is the number of symbols in clause c's premise
    inferred ← a table where inferred[s] is initially false for all symbols
    agenda ← a queue of symbols known to be true in KB (the facts)
    while agenda is not empty do
        p ← POP(agenda)
        if p = q then return true
        if inferred[p] = false then
            inferred[p] ← true
            for each clause c in KB where p appears in c's premise do
                decrement count[c]
                if count[c] = 0 then add c's conclusion to agenda
    return false

The count table is what delivers linear time. Instead of rescanning every rule on every iteration to see whether its premises are now satisfied, the algorithm stores, for each rule, how many premises remain unsatisfied. When a symbol becomes known, only the rules mentioning that symbol in their premise are touched, and each is decremented by one. A rule fires exactly when its counter hits zero. Over the whole run, each premise occurrence in the KB is processed exactly once, so the total work is proportional to the number of symbol occurrences in the KB — linear in its size.

Forward chaining is sound, because every fact it adds is produced by one application of Modus Ponens, which is a sound rule. It is complete for definite-clause KBs, and the argument is instructive: when the algorithm reaches its fixed point, take the set of inferred atoms and build the model that assigns true to each of them and false to every other symbol. This model satisfies every clause of the KB — if some clause L₁ ∧ … ∧ Lₖ ⇒ H were false in it, all the Lᵢ would be inferred and H would not be, contradicting the fact that the algorithm had reached a fixed point. Hence this is a model of the KB, so any atom entailed by the KB must be true in it, so any entailed atom is one of the inferred atoms. This model is in fact the unique minimal model of the KB, the one that makes as few atoms true as possible.

Backward chaining. Backward chaining is goal-driven. It starts from the query q and works backwards. If q is already a known fact, it succeeds. Otherwise it finds every rule whose conclusion is q and, for each, recursively attempts to prove all of that rule's premises; if every premise of some rule can be proved, q is proved. The search is depth-first and must keep track of the goals currently on the stack so that it does not loop forever on cyclic rule sets (for instance P ⇒ Q together with Q ⇒ P). This is the inference strategy used by Prolog.

Running backward chaining on the same KB with query Q: the goal Q matches the conclusion of r1, so the subgoal is P; P is the conclusion of r2, giving subgoals L and M; L is the conclusion of r4 (subgoals A and P — but P is already on the stack, so this branch is abandoned to avoid a loop) and of r5 (subgoals A and B, both facts — success); M is the conclusion of r3, with subgoals B (a fact) and L (already proved). All subgoals succeed, so Q is proved. Like forward chaining, backward chaining is sound and complete for definite-clause knowledge bases.

The practical rule of thumb: if the agent continuously receives new percepts and should maintain everything derivable from them — a sensor-monitoring or alarm application — forward chaining is appropriate, since the incremental cost of each new fact is small and all consequences are kept up to date. If the agent has a large KB but a specific question, backward chaining is usually far cheaper, because the cost is proportional to the size of the proof tree rather than the size of the KB. A medical KB with a hundred thousand rules asked "does this patient have condition X?" should certainly not derive every consequence of the patient's record; it should chain backwards from X. Many real systems combine both, using forward chaining to propagate cheap, highly constraining facts and backward chaining to resolve the remaining questions on demand.

Once a knowledge base is written in propositional logic, the central question becomes: does what we already know force some new sentence to be true? That is the entailment question, and this topic covers the main algorithmic answers to it. Model checking attacks entailment semantically, by enumerating truth assignments (the truth table) or by searching more cleverly, as in DPLL-style backtracking with unit propagation and pure-literal reasoning. First-Order Logic: Objects, Relations, and Quantifiers

▶

Propositional logic gives us a clean, well-understood framework: a vocabulary of atomic symbols, five connectives, a truth-functional semantics, and complete inference procedures such as resolution refutation. What it does not give us is a way to talk about things. A propositional symbol like KingJohnIsGreedy is atomic — the logic cannot see inside it to notice that it concerns a particular individual (John) and a particular property (greed), and therefore it cannot relate that symbol to KingRichardIsGreedy in any systematic way. First-order logic (FOL), sometimes called first-order predicate calculus, repairs this by committing to a richer ontology: the world is assumed to contain objects, relations that hold among those objects, and functions that map objects to other objects. This single change in what the logic "sees" produces an enormous gain in expressive power while leaving the familiar machinery of connectives, models, and entailment intact.

Why first-order logic extends propositional logic

The central weakness of propositional logic is that it has no way to state a general rule about a class of objects. Consider the classic statement "All humans are mortal." In propositional logic, the only way to capture its content is to enumerate instances:

HumanSocrates ⇒ MortalSocrates
HumanPlato   ⇒ MortalPlato
HumanAristotle ⇒ MortalAristotle
...

Every human in the domain needs its own sentence. If the domain has ten thousand humans, the knowledge base needs ten thousand sentences; if a new human appears, the knowledge base must be extended by hand; and if the domain is infinite (say, the natural numbers), no finite propositional knowledge base can express the rule at all. In first-order logic the same content is one sentence:

∀x Human(x) ⇒ Mortal(x)

This sentence quantifies over the domain: it asserts something about every object at once, without naming any of them. The saving is not merely one of typing. It means the knowledge base states the rule once and the inference procedure instantiates it on demand for whichever objects turn out to be relevant.

The three ontological commitments of FOL map directly onto three kinds of symbol:

  • Objects — people, numbers, times, wars, colours, countries, legs, centuries. Anything with an individual identity can be an object. Objects are denoted by constant symbols, variables, and function terms.
  • Relations — properties and relationships that may hold among objects: is a king, is the brother of, is between. Relations are denoted by predicate symbols and evaluate to true or false.
  • Functions — mappings in which each object (or tuple of objects) is associated with exactly one other object: the father of, the left leg of, the successor of. Functions are denoted by function symbols and evaluate to objects, not to truth values.

Crucially, FOL retains everything propositional logic had. The connectives ¬, ∧, ∨, ⇒ and ⇔ behave exactly as before, with the same truth tables; the notions of model, entailment, validity and satisfiability are generalised rather than replaced. What is added is the layer of terms (expressions that denote objects) and quantifiers (operators that range over the domain).

The last row is the price of admission. Because a first-order knowledge base may have infinitely many models over infinitely large domains, model checking by enumeration is no longer an option, and no algorithm can in general decide non-entailment. Church and Turing showed that first-order validity is undecidable; what remains is semi-decidability (a property sometimes called refutation completeness): if KB ⊨ α, a complete procedure such as first-order resolution will eventually find a proof, but if KB ⊭ α the procedure may never halt. Practical systems therefore restrict the language — for example to definite clauses, where forward and backward chaining are well behaved — or impose depth and time limits.

Syntax: constants, variables, predicates, and functions

A first-order vocabulary (or signature) consists of three classes of non-logical symbol, plus the logical apparatus shared by all first-order languages.

  • Constant symbols name particular objects: Richard, John, Crown, 2, UK. By convention they are capitalised. A constant always denotes exactly one object, though two different constants may denote the same object unless we say otherwise.
  • Variable symbols — conventionally lower case: x, y, z, p — do not name any fixed object. They are placeholders that range over the domain and acquire meaning only when bound by a quantifier.
  • Predicate symbols denote relations: King(x) (unary, so really a property), Brother(x, y) (binary), Between(x, y, z) (ternary). Each predicate has a fixed arity. A predicate applied to the right number of terms yields something that is true or false.
  • Function symbols denote functions: FatherOf(x), LeftLeg(x), Plus(x, y), Capital(c). A function symbol applied to terms yields another term — it denotes an object, and so it is not truth-valued. Writing LeftLeg(John) ∧ King(John) is a syntax error, because LeftLeg(John) is a leg, not a truth value.

Functions are the right choice whenever the relationship is total and single-valued: every person has exactly one biological father, so FatherOf can be a function; a person may have zero, one or many brothers, so Brother must be a predicate. One can always convert a function into a predicate with an extra argument (Father(x, y) meaning "y is the father of x") at the cost of needing extra axioms to say that such a y always exists and is unique.

These pieces compose according to a simple recursive grammar. A term is an expression that denotes an object:

Term → Constant | Variable | Function(Term, ..., Term)

So John, x, LeftLeg(John) and FatherOf(FatherOf(John)) are all terms; the last shows that function application nests arbitrarily deeply, which is how FOL can refer to infinitely many objects using a finite vocabulary. An atomic sentence (or atom) is a predicate symbol applied to the correct number of terms, or an equality between two terms:

AtomicSentence → Predicate(Term, ..., Term) | Term = Term

Examples: King(John), Brother(John, Richard), Married(FatherOf(Richard), MotherOf(John)), FatherOf(John) = Henry. An atomic sentence is true in a model exactly when the relation named by the predicate holds among the objects named by the terms.

¬Brother(LeftLeg(Richard), John)

The universal quantifier (∀)

The sentence ∀x P(x) — read "for all x, P of x" — is true in a model precisely when P(x) comes out true under every possible substitution of a domain object for the variable x. Conceptually, if the domain were the finite set {Richard, John, Crown, LeftLeg(Richard)}, then

∀x King(x) ⇒ Person(x)

would be equivalent to the conjunction of its four instances:

(King(Richard) ⇒ Person(Richard))
∧ (King(John) ⇒ Person(John))
∧ (King(Crown) ⇒ Person(Crown))
∧ (King(LeftLeg(Richard)) ⇒ Person(LeftLeg(Richard)))

This "conjunction over the domain" reading is the best intuition pump for ∀, and it immediately explains the single most important stylistic rule in first-order logic: ∀ goes with ⇒. The quantifier ranges over everything in the domain, including crowns and legs, so a universal sentence needs an antecedent to filter down to the objects we actually mean. In the example above, the instances about the crown and the leg are vacuously true because King(Crown) is false, and an implication with a false antecedent is true. The implication acts as a guard.

Replacing ⇒ with ∧ is the classic beginner's error:

∀x King(x) ∧ Person(x)        ← WRONG

Expanded over the same domain, this says (King(Richard) ∧ Person(Richard)) ∧ (King(John) ∧ Person(John)) ∧ (King(Crown) ∧ Person(Crown)) ∧ ... — that is, everything in the universe is a king and a person, including the crown and Richard's left leg. It is a far stronger claim than intended and will be false in essentially every realistic model, poisoning the knowledge base.

Universals nest freely. To say that the sibling relation is symmetric:

∀x ∀y Sibling(x, y) ⇒ Sibling(y, x)

and transitivity of ancestry, with three nested universals:

∀x ∀y ∀z Ancestor(x, y) ∧ Ancestor(y, z) ⇒ Ancestor(x, z)

A single quantifier symbol with a list of variables, as in ∀x, y, z, is a common abbreviation for a run of quantifiers of the same type. Note also that the guard need not be a single predicate: ∀x Person(x) ∧ Adult(x) ⇒ CanVote(x) uses a conjunctive antecedent, which is perfectly correct because the ∧ is inside the implication, not replacing it.

The existential quantifier (∃)

The sentence ∃x P(x) — "there exists an x such that P of x" — is true in a model when there is at least one object which, substituted for x, makes P(x) true. The mirror-image intuition applies: ∃ behaves like a disjunction over the domain. For the four-object domain above,

∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John)

is equivalent to

(Crown(Richard) ∧ OnHead(Richard, John))
∨ (Crown(John) ∧ OnHead(John, John))
∨ (Crown(Crown) ∧ OnHead(Crown, John))
∨ (Crown(LeftLeg(Richard)) ∧ OnHead(LeftLeg(Richard), John))

and exactly one disjunct — the third — needs to be true for the whole sentence to hold. Note also that ∃ asserts "at least one", not "exactly one"; the sentence remains true if John is wearing two crowns at once.

Because ∃ is a disjunction, the natural partner connective is ∧: we want to assert that a single object has several properties simultaneously. Using ⇒ with ∃ is the complementary classic error:

∃x Crown(x) ⇒ OnHead(x, John)    ← WRONG

Expanded, this is a disjunction of implications, and it is satisfied as soon as any object in the domain fails the antecedent. Richard is not a crown, so Crown(Richard) ⇒ OnHead(Richard, John) is vacuously true, so the whole sentence is true — even in a world with no crowns at all, or where John is bare-headed. The sentence is almost a tautology and asserts essentially nothing. The symmetry is worth memorising:

QuantifierReading over the domainNatural partner connectiveTypical error and why it fails
∀xConjunction of all instances⇒ (guards the class)∀ with ∧ claims every object in the universe has the property
∃xDisjunction of all instances∧ (bundles properties on one object)∃ with ⇒ is trivially satisfied by any object failing the antecedent

When ∀ and ∃ are mixed, order matters enormously. Compare:

∀x ∃y Loves(x, y)     "Everybody loves somebody (possibly a different somebody each time)."
∃y ∀x Loves(x, y)     "There is one particular person whom everybody loves."

The second entails the first — if a universally beloved individual exists, then certainly everyone loves someone — but not conversely. In a world with Alice loving Bob, Bob loving Carol, and Carol loving Alice, the first sentence is true and the second is false. The reason is scoping: in ∀x ∃y ... the choice of y occurs inside the scope of x, so y may depend on x; in ∃y ∀x ... the choice of y is made first and must work for all x. (This dependency is exactly what Skolemisation makes explicit when converting to clausal form: the inner existential becomes a Skolem function of the outer universally quantified variable, Loves(x, F(x)), whereas an outer existential becomes a Skolem constant, Loves(x, B).)

Quantifier equivalences and nesting

Because ∀ is a generalised conjunction and ∃ a generalised disjunction, De Morgan's laws lift straight from propositional logic to the quantifiers. The two quantifiers are interdefinable through negation:

EquivalenceReading
∀x P(x) ≡ ¬∃x ¬P(x)"Everyone likes ice cream" = "There is no one who does not like ice cream"
∃x P(x) ≡ ¬∀x ¬P(x)"Someone likes broccoli" = "Not everyone dislikes broccoli"
¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x)"Not everyone likes broccoli" = "Someone does not like broccoli"
¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x)"No one likes broccoli" = "Everyone does not like broccoli"

These mirror ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ ¬A ∨ ¬B and ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ ¬A ∧ ¬B exactly. Strictly speaking only one quantifier is primitive; the other is definable. Practically, the equivalences are the tool for pushing negations inward when converting sentences to conjunctive normal form for resolution.

Regarding reordering: adjacent quantifiers of the same type commute freely, so ∀x ∀y S(x, y) ≡ ∀y ∀x S(x, y) and ∃x ∃y S(x, y) ≡ ∃y ∃x S(x, y). Mixed quantifiers do not commute, as the Loves example showed; the only valid one-way relationship is ∃y ∀x S(x, y) ⊨ ∀x ∃y S(x, y).

Scope and binding. The scope of a quantifier is the sentence immediately following it (after precedence is resolved). An occurrence of a variable inside the scope of a quantifier for that variable is bound; an occurrence outside any such scope is free. In

(∀x P(x, y)) ∧ Q(x)

the x in P is bound, but the y and the x in Q are free. A formula containing free variables is an open formula, not a sentence: it has no truth value in a model until the free variables are assigned objects. Only closed formulas (sentences) can be asserted into a knowledge base. Renaming a bound variable consistently throughout its scope changes nothing — ∀x King(x) and ∀z King(z) are the same sentence (alphabetic variants).

Shadowing. Reusing the same variable name in nested scopes is legal but confusing, exactly as in a programming language:

∀x [Person(x) ⇒ (∃x Dog(x) ∧ Owns(x, x))]

Here the inner ∃x re-binds x, so the outer x is invisible inside the brackets and Owns(x, x) says the dog owns itself — certainly not the intended "every person owns a dog". Rewriting with fresh names makes the intent unambiguous:

∀x Person(x) ⇒ (∃y Dog(y) ∧ Owns(x, y))

The practical rule is simple: give every quantifier its own variable name (standardising apart), which is also a mandatory preprocessing step in CNF conversion for first-order resolution.

Semantics: interpretations and models

In propositional logic a model is just a truth assignment. In first-order logic a model is a richer structure with two components:

  • A domain (or universe of discourse) D: a non-empty set of objects. Non-emptiness is required so that ∀x P(x) ⇒ ∃x P(x) remains valid and quantified sentences behave sensibly. Objects in the domain are mathematical abstractions — they need not be nameable by any constant in the vocabulary.
  • An interpretation I: a mapping that assigns a meaning to every non-logical symbol:
    • each constant symbol to exactly one object in D;
    • each predicate symbol of arity n to a set of n-tuples of domain objects — the tuples for which the relation holds (the relation's extension);
    • each function symbol of arity n to a total function from Dn to D, so that every input tuple has exactly one output object.

Consider a tiny model of the Richard-and-John world. Let the domain be D = {r, j, c, l}, where r is Richard the Lionheart, j is King John, c is a crown and l is Richard's left leg. An interpretation might be:

SymbolKindInterpretation in this model
RichardConstantr
JohnConstantj
KingUnary predicate{ ⟨j⟩ }
PersonUnary predicate{ ⟨r⟩, ⟨j⟩ }
CrownUnary predicate{ ⟨c⟩ }
BrotherBinary predicate{ ⟨r, j⟩, ⟨j, r⟩ }

LeftLegUnary functionr ↦ l, j ↦ l, c ↦ l, l ↦ l

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the interpretation of LeftLeg must be total: even though crowns have no legs, the function symbol must return something for every argument, so the model simply maps the awkward cases somewhere (here, to l). Modellers often introduce a dummy "invisible object" for this purpose, or avoid the issue by using a predicate instead. Second, nothing forces distinct constants to denote distinct objects, and nothing forces every object to have a name — l is unnamed by any constant and is reachable only through the term LeftLeg(Richard).

Truth is then defined compositionally. A term denotes an object: a constant denotes whatever I assigns it, a bound variable denotes the object currently substituted for it, and f(t₁,…,tₙ) denotes the result of applying I(f) to the objects denoted by t₁,…,tₙ. An atomic sentence P(t₁,…,tₙ) is true exactly when the tuple of denoted objects lies in I(P). Complex sentences are evaluated by the usual truth tables, and quantified sentences by the conjunction/disjunction-over-the-domain rules described earlier. So in our model:

  • Brother(Richard, John) is true, because ⟨r, j⟩ ∈ I(Brother).
  • King(Richard) is false, because ⟨r⟩ ∉ I(King).
  • ∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John) is true, witnessed by c.
  • ∀x King(x) ⇒ Person(x) is true: the only king is j, and ⟨j⟩ ∈ I(Person); the other three objects satisfy the implication vacuously.
  • ∀x King(x) ∧ Person(x) is false, since c is neither.
  • Brother(LeftLeg(Richard), John) is false, because ⟨l, j⟩ ∉ I(Brother).

With truth-in-a-model defined, the remaining semantic notions carry over unchanged from propositional logic, which is one of the great economies of the first-order framework:

  • Entailment. KB ⊨ α when α is true in every model in which every sentence of KB is true. Equivalently, there is no model that satisfies KB ∪ {¬α} — the fact exploited by resolution refutation.
  • Validity. α is valid when it is true in every model under every interpretation: for example ∀x P(x) ⇒ P(A), or ∀x (P(x) ∨ ¬P(x)). Valid first-order sentences are the theorems of pure logic.
  • Satisfiability. α is satisfiable when some model makes it true; unsatisfiable sentences such as ∃x (P(x) ∧ ¬P(x)) are true in none. As before, KB ⊨ α if and only if KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable.

The difference from the propositional case is purely computational. There, "every model" meant one of 2n rows of a truth table and could be enumerated. Here, "every model" means every choice of domain — of every finite size and infinite ones too — and every interpretation over it. Enumeration is impossible, so first-order reasoning must proceed by proof (unification, resolution, chaining) rather than by model checking.

Translating natural language into first-order sentences

Formalisation is a design task, not a mechanical one, and it is where most practical errors originate. A workable discipline is:

  • Fix the ontology first. Decide what the objects are (people? courses? time intervals? events?), then which properties and relationships you need as predicates, then which single-valued mappings are better expressed as functions. Write down the intended meaning of every symbol in English before writing any sentences — an undocumented predicate is a future bug.
  • Watch the quantifier cues. "Every", "all", "any", "each", "whoever" and bare plurals ("dogs bark") signal ∀. "Some", "a", "there is", "at least one", "someone" signal ∃.
  • Apply the connective rule. ∀ with ⇒, ∃ with ∧ — the overwhelmingly common pattern.
  • Test against small models. Invent a two- or three-object world, evaluate the candidate sentence, and ask whether its truth value matches your intuition. This catches both over-commitment (the sentence says more than the English, e.g. ∀ with ∧) and under-commitment (the sentence is vacuously true, e.g. ∃ with ⇒).

Worked examples, with a domain of people and animals:

EnglishFirst-order sentenceComment
Every student passed the exam.∀x Student(x) ⇒ Passed(x, Exam1)Universal guarded by implication.
Some student passed the exam.∃x Student(x) ∧ Passed(x, Exam1)Existential bundled with conjunction.
Every person owns a dog.∀x Person(x) ⇒ (∃y Dog(y) ∧ Owns(x, y))Mixed quantifiers; the dog may differ per person.
There is a dog that everyone owns.∃y Dog(y) ∧ (∀x Person(x) ⇒ Owns(x, y))Stronger claim: one shared dog.

Negative quantification. "No", "none", "nobody" and "never" have two equally correct renderings, related by the equivalences above. "No student failed" becomes either

¬∃x Student(x) ∧ Failed(x)           (negated existential)
∀x Student(x) ⇒ ¬Failed(x)           (universal with negated consequent)

Both are logically equivalent; the second is usually preferred in knowledge bases because it is already close to clause form. A sanity check: do not write ¬∀x Student(x) ⇒ Failed(x), which merely denies that all students failed — a far weaker claim.

Equality. The equality symbol is a built-in binary predicate whose interpretation is fixed in every model: t₁ = t₂ is true exactly when t₁ and t₂ denote the same object. It is indispensable for several idioms:

  • Asserting identity: FatherOf(John) = Henry.
  • Asserting distinctness: ¬(Richard = John), often written Richard ≠ John. Without such axioms a model is free to make two constants denote the same object — a "unique names" assumption must be stated explicitly in pure FOL.
  • Defining relations: ∀x ∀y Sibling(x, y) ⇔ (¬(x = y) ∧ ∃m ∃f ¬(m = f) ∧ Parent(m, x) ∧ Parent(f, x) ∧ Parent(m, y) ∧ Parent(f, y)) — note the explicit inequalities, without which a person would count as their own sibling.
  • Counting: "John has at least two brothers" is ∃x ∃y Brother(x, John) ∧ Brother(y, John) ∧ ¬(x = y); "exactly one" is ∃x P(x) ∧ (∀y P(y) ⇒ x = y).

Finally, a note on over- and under-commitment in practice. Under-commitment leaves out information the English conveys: translating "Spouses are of legal age" as ∀x ∀y Spouse(x, y) ⇒ Adult(x) forgets to say anything about y. Over-commitment asserts more than was meant: translating "Students take courses" as ∀x ∀y Student(x) ∧ Course(y) ⇒ Takes(x, y) claims every student takes every course, when the intended reading is ∀x Student(x) ⇒ (∃y Course(y) ∧ Takes(x, y)). Both failures are invisible on inspection and obvious the moment you evaluate the sentence in a three-object test model — which is exactly why the testing step belongs in the workflow.

Propositional logic treats each statement as an indivisible atom: "Socrates is mortal" is just a symbol P, with no internal structure. That makes it impossible to express the fact that whatever holds for every human also holds for Socrates. First-order logic (also called predicate logic) fixes this by looking inside sentences, introducing constants that name objects, predicates that assert properties and relations, functions that map objects to objects, variables that stand for unspecified objects, and the two quantifiers — the universal quantifier for "for all" and the existential quantifier for "there exists."

This added expressive power is what lets a knowledge-based agent say general things about a world rather than enumerating every case. In this course it underpins everything that follows: once you can write first-order sentences, you can describe domains compactly, reason about them with inference procedures, and connect logical representation to the agent architectures introduced earlier in the module. Knowledge Engineering in First-Order Logic

▶

Knowledge engineering is the craft of turning what people know about a domain into a formal, machine-usable body of sentences. In first-order logic (FOL) this means choosing a vocabulary of constants, functions and predicates, writing axioms that constrain how that vocabulary behaves, and then letting a general-purpose inference engine answer questions by deduction rather than by running hand-written procedures. The engineer is not writing an algorithm for a single question; they are writing a theory of a domain. The pay-off, and the discipline required, both follow from that distinction.

What knowledge engineering is

A knowledge engineer investigates a domain, decides what concepts matter in it, and produces a formal representation of the objects, relations and laws of that domain. The result is a knowledge base (KB) — a set of first-order sentences — that is handed to an inference engine. Crucially, the KB is not tied to one question. Once the behaviour of logic gates, connectivity and signals has been axiomatised, the same KB can answer "what output does input vector ⟨1,1,0⟩ produce?", "which input vectors make the sum bit 0 and the carry bit 1?", "which gates feed terminal In(2, X2)?" and "is this circuit's output independent of input 3?" without a single line of new reasoning code.

This is the practical meaning of the declarative approach. In a procedural program, knowledge about adders is fused with the control flow that uses it; to ask a new question you write a new program. In a declarative system, knowledge and inference are separated:

Direction of useInputs → outputs onlyInputs → outputs, outputs → inputs, partial information

Error modeWrong outputNo answer (missing axiom) or false answer (wrong axiom)

Reusability is the headline benefit. The axiom "the output of an AND gate is 0 if and only if some input is 0" is used identically when simulating a circuit, when verifying it against a specification, when diagnosing a fault, and when searching for an input that triggers a given output. The reasoning procedure — unification, chaining, resolution — never changes.

Knowledge bases come in two broad flavours. A special-purpose KB covers one narrow domain: digital circuits, a family tree, the rules of a card game, the drug interactions of one pharmacological class. Its vocabulary is small, its axioms are tailored, and it is usually built by a single engineer in days or weeks. A general ontology (sometimes called an upper ontology) aims to provide concepts that recur across all domains — objects, events, time, space, substances, categories, measures, beliefs — so that special-purpose knowledge bases can be bolted onto a shared skeleton rather than reinventing "is a part of" and "happens before" each time. General ontologies are far harder: they must accommodate many different special-purpose views at once, which means no single view can be baked in. A useful rule of thumb is that a general ontology must unify at least two distinct domains before it has earned the name.

Finally, the quality of a knowledge base depends much more on the representation chosen than on logical virtuosity. Experienced engineers spend most of their time deciding what the objects are, not deriving clever lemmas. A representation that matches the structure of the domain makes the axioms short, general and obviously correct; a mismatched one makes them long, repetitive, riddled with special cases, or simply unwriteable. The six steps below are a disciplined way of arriving at a good representation — and of noticing early when you have a bad one.

Step 1: Identify the task and gather knowledge

The first job is to pin down the task precisely, and the sharpest way to do that is to write down the queries the KB will have to answer together with the facts that will accompany each query. Vague goals like "represent digital circuits" are useless; "given a gate-level netlist, determine the output values for any input vector, and determine which input vectors produce a specified output" is a task. The difference matters because the queries determine the vocabulary. If the task is circuit simulation and verification, you need signals, gates, terminals and connections. If the task were timing analysis, you would also need propagation delays, clock edges and real-valued durations — a different ontology altogether.

A good way to make the task concrete is to tabulate it:

Query the KB must answerFacts supplied with the query
What are the circuit's outputs for inputs ⟨1,0,1⟩?The netlist; the three input signal values
Which input vectors give sum=0, carry=1?The netlist; the desired output values
Which terminals are connected to In(1, C1)?The netlist only
Does the circuit compute one-bit addition?The netlist; the specification as a set of queries

Next comes knowledge acquisition: finding out how the domain actually works. This stage is deliberately informal. The knowledge engineer interviews domain experts, reads manuals and standards, examines worked examples, and takes notes in English, diagrams and tables. Nothing is written in logic yet. For a circuit KB, a hardware engineer will explain that a gate's output is determined by its inputs, that wires carry a single value, that an XOR gate outputs 1 exactly when its inputs differ, and — a point outsiders often miss — that a "connection" between two terminals is symmetric and transitive, so a fan-out node forces several terminals to share one value. Acquisition is where most domain errors are prevented; a misunderstood sentence here becomes a wrong axiom in step 3.

The acquisition stage must also decide, explicitly and in writing, what to leave out. Real logic gates have propagation delays, fan-out limits, supply voltages, physical sizes, wire gauges, temperature coefficients and prices. For the stated task, none of these appear in any query, so none of them need appear in the ontology. Omitting them is not sloppiness; it is the modelling decision that makes the KB tractable. The criterion is simple: if no query and no supplied fact mentions a property, leave it out — but record the omission, because tomorrow's task may need it.

Scoping errors run in both directions:

  • Under-scoping. You omit something real queries depend on. A circuit KB with no notion of terminal connection can describe gates individually but cannot compute a circuit's output at all; a medical KB that records diagnoses but not dosages cannot answer the questions clinicians actually ask. The symptom is a KB that is elegant and useless.
  • Over-scoping. You model timing, geometry and cost "in case they're needed". Every extra predicate must be axiomatised, every instance must be annotated with values for it, and every inference step now has more clauses to consider, so the search space grows. Worse, half-modelled extras tend to be inconsistently populated, which produces mysterious failures later.

Step 2: Decide on a vocabulary of predicates, functions and constants

This step translates the informal concepts of step 1 into FOL vocabulary — the ontology of the domain. The basic mapping is:

  • Objects → constants and terms. Individual gates, terminals and circuits become constants (X1, A2, C1) or compound terms built by functions.
  • Properties and relations → predicates. "x is a gate" becomes Gate(x); "t1 and t2 are wired together" becomes Connected(t1, t2).
  • Single-valued attributes → functions. "The value on terminal t" becomes Signal(t); "the ith input terminal of gate g" becomes In(i, g); "the type of gate g" becomes Type(g).

The function-versus-predicate choice deserves care because it is where a lot of hidden knowledge gets encoded for free. Compare two ways of saying what kind of gate X1 is:

Predicate version:  Type(X1, XOR)
Function version:   Type(X1) = XOR

With the predicate version, nothing stops the KB from also containing Type(X1, AND); to rule that out you must add an explicit uniqueness axiom:

∀ g,t1,t2  Type(g,t1) ∧ Type(g,t2) ⇒ t1 = t2

With the function version, the uniqueness is guaranteed by the semantics of FOL: a function symbol denotes a total function, so Type(X1) has exactly one value in every model. Using a function therefore enforces single-valuedness for free. The same reasoning applies to Signal(t) (a wire carries one value), Mother(x) (a person has one biological mother) and Arity(g). Conversely, use a predicate when the attribute can hold of several things at once: Sibling(x, y), Connected(t1, t2), Likes(x, y).

Domain notionBest encoded asWhy
Value on a wireFunction Signal(t)Exactly one value per terminal
Kind of a gateFunction Type(g)Exactly one type per gate

Wiring between terminalsPredicate Connected(t1,t2)A terminal may connect to many others
Being a gate / terminal / circuitPredicates Gate(x), Terminal(x), Circuit(x)Category membership, many instances

Notice how much work In(i, g) and Out(i, g) do. Instead of inventing constants X1In1, X1In2, X1Out1, … for every terminal of every gate, one function pair names them all systematically, and axioms can quantify over indices. This is typical: a well-chosen function turns an unbounded list of constants into a compact, generalisable term structure.

A further representational device is reification — promoting something that might naively be a predicate into a first-class object so that you can say things about it. Consider marriage. If marriage is only the predicate Married(x, y), you cannot say when it took place, where, who officiated, or that it was later annulled, because FOL does not allow predicates to take predicates as arguments or to be given properties. Reify instead:

Marriage(M17) ∧ Spouse(M17, John) ∧ Spouse(M17, Mary)
  ∧ Date(M17) = Jan-12-2019 ∧ Officiant(M17) = Rev-Smith
  ∧ Location(M17) = StMarys

The same move is used for events, measurements, purchases, diagnoses and categories. In the circuit domain, gate types are reified as objects (AND, OR, XOR, NOT) rather than being left as predicates AndGate(x). That lets the KB state Type(X1) = Type(X2) ("these two gates are the same kind"), compare types, count them, or quantify over types — all impossible if "AND-ness" were only a predicate. The cost of reification is an extra layer of indirection; the benefit is expressive power, so reify when the task's queries need to talk about the thing itself.

Expect the vocabulary to be wrong the first time. The diagnostic signal is the shape of the axioms you then have to write. Symptoms of a poor vocabulary include axioms that must be restated once per gate type, axioms containing long disjunctions over named individuals, facts that must be entered twice to keep two representations in step, and — the worst case — a true statement about the domain that simply cannot be written down. Any of these is a cue to return to step 2 rather than to patch step 3.

Step 3: Encode general knowledge as axioms

Now the domain laws are written in FOL using the chosen vocabulary. The best axioms are definitions: biconditionals that fully specify when a predicate or function value holds, so that the inference engine can reason in both directions. For the circuit domain, the behaviour of each gate type is given definitionally:

1. Connected terminals share a signal:
   ∀ t1,t2  Connected(t1,t2) ⇒ Signal(t1) = Signal(t2)

2. Signals are binary:
   ∀ t  Terminal(t) ⇒ Signal(t) = 1 ∨ Signal(t) = 0
   1 ≠ 0

3. Connection is commutative:
   ∀ t1,t2  Connected(t1,t2) ⇔ Connected(t2,t1)

4. OR gate: output is 1 iff some input is 1
   ∀ g  Type(g) = OR ⇒
        (Signal(Out(1,g)) = 1 ⇔ ∃ n  Signal(In(n,g)) = 1)

5. AND gate: output is 0 iff some input is 0
   ∀ g  Type(g) = AND ⇒
        (Signal(Out(1,g)) = 0 ⇔ ∃ n  Signal(In(n,g)) = 0)

6. XOR gate: output is 1 iff its inputs differ
   ∀ g  Type(g) = XOR ⇒
        (Signal(Out(1,g)) = 1 ⇔ Signal(In(1,g)) ≠ Signal(In(2,g)))

7. NOT gate: output differs from input
   ∀ g  Type(g) = NOT ⇒
        Signal(Out(1,g)) ≠ Signal(In(1,g))

Each of these is a bidirectional statement where possible, which is exactly what allows the KB to run "backwards": from a desired output back to the inputs that could produce it. Had axiom 6 been written only as "if the inputs differ then the output is 1", the engine could simulate but not verify.

Alongside behavioural definitions, a KB needs structural facts: how things are composed, what is part of what, and how categories relate. In the circuit domain the structural axioms describe arity and the gate/terminal/circuit type hierarchy:

8. Gates have the right number of terminals:
   ∀ g  Gate(g) ∧ Type(g) = NOT ⇒ Arity(g,1,1)
   ∀ g  Gate(g) ∧ (Type(g) = AND ∨ Type(g) = OR ∨ Type(g) = XOR)
        ⇒ Arity(g,2,1)

9. Terminals exist for the declared arity:
   ∀ c,i,j  Circuit(c) ∧ Arity(c,i,j) ⇒
        (∀ n  n ≤ i ⇒ Terminal(In(n,c))) ∧
        (∀ n  n > i ⇒ In(n,c) = Nothing) ∧
        (∀ n  n ≤ j ⇒ Terminal(Out(n,c))) ∧
        (∀ n  n > j ⇒ Out(n,c) = Nothing)

10. Gates are circuits; circuits and terminals are distinct things:
    ∀ g  Gate(g) ⇒ Circuit(g)
    ∀ x  ¬(Gate(x) ∧ Terminal(x))

In other domains the same category of axiom shows up as part-of hierarchies (PartOf(Engine7, Car3) with transitivity), taxonomies (∀x Dog(x) ⇒ Mammal(x)) and containment or adjacency relations.

The third category is the one novices forget: obvious background truths that the system cannot infer for itself. Logic has no common sense. If you do not state that Connected is symmetric, then asserting Connected(Out(1,X1), In(1,A2)) does not license Connected(In(1,A2), Out(1,X1)), and half your signal propagation silently fails. Similarly, FOL does not assume that distinct names denote distinct objects, so unless you say so, a model may identify AND with OR, or X1 with X2, and axiom 4 will then fire on XOR gates:

11. Distinctness of names:
    AND ≠ OR,  AND ≠ XOR,  AND ≠ NOT,
    OR  ≠ XOR, OR  ≠ NOT,  XOR ≠ NOT
    1 ≠ 0

(Some systems adopt a unique names assumption that supplies these automatically; if yours does not, write them.) Other commonly omitted background truths include irreflexivity (∀x ¬Parent(x,x)), mutual exclusivity of categories, and the fact that an object's parts are not identical to the whole.

Finally, keep axioms general. The whole point of quantification is that one sentence covers unboundedly many cases. An axiom written as 

Signal(Out(1,X1)) = 1 ⇔ Signal(In(1,X1)) ≠ Signal(In(2,X1))
Signal(Out(1,X2)) = 1 ⇔ Signal(In(1,X2)) ≠ Signal(In(2,X2))

says about the two XOR gates of one adder exactly what axiom 6 says about every XOR gate in every circuit anyone will ever describe. Enumerated axioms are a maintenance disaster: adding a third XOR gate means editing the general knowledge instead of just the instance description, and a correction must be applied in n places. The test is whether the sentence mentions any instance constant. General axioms should mention only type constants (AND, XOR, 1, 0) and variables; instance constants (X1, C1) belong in step 4.

Step 4: Encode the specific problem instance

With the general theory in place, describing a particular circuit is mechanical — it is essentially transcribing a diagram. Take the standard one-bit full adder C1, built from two XOR gates (X1, X2), two AND gates (A1, A2) and one OR gate (O1). The instance description has three parts.

First, introduce a constant for each individual object and assert its category:

Circuit(C1) ∧ Arity(C1, 3, 2)
Gate(X1) ∧ Gate(X2) ∧ Gate(A1) ∧ Gate(A2) ∧ Gate(O1)

Note that the terminals need no constants of their own: the functions In and Out from step 2 name them (In(1,C1), Out(1,X1), …). That is the payoff of the earlier vocabulary decision.

Second, assert each object's attributes using the chosen functions and predicates:

Type(X1) = XOR   Type(X2) = XOR
Type(A1) = AND   Type(A2) = AND
Type(O1) = OR

Third, assert the relations that link the instances — here, the wiring:

Connected(Out(1,X1), In(1,X2))   Connected(In(1,C1), In(1,X1))
Connected(Out(1,X1), In(2,A2))   Connected(In(1,C1), In(1,A1))
Connected(Out(1,A2), In(1,O1))   Connected(In(2,C1), In(2,X1))
Connected(Out(1,A1), In(2,O1))   Connected(In(2,C1), In(2,A1))
Connected(Out(1,X2), Out(1,C1))  Connected(In(3,C1), In(2,X2))
Connected(Out(1,O1), Out(2,C1))  Connected(In(3,C1), In(1,A2))
Instance elementSentence formExample
Existence of an objectCategory predicate on a new constantGate(A1)
Attribute of an objectFunction equationType(A1) = AND
Link between objectsRelation predicate on termsConnected(Out(1,A1), In(2,O1))
Externally supplied dataFunction equation on a terminalSignal(In(1,C1)) = 1

The division of labour between steps 3 and 4 is what makes the KB reusable. To reason about a four-bit ripple-carry adder, a multiplexer or a parity checker, you discard the instance block and write a new one; axioms 1–11 are untouched. The same pattern recurs everywhere: a general theory of kinship plus one family's facts; a general theory of drug interaction plus one patient's medication list; a general theory of taxation plus one taxpayer's return. If you ever find yourself editing step 3 to accommodate a new instance, something in the general theory was not general enough.

Step 5: Pose queries and interpret answers

Queries are posed as existentially quantified sentences, and the answer is not merely "yes" but the set of substitutions (bindings for the existential variables) under which the KB entails the sentence. This is why a logical KB is more useful than a yes/no theorem prover: the proof carries the answer with it.

Suppose we add the input facts Signal(In(1,C1)) = 1, Signal(In(2,C1)) = 1, Signal(In(3,C1)) = 0 and ask:

∃ i1,i2  Signal(Out(1,C1)) = i1 ∧ Signal(Out(2,C1)) = i2

Inference chains through axioms 1 and 6 (both XOR gates), 5 (the ANDs) and 4 (the OR), returning the single substitution {i1/0, i2/1}: sum 0, carry 1 — correct for 1+1+0.

The more interesting direction is verification, which runs the circuit backwards. Remove the input assertions and ask which input vectors produce a given output pattern:

∃ i1,i2,i3  Signal(In(1,C1)) = i1 ∧ Signal(In(2,C1)) = i2 ∧ Signal(In(3,C1)) = i3
            ∧ Signal(Out(1,C1)) = 0 ∧ Signal(Out(2,C1)) = 1

Because the gate axioms are biconditionals and signals are constrained to {0,1}, the engine returns three substitutions: {i1/1, i2/1, i3/0}, {i1/1, i2/0, i3/1} and {i1/0, i2/1, i3/1} — precisely the input vectors with exactly two ones. Repeating the query for each of the four possible output patterns enumerates the complete truth table, which is the specification of a one-bit adder:

Verification then amounts to checking that this derived table matches the intended one. Notice that no simulator was written: the same axioms that computed outputs from inputs computed inputs from outputs, simply by leaving different variables unbound.

Queries need not be about behaviour at all. Structural queries interrogate the wiring itself:

∃ t  Connected(t, In(1,A2))          → {t / Out(1,X1)}, {t / In(3,C1)}
∃ g,t  Type(g) = XOR ∧ Connected(Out(1,g), t)
∃ g  Gate(g) ∧ ¬∃t Connected(Out(1,g), t)   (an unconnected output — a design bug)

These are the queries that matter for design-rule checking, netlist navigation and fault isolation, and they come free with the same KB.

Interpreting answers requires care about one point: in classical FOL, "no answer returned" means "not entailed", not "false". Because the KB makes no closed-world assumption, failure to find a substitution may equally mean that a necessary axiom is missing. If the adder query returned nothing, the most likely explanations are a forgotten Connected fact, a missing symmetry axiom, an absent "signals are binary" axiom (so the engine cannot do case analysis), or a missing distinctness axiom such as 1 ≠ 0. Treat silence as a bug report about the KB, not a verdict about the world.

Step 6: Debug and refine the knowledge base

Knowledge bases are rarely right first time, and the final step — debugging — usually consumes more effort than the writing. Logical debugging has a distinctive character: errors come in two clean flavours, and each has its own signature.

FaultSymptomTypical causeDiagnosis technique
Axiom too weak / missingQuery silently returns no answerForgotten background truth, forgotten connection, one-directional implication where a biconditional was neededAsk progressively smaller sub-queries until one fails; the first failure localises the gap
Axiom too strong / wrongFalse conclusion, or an outright contradictionOver-general quantification, missing precondition, wrong direction of an implicationInspect the proof tree for the false conclusion; every leaf is an asserted sentence, so the culprit is among them

The missing-axiom case is treacherous because logic gives no error message. Asking "what is the output of X1?" when the KB lacks Type(X1) = XOR does not raise an exception; it simply produces nothing, exactly as a well-posed but unprovable query would. The remedy is systematic narrowing. If Signal(Out(1,C1)) cannot be derived, ask for Signal(Out(1,X2)), then Signal(In(1,X2)), then Signal(Out(1,X1)), and so on back toward the inputs. The first query in that chain that does succeed brackets the fault: the gap lies between it and its failing successor. Classic culprits are an omitted Connected fact for one wire, an axiom stated for AND but never for XOR, or an implication written ⇒ where the query needs the converse.

The over-strong-axiom case is noisier but easier. Suppose someone writes

∀ g  Signal(Out(1,g)) = 1 ⇔ ∃n Signal(In(n,g)) = 1

forgetting the guard Type(g) = OR. Now every gate behaves like an OR gate, and the adder's derived truth table is wrong; worse, combined with axiom 5 the KB may become inconsistent, from which anything follows. Because the conclusion was reached by a proof, the proof itself is the diagnostic artefact: walk it from the false conclusion back to its leaves, and the offending sentence is one of them. Contradictions are detected the same way — a refutation proof names exactly the premises it used.

Testing should proceed from cases whose answers are independently known and then widen:

  • Start with ground sanity checks whose answers you can verify by hand (all-zero inputs; a single NOT gate).
  • Move to the full specification — all eight input vectors of the adder — comparing against the published truth table.
  • Add negative tests: deliberately mis-wire a copy of the circuit and confirm the KB no longer entails the specification. A KB that "verifies" a broken circuit is over-strong somewhere.
  • Test structural queries and boundary cases: a gate with an unconnected input, an index out of arity range, two circuits sharing a terminal name.
  • Re-run the whole suite after every change; a fix to one axiom routinely breaks another.

Expect iteration, and expect it to reach back further than step 3. Many debugging sessions end with the realisation that the vocabulary chosen in step 2 was wrong. Suppose the adder KB must now handle gates with three or more inputs, or tri-state outputs with a third signal value, or faults in which a wire is stuck at 0 regardless of its driver. Each of these breaks an assumption baked into the ontology: that Arity is fixed per type, that Signal ranges over {0,1}, that Connected terminals always share a value. Patching axioms to work around an ontological mismatch produces the symptoms listed in step 2 — proliferating special cases, duplicated facts, statements that cannot be expressed at all — and the right response is to redesign the vocabulary (for instance, reifying a driver relation separate from connection, or introducing a Fault object per component) and re-encode. This loop — task, vocabulary, axioms, instance, query, debug, back to vocabulary — is the normal rhythm of knowledge engineering, and recognising when to go backwards rather than patch forwards is the main skill the six-step discipline is meant to teach.

Knowledge engineering is the practical craft behind first-order logic: taking a real domain, such as electronic circuits, family relationships, or a shopping world, and turning it into a knowledge base that an inference engine can reason over. The standard process runs in roughly six steps: identify the task and the questions the system must answer, gather the relevant domain knowledge, settle on a vocabulary of predicates, functions and constants (the ontology), encode the general rules of the domain as axioms, encode the specific problem instance as facts, pose queries and interpret the answers, and finally debug and refine when the answers are wrong or missing.

The hard part is usually not the logic notation but the design decisions. Should "the parent of x" be a function Parent(x) or a relation Parent(x,y)? Should a circuit gate's type be a constant, a predicate, or a term? These choices determine how naturally the axioms can be written and how efficiently queries can be answered, and they are exactly the choices that make knowledge bases easy or painful to debug later.

Unification and Lifted Inference

1

Unification and Lifted Inference

▶

When reasoning moves from propositional logic to first-order logic, the inference machinery gains enormous expressive power but also faces a new technical problem. In propositional logic, a rule such as Rain ⇒ WetGround matches a fact only if the fact is literally the symbol Rain. In first-order logic, a rule such as ∀x King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x) is supposed to apply to every individual, so the inference engine must recognise that the stored fact King(John) is an instance of the rule's premise King(x). The operation that performs this recognition — finding the variable assignments that make two logical expressions syntactically identical — is called unification, and the data structure that records those assignments is a substitution. Unification is the single mechanism that allows propositional inference rules to be "lifted" so that one first-order inference step stands in for an unbounded number of propositional steps.

This topic develops that machinery from the ground up: what substitutions are and how they compose, what it means for two expressions to unify, why we insist on the most general unifier, how the recursive unification algorithm works (including the subtle occur-check), and finally how unification powers the two central lifted inference rules — Generalized Modus Ponens for definite clauses and first-order resolution for full first-order logic.

Substitutions: binding variables to terms. A substitution is a finite set of variable/term pairs, written with a slash, such as θ = {x/John, y/Richard}. Each pair is a binding: it says "wherever the variable x appears, put the term John instead." The variables on the left-hand sides must be distinct, so a substitution is really a function from a finite set of variables to terms. Terms on the right may themselves be constants (John), other variables (z), or compound function terms (Mother(y), F(G(z))).

Applying a substitution θ to a sentence or term α is written SUBST(θ, α), or more compactly αθ. The operation walks through α and simultaneously replaces every free occurrence of each bound variable by its associated term. "Simultaneously" matters: the replacement is not iterated, so terms introduced by the substitution are not themselves re-scanned for further replacement.

θ = {x/John, y/Richard}

SUBST(θ, Knows(x, y))            =  Knows(John, Richard)
SUBST(θ, Loves(Father(x), y))    =  Loves(Father(John), Richard)
SUBST(θ, Knows(x, z))            =  Knows(John, z)        (z is untouched)
SUBST(θ, King(Arthur))           =  King(Arthur)          (no variables, no change)

σ = {x/y, y/John}
SUBST(σ, P(x, y))                =  P(y, John)            NOT P(John, John)

That last example is worth dwelling on. Because the replacement is simultaneous, the y produced from x is not then rewritten to John. Implementations that naively apply bindings one at a time in a loop will get this wrong and produce unsound results.

A substitution is legal (sometimes called idempotent, or free of circularity) only if no variable is bound to a term that contains that same variable. The binding {x/F(x)} is illegal: applying it to P(x) gives P(F(x)), which still contains x, and applying it again gives P(F(F(x))), and so on without limit. This is the occur-check condition, and it is the syntactic guard that prevents an inference engine from constructing infinite terms. More generally, a substitution should contain no cycles among its bindings: {x/G(y), y/H(x)} is equally illegal even though neither binding alone violates the simple test, because resolving the bindings leads to x/G(H(G(H(…)))).

A ground substitution binds every variable in the expression to a term containing no variables at all. The result is a ground sentence: one with no variables, which behaves essentially like a propositional symbol as far as truth-value evaluation is concerned. Ground sentences are the bridge between first-order logic and propositional reasoning, and they are what a model checker ultimately evaluates.

θg = {x/John, y/Mother(John)}
SUBST(θg, Knows(x, y))  =  Knows(John, Mother(John))     -- ground
σ  = {x/John}
SUBST(σ,  Knows(x, y))  =  Knows(John, y)                -- not ground, y remains

Composing substitutions lets an inference engine chain results together across several steps. The composition θ ∘ σ (often written θσ) means "apply θ first, then σ," so that α(θσ) = (αθ)σ. Computationally, the composition is built by applying σ to every term on the right-hand side of θ, and then adding any bindings of σ whose variables do not already appear on the left of θ.

θ = {x/F(y)}        σ = {y/Albert, z/John}

θσ = {x/F(Albert), y/Albert, z/John}

Check:  P(x, y, z) θ   =  P(F(y), y, z)
        P(F(y), y, z) σ =  P(F(Albert), Albert, John)
        P(x, y, z) (θσ) =  P(F(Albert), Albert, John)     ✓ same result

Composition is associative but not commutative: σθ in the example above would be {y/Albert, z/John, x/F(y)}, which still leaves a free y inside the binding for x. This is exactly why a chain of resolution or chaining steps must thread its accumulated substitution forward in a fixed order.

The unification problem. Unification inverts the question. Rather than being given a substitution and asked for the result, we are given two expressions and asked for a substitution that makes them identical. Formally, UNIFY(p, q) returns a substitution θ such that SUBST(θ, p) = SUBST(θ, q), and returns failure if no such substitution exists. Two expressions for which such a θ exists are called unifiable, and θ is a unifier.

The classic series of examples uses a knowledge base of Knows facts together with the query Knows(John, x) — "whom does John know?":

pqUNIFY(p, q)Comment
Knows(John, x)Knows(John, Jane){x/Jane}Constants match; the variable binds to Jane.
Knows(John, x)Knows(y, Bill){x/Bill, y/John}Bindings flow in both directions.
Knows(John, x)Knows(y, Mother(y)){y/John, x/Mother(John)}A variable may bind to a compound term.
Knows(John, x)Knows(x, Elizabeth)failurex cannot be John and Elizabeth at once.
Knows(John, x)Likes(John, x)failureDifferent predicate symbols.
P(A, F(x))P(y, F(G(y))){y/A, x/G(A)}Nested function terms unify argument-wise.
P(x, x)P(F(y), y)failure (occur-check)Would require y = F(y).

The fourth row deserves careful attention, because its failure is accidental rather than fundamental. "John knows everyone" and "everyone knows Elizabeth" ought to let us conclude that John knows Elizabeth; the only reason unification fails is that the two sentences happen to reuse the same variable letter. Since variables in distinct sentences are independently quantified, the letter is meaningless — ∀x Knows(John, x) and ∀z Knows(z, Elizabeth) say exactly the same things. The fix is standardizing apart: before attempting unification, rename all variables in one of the sentences to fresh names that occur nowhere else, for example turning x into x17.

Original:        Knows(John, x)    and    Knows(x, Elizabeth)     -- fails

Standardized:    Knows(John, x)    and    Knows(x17, Elizabeth)
UNIFY returns:   {x/Elizabeth, x17/John}
Both become:     Knows(John, Elizabeth)                            -- succeeds

Practical systems standardize apart every time a clause or rule is retrieved from the knowledge base, usually by attaching an incrementing counter to each variable name. Omitting this step is one of the most common sources of silently missed inferences in a first-order reasoner.

The matching rules that underlie unification are purely syntactic and can be stated compactly:

  • Constant vs. constant: unify only if they are the identical symbol. John and John succeed with the empty substitution {}; John and Richard fail.
  • Variable vs. anything: succeeds by binding the variable to the other term, provided the variable is not already bound inconsistently and the occur-check passes.
  • Function or predicate terms: unify only if the function/predicate symbols are identical and the arities match and the argument lists unify pairwise, left to right, with bindings accumulated as we go. F(x, A) and F(B, y) unify as {x/B, y/A}; F(x) and G(x) fail; F(x) and F(x, y) fail on arity.
  • Constant vs. compound term: always fails. John cannot be made identical to Mother(y) by any substitution, since substitution never alters a term's outermost symbol.

Most general unifier (MGU). When two expressions are unifiable, they are typically unifiable in many ways. Consider Knows(John, x) and Knows(y, z). All of the following are unifiers:

θ1 = {y/John, x/z}
θ2 = {y/John, x/z, z/Jane}        -- forces z to be Jane
θ3 = {y/John, x/John, z/John}     -- forces everything to John
θ4 = {y/John, x/Mother(w), z/Mother(w)}

All four make the two expressions identical, but θ2, θ3 and θ4 impose extra commitments that the matching problem never demanded. A unifier θ1 is more general than θ2 if there exists a further substitution λ such that θ2 = θ1λ — that is, θ2 can be obtained from θ1 by additional specialisation. Here θ2 = θ1{z/Jane} and θ3 = θ1{z/John}, so θ1 is more general than both. The most general unifier is a unifier that is more general than every other unifier of the pair; for this example it is θ1 = {y/John, x/z}.

Why insist on the MGU? Because an inference engine that commits to θ3 has silently decided that z is John, and any later step that needed z to be Jane will now fail even though the overall proof was available. The MGU places the fewest possible restrictions on the variables, leaving every downstream option open; it is the least-commitment choice and therefore preserves the completeness of the search. Premature commitment would force the engine to backtrack over arbitrary bindings, converting a deterministic matching step into an unnecessary branch point.

For any two unifiable expressions, the MGU is unique up to renaming of variables. For the example above, {y/John, z/x} is also an MGU; it differs from {y/John, x/z} only in which of the two equivalent variables was kept. Either can be converted to the other by a bijective renaming (a variable permutation), so they are considered the same unifier for all practical purposes. This uniqueness is what makes unification a well-defined, deterministic subroutine rather than a search.

The unification algorithm. The standard algorithm is a recursive procedure over pairs of expressions that threads an accumulating substitution θ through the recursion. It returns either the extended substitution or failure. In pseudocode:

function UNIFY(x, y, θ) returns a substitution or failure
  if θ = failure then return failure
  else if x = y                      then return θ
  else if VARIABLE?(x)               then return UNIFY-VAR(x, y, θ)
  else if VARIABLE?(y)               then return UNIFY-VAR(y, x, θ)
  else if COMPOUND?(x) and COMPOUND?(y) then
        return UNIFY(ARGS(x), ARGS(y), UNIFY(OP(x), OP(y), θ))
  else if LIST?(x) and LIST?(y) then
        return UNIFY(REST(x), REST(y), UNIFY(FIRST(x), FIRST(y), θ))
  else return failure

function UNIFY-VAR(var, x, θ) returns a substitution or failure
  if {var/val} ∈ θ            then return UNIFY(val, x, θ)
  else if {x/val} ∈ θ         then return UNIFY(var, val, θ)
  else if OCCUR-CHECK?(var, x) then return failure
  else return add {var/x} to θ

Several design features are worth unpacking.

  • Accumulation and propagation. The substitution built so far is passed into each recursive call, so a binding made while unifying the first argument constrains the unification of later arguments. Unifying P(x, x) with P(A, B) first yields {x/A}; the second argument pair then becomes effectively A against B, which fails — exactly as it should.
  • UNIFY-VAR and existing bindings. When one side is a variable, UNIFY-VAR first checks whether that variable already has a binding in θ. If it does, the algorithm follows the existing binding and recursively unifies the bound value against the other term, rather than overwriting. If the other term is itself an already-bound variable, the same dereferencing applies to it. Only when neither side is already bound is a genuinely new binding added.
  • The occur-check. Before adding {var/x}, the algorithm tests whether var occurs anywhere inside x. Attempting to unify x with F(x) triggers this test and returns failure, which is correct: no finite term satisfies t = F(t). Without the check, the engine would build a cyclic structure and typically loop forever when it later tried to print or traverse the term. The check costs linear time in the size of x, which in the worst case makes naive unification quadratic; for this reason many Prolog implementations omit it by default (trading soundness for speed) and provide a separate sound unification predicate for when it matters.
  • Compound expressions. A compound is decomposed into its operator (the function or predicate symbol) and its argument list. The operators must unify — in practice, be identical — and then the argument lists are traversed element by element, with each element's result feeding the next.
  • Failure propagation. The very first line, if θ = failure then return failure, means a failure anywhere deep in the recursion short-circuits every enclosing call. There is no partial success: unification is all or nothing.

A worked trace makes the flow concrete. Unify P(A, F(x), z) with P(y, F(G(w)), G(y)), starting from θ = {}:

1. OP: P vs P                     → θ = {}
2. Arg1: A vs y   → y is a variable, unbound, occur-check OK
                                  → θ = {y/A}
3. Arg2: F(x) vs F(G(w))
     OP: F vs F                   → θ = {y/A}
     Arg: x vs G(w)  → x unbound, x does not occur in G(w)
                                  → θ = {y/A, x/G(w)}
4. Arg3: z vs G(y)  → z unbound, z does not occur in G(y)
                                  → θ = {y/A, x/G(w), z/G(y)}

MGU = {y/A, x/G(w), z/G(y)}
Both sides become: P(A, F(G(w)), G(A))      [after resolving z/G(y), y/A]

Contrast that with a failing case, UNIFY(P(x, x), P(F(y), y)):

1. OP: P vs P                     → θ = {}
2. Arg1: x vs F(y)  → bind        → θ = {x/F(y)}
3. Arg2: x vs y     → x is bound to F(y), so UNIFY(F(y), y, θ)
                    → y is a variable; OCCUR-CHECK?(y, F(y)) is TRUE
                    → failure
Result: failure (propagates up through every enclosing call)

Efficient retrieval. In a knowledge base with hundreds of thousands of sentences, running UNIFY against every stored sentence to answer a query is hopeless. Two complementary indexing techniques narrow the candidate set:

  • Predicate indexing stores all facts under a key formed from the predicate symbol (and often the arity, and sometimes the principal functor of the first argument). A query Knows(John, x) then retrieves only the Knows/2 bucket instead of scanning everything. Combined indexing on (predicate, first-argument-constant) is standard in Prolog systems and is often enough to reduce the candidate set to a handful.
  • Subsumption lattices organise queries by generality. Each node is a pattern, with edges from more general patterns to more specific ones: Employs(x, y) sits above Employs(x, Richard) and Employs(AIMA, y), which in turn sit above the ground fact Employs(AIMA, Richard). Facts are stored at the nodes that subsume them, so answering a query means collecting the facts stored at or below the query's node. The lattice grows exponentially in the number of arguments (a pattern with n arguments has up to 2n generalisations), so real systems index on a selected subset of argument positions chosen to match the expected query mix.

Generalized Modus Ponens: lifting propositional inference. With unification in hand we can lift Modus Ponens itself. The Generalized Modus Ponens (GMP) rule states: given atomic sentences p1′, p2′, …, pn′ and an implication (p1 ∧ p2 ∧ … ∧ pn ⇒ q), where there exists a substitution θ with SUBST(θ, pi′) = SUBST(θ, pi) for every i, infer SUBST(θ, q). Written as an inference schema:

   p1′, p2′, …, pn′,   (p1 ∧ p2 ∧ … ∧ pn ⇒ q)
  ------------------------------------------------   where piθ = pi′θ for all i
                   SUBST(θ, q)

The canonical example: the knowledge base contains

(1)  ∀x  King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x)
(2)  King(John)
(3)  ∀y  Greedy(y)

Here p1′ = King(John), p2′ = Greedy(y), p1 = King(x), p2 = Greedy(x), and q = Evil(x). The substitution θ = {x/John, y/John} satisfies the condition, since King(x)θ = King(John) = King(John)θ and Greedy(x)θ = Greedy(John) = Greedy(y)θ. GMP therefore infers SUBST(θ, Evil(x)) = Evil(John) in a single step.

Contrast this with the propositional route. To use propositional Modus Ponens we would first have to propositionalize the knowledge base: instantiate every universally quantified sentence with every ground term in the language, then treat each resulting ground atom as a propositional symbol. With sentence (1) and a domain containing n constants, that produces n ground copies of the rule, of which exactly one is useful. Worse, if the language contains any function symbol — say Father — the set of ground terms is infinite (John, Father(John), Father(Father(John)), …), so full propositionalization never terminates. Herbrand's theorem guarantees that if a sentence is entailed, some finite subset of the ground instances suffices, which is why iterative-deepening propositionalization is semi-decidable; but it is hopelessly inefficient, since it generates vast numbers of irrelevant instances such as King(Father(Father(Richard))). Lifting avoids all of this: unification finds exactly the instantiations the proof needs, on demand, and ignores the rest.

AspectPropositionalization + propositional MPLifted inference with GMP
Instances createdAll ground instances of every ruleOnly those demanded by unification
Behaviour with function symbolsInfinite ground term set; may not terminateTerms constructed only as needed
Steps for the King/Greedy examplen rule instances for n constants, then 1 MP step1 step

GMP is sound: every conclusion it draws is entailed by its premises. The argument is short and instructive. For any sentence p that is universally quantified and any substitution θ, Universal Instantiation gives p ⊨ SUBST(θ, p). So from the premises p1′, …, pn′ we may infer p1′θ, …, pn′θ, and from the implication we may infer (p1θ ∧ … ∧ pnθ ⇒ qθ). By the unification condition, piθ = pi′θ for each i, so the instantiated premises exactly match the antecedent conjuncts, and ordinary propositional Modus Ponens yields qθ. GMP is thus simply Universal Instantiation plus Modus Ponens, compressed into one rule and with the instantiation chosen automatically by unification.

GMP applies to definite clauses: disjunctions of literals with exactly one positive literal, equivalently implications whose antecedent is a conjunction of positive atoms and whose consequent is a single positive atom. Facts such as King(John) are the degenerate case with an empty antecedent. This restricted form is precisely what makes GMP usable as the single inference rule of the two main first-order chaining algorithms:

  • Forward chaining starts from the known facts and repeatedly applies GMP to derive new facts, adding each one to the knowledge base, until the query appears or no new facts can be produced. It is data-driven and naturally computes all consequences; production systems and deductive databases (Datalog) work this way.
  • Backward chaining starts from the query, unifies it with the consequent of some rule, and recursively attempts to prove that rule's antecedents, composing substitutions along the way. It is goal-driven and is the execution model of Prolog.

In both algorithms every match between a goal (or fact) and a clause head is performed by UNIFY, and every answer returned is a substitution — the composition of all the MGUs used along the successful branch. A short backward-chaining trace for the query Evil(q) against the King/Greedy knowledge base shows the composition in action:

Goal: Evil(q)
  unify Evil(q) with head Evil(x17)      → θ1 = {q/x17}
  subgoals: King(x17), Greedy(x17)
    Goal King(x17)
      unify with fact King(John)         → θ2 = {x17/John}
    Goal Greedy(John)        (θ2 applied)
      unify with fact Greedy(y18)        → θ3 = {y18/John}
Answer substitution: θ1θ2θ3 restricted to q  =  {q/John}

The efficiency gain from lifting is not merely constant-factor. A single lifted step can stand in for an entire family of propositional steps; if a rule has k variables ranging over n constants, one GMP application replaces up to nk propositional rule instances that would otherwise have to be generated and tested. This is the central reason first-order reasoners are practical at all.

First-order resolution with unification. GMP is complete only for definite-clause knowledge bases. For full first-order logic — including disjunction, negation and existential quantification — we need resolution, which is likewise lifted by unification. The binary resolution rule for first-order clauses states: given two clauses that have been standardized apart,

  ℓ1 ∨ … ∨ ℓk          and          m1 ∨ … ∨ mn

and a substitution θ = UNIFY(ℓi, ¬mj) ,  infer the resolvent

  SUBST(θ,  ℓ1 ∨ … ∨ ℓi−1 ∨ ℓi+1 ∨ … ∨ ℓk ∨ m1 ∨ … ∨ mj−1 ∨ mj+1 ∨ … ∨ mn)

In words: two clauses resolve when a literal in one and a complementary literal in the other (same predicate, opposite sign) unify under an MGU θ. The resolvent is the disjunction of all the remaining literals from both clauses, with θ applied to every one of them. Applying θ to the leftovers — not just to the resolved pair — is essential, because the bindings discovered during the match constrain the rest of the clause as well.

A worked example. Suppose the knowledge base contains "everyone who is rich is happy" and "John is rich":

C1:  ¬Rich(x) ∨ Happy(x)
C2:  Rich(John)

Resolve Happy? No — pick the complementary pair:
   ℓ1 = ¬Rich(x)   from C1
   m1 =  Rich(John) from C2
   UNIFY(Rich(x), Rich(John)) = θ = {x/John}

Resolvent = SUBST(θ, Happy(x)) = Happy(John)

A richer case, showing why leftovers must be substituted too:

C1:  ¬Animal(y) ∨ Loves(Jack, y)            ("Jack loves all animals")
C2:  ¬Loves(Jack, z) ∨ ¬Kills(z, Jack)      ("whoever Jack loves does not kill Jack")

UNIFY(Loves(Jack, y), Loves(Jack, z)) = {z/y}

Resolvent = ¬Animal(y) ∨ ¬Kills(y, Jack)
            ("no animal kills Jack")

Standardizing apart is mandatory before each resolution step, for the same reason as before. If two clauses both happen to use the variable x, unification may fail spuriously — or, worse, succeed with bindings that illegitimately tie together two independently quantified variables, producing an unsound resolvent. Consider resolving P(x) ∨ Q(x) with ¬P(A) ∨ R(x): without renaming, θ = {x/A} would be applied to the R(x) literal as well, yielding Q(A) ∨ R(A) and wrongly discarding the more general conclusion. After renaming the second clause's variable to x9, we obtain the correct resolvent Q(A) ∨ R(x9).

Factoring handles a subtlety that has no propositional counterpart. Substitution can make two literals of the same clause become identical; since a clause is logically a set of literals, duplicates must be collapsed. The factoring rule says: if two or more literals of the same sign in one clause unify with MGU θ, the clause may be replaced by its factor, obtained by applying θ and merging the now-identical literals.

Clause:  P(x) ∨ P(F(y)) ∨ Q(y)
UNIFY(P(x), P(F(y))) = {x/F(y)}
Factor:  P(F(y)) ∨ Q(y)

Factoring is not an optional tidy-up: binary resolution without factoring is incomplete. The standard illustration is the unsatisfiable pair {P(x) ∨ P(y), ¬P(u) ∨ ¬P(v)}. Repeated binary resolution between them only ever produces clauses of the form P(a) ∨ ¬P(b), never the empty clause; factoring each clause first to P(x) and ¬P(u) lets a single resolution step close the proof.

Resolution refutation. Resolution is used not to derive the goal directly but to show that its negation is contradictory. To prove KB ⊨ α, the procedure is:

  • Form KB ∧ ¬α.
  • Convert the result to conjunctive normal form: eliminate ⇔ and ⇒, drive negations inward (¬∀x p becomes ∃x ¬p, and ¬∃x p becomes ∀x ¬p), standardize variables apart, Skolemize existentials (replacing ∃x by a fresh Skolem constant, or by a Skolem function of the enclosing universally quantified variables), drop the now-redundant universal quantifiers, and distribute ∨ over ∧.
  • Repeatedly resolve pairs of clauses, standardizing apart and factoring as required, adding each resolvent to the clause set.
  • If the empty clause □ — a disjunction of no literals, which is unsatisfiable — is derived, then KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable and therefore KB ⊨ α.

A compact end-to-end refutation, proving Evil(John) from the earlier knowledge base:

StepClauseJustification
1¬King(x) ∨ ¬Greedy(x) ∨ Evil(x)CNF of the rule
2King(John)Given fact
3Greedy(y)Given fact
4¬Evil(John)Negated query
5¬Greedy(John) ∨ Evil(John)Resolve 1 and 2, θ = {x/John}
6Evil(John)Resolve 5 and 3, θ = {y/John}
7□Resolve 6 and 4, θ = {}

Notice that the query variable bindings fall out of the proof for free: had the query been ∃q Evil(q), the negated query would be ¬Evil(q), and the substitution {q/John} accumulated during the refutation would be the answer.

First-order resolution is refutation-complete: if a set of clauses is unsatisfiable, resolution will eventually derive the empty clause. This result — essentially Robinson's 1965 theorem, resting on Herbrand's theorem plus the lifting lemma (any propositional resolution step on ground instances can be mirrored by a first-order resolution step on the general clauses, whose MGU is at least as general) — is the theoretical foundation of automated theorem proving. Two caveats temper it. First, refutation-completeness is weaker than generative completeness: resolution is not guaranteed to generate every entailed sentence, only to refute every unsatisfiable set. Second, "eventually" carries real weight: first-order entailment is only semi-decidable, so if KB ⊭ α the procedure may run forever without ever concluding anything. Practical provers therefore layer on strategies — unit preference, set-of-support, input resolution, subsumption deletion and term indexing — that prune the enormous space of possible resolvents while preserving completeness where possible.

Stepping back, the three layers of this topic fit together as a single idea. Substitutions give us a representation for "partial knowledge about which individuals a sentence is talking about." Unification gives us a deterministic, least-commitment way to compute the weakest such partial knowledge that makes two expressions match. And once we have that, every propositional inference rule has a first-order counterpart obtained by inserting a unification step: Modus Ponens becomes Generalized Modus Ponens, and propositional resolution becomes first-order resolution. The payoff is that the inference engine reasons with general rules directly, instantiating them only as far as a particular proof requires.

Propositional logic lets us reason about fixed, atomic facts, but first-order logic adds variables, predicates, functions, and quantifiers. That extra expressive power creates a new problem: before we can apply an inference rule, we have to decide whether two expressions that look different can be made identical. A substitution binds variables to terms, and unification is the procedure that finds a substitution making two expressions match. When such a substitution exists, the most general unifier (MGU) is the one that commits to as little as possible, leaving the result as flexible as it can be for later inference steps.

Unification matters in this course because it is the mechanism that "lifts" propositional inference rules to first-order logic. Instead of grounding a knowledge base by substituting every constant into every quantified sentence, which can blow up or even be infinite, generalized Modus Ponens applies a rule directly to matching facts using a unifier. The same idea turns propositional resolution into first-order resolution: two clauses resolve when a literal in one unifies with the negation of a literal in the other, and the MGU is applied to the resolvent. This is the engine behind logic programming languages like Prolog and behind practical first-order theorem provers.

As you watch, look for three things. First, how a substitution is written and applied to an expression. Second, how matching is carried out term by term, including the case where unification fails because of conflicting bindings or a constant clashing with a different constant. Third, how the resulting unifier gets applied to the conclusion of a rule so that the inference produces a correctly instantiated new fact.

Forward and Backward Chaining in First-Order Logic

▶

First-order logic gives us an expressive language for describing worlds, but expressiveness comes at a cost: general resolution theorem proving over full first-order logic is only semidecidable, and in practice the search space explodes. A great deal of useful knowledge, however, can be written in a highly restricted but still powerful form — the definite clause. For knowledge bases made only of definite clauses, two simple and complementary inference strategies become available. Forward chaining works from the facts towards whatever conclusions they support; backward chaining works from a goal backwards towards the facts that would establish it. These two strategies underpin production-rule systems, deductive databases, expert systems and logic programming languages such as Prolog. This topic develops both algorithms in detail, examines how they are made efficient in practice, and sets out the criteria an engineer uses to choose between them.

Definite clauses and the chaining setting

A literal is an atomic sentence or its negation. A clause is a disjunction of literals. A definite clause is a clause containing exactly one positive literal. Because of the logical equivalence

(¬P₁ ∨ ¬P₂ ∨ … ∨ ¬Pₙ ∨ Q)  ≡  (P₁ ∧ P₂ ∧ … ∧ Pₙ) ⇒ Q

a definite clause is almost always written in the far more readable implication form, with a conjunction of positive atoms as the body (or premise, or antecedent) and a single positive atom as the head (or conclusion, or consequent). For example:

King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x)
Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) ⇒ Sells(West, x, Nono)
Parent(x, y) ∧ Parent(y, z) ⇒ Grandparent(x, z)

A fact is simply a definite clause with an empty body — there are no premises to satisfy, so the head is asserted unconditionally. Greedy(John), King(John), Owns(Nono, M1) and American(West) are all facts. Logically, the fact Greedy(John) is the degenerate clause consisting of the single positive literal Greedy(John); the "empty conjunction" in the body is vacuously true.

All variables appearing in a definite clause are implicitly universally quantified over the whole clause. Thus King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x) is shorthand for ∀x (King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x)). Note where the quantifier sits: it scopes over the entire implication, not just the body. A consequence is that variable names are local to a clause — the x in one rule has nothing to do with the x in another — which is why implementations standardise apart (rename variables) before each unification attempt.

It is useful to place definite clauses within the wider family of clause forms:

Clause typePositive literalsExampleImplication reading
Fact (unit definite clause)exactly one, no negativesGreedy(John)True ⇒ Greedy(John)
Rule (definite clause)exactly one¬King(x) ∨ ¬Greedy(x) ∨ Evil(x)King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x)

Horn clauseat most oneeither of the two rows abovedefinite clauses plus goals

The restriction matters enormously. Because definite clauses admit no negated conclusions and no disjunctive conclusions, a definite-clause knowledge base can never express uncertainty about which of several things is true — and therefore it never requires case analysis or proof by contradiction. There is always a unique minimal model (the set of ground atoms derivable from the facts), and every entailed atomic sentence is derivable by repeated application of Generalized Modus Ponens:

  p₁′, p₂′, …, pₙ′ ,   (p₁ ∧ p₂ ∧ … ∧ pₙ ⇒ q)
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────   where θ = a substitution with pᵢ′θ = pᵢθ for all i
                  SUBST(θ, q)

Generalized Modus Ponens is a lifted inference rule: it performs in a single step what propositional inference would require after exhaustive ground instantiation of the rule. Inference over definite clauses is sound (nothing false is derived) and complete (every entailed atom is derived), and it avoids the branching and factoring machinery of full resolution. The practical payoff is large: entailment for propositional Horn clauses is decidable in linear time, and for function-free first-order definite clauses (Datalog) it is decidable in time polynomial in the number of ground facts.

This is not an exotic corner of logic. Enormous amounts of real knowledge fit the definite-clause pattern naturally:

  • Business/product rules: OrderValue(o, v) ∧ GreaterThan(v, 500) ∧ Customer(o, c) ∧ Gold(c) ⇒ FreeShipping(o).
  • Diagnostic rules: Fever(p) ∧ Rash(p) ∧ Unvaccinated(p) ⇒ SuspectMeasles(p), the shape used by classic expert systems such as MYCIN and R1/XCON.
  • Deductive database views: transitive closure rules like Edge(x, y) ⇒ Path(x, y) and Edge(x, z) ∧ Path(z, y) ⇒ Path(x, y).
  • Prolog programs: every pure Prolog clause head :- body. is literally a definite clause.

Throughout this topic we use a standard worked example. The knowledge base says that it is a crime for an American to sell weapons to a hostile nation; the country Nono has some missiles, all sold to it by Colonel West, who is American; missiles are weapons; and an enemy of America counts as hostile.

(1) American(x) ∧ Weapon(y) ∧ Sells(x, y, z) ∧ Hostile(z) ⇒ Criminal(x)
(2) Owns(Nono, M1)
(3) Missile(M1)
(4) Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) ⇒ Sells(West, x, Nono)
(5) Missile(x) ⇒ Weapon(x)
(6) Enemy(x, America) ⇒ Hostile(x)
(7) American(West)
(8) Enemy(Nono, America)

Query:  Criminal(West)?

Forward chaining: data-driven inference

Forward chaining is data-driven: it starts from what is known and pushes conclusions outwards. One iteration of the basic algorithm does the following:

  • For every rule p₁ ∧ … ∧ pₙ ⇒ q in the knowledge base, find every substitution θ such that each premise pᵢθ matches a fact already in the knowledge base. Finding such a θ is exactly the problem of unifying a conjunctive pattern against the fact base.
  • For each such θ, compute the instantiated conclusion qθ. If qθ is not already in the knowledge base (and is not a renaming of something already there), add it.
  • If the new conclusion unifies with the query, return the answer substitution.

Iterations repeat until a pass adds nothing new. At that moment the algorithm has reached a fixed point, and the set of facts present is the deductive closure of the original knowledge base — precisely the set of ground atoms entailed by it. Running the algorithm on the Colonel West knowledge base gives:

IterationRule firedSubstitutionNew fact added
1(5) Missile(x) ⇒ Weapon(x){x/M1}Weapon(M1)
1(4) Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono,x) ⇒ Sells(West,x,Nono){x/M1}Sells(West, M1, Nono)
1(6) Enemy(x, America) ⇒ Hostile(x){x/Nono}Hostile(Nono)
2(1) the crime rule{x/West, y/M1, z/Nono}Criminal(West)
3— none applicable ——fixed point reached

Notice that the crime rule could not fire in iteration 1, because Weapon(M1), Sells(West, M1, Nono) and Hostile(Nono) did not yet exist. Forward chaining naturally layers its derivations by "depth": each iteration computes all facts whose shortest proof has length equal to the iteration number. This makes it a breadth-first strategy in proof space.

Three formal properties are worth stating precisely:

  • Soundness. Every fact added is a conclusion of Generalized Modus Ponens applied to sentences in the knowledge base, and GMP is sound; therefore everything derived is entailed.
  • Completeness. For definite-clause knowledge bases, forward chaining derives every entailed ground atom. The argument runs via the fixed point: the set of facts at the fixed point, read as a Herbrand interpretation, satisfies every clause in the knowledge base (otherwise some rule would still be fireable), so it is a model; and because it is the minimal model, any ground atom true in all models must be in it.
  • Termination. If the knowledge base contains no function symbols — the Datalog case — then with k predicates of arity at most a and n constants, there are at most k·nᵃ distinct ground atoms. Since each iteration either adds a fact or halts, the process must terminate; the whole closure is computable in time polynomial in n (though exponential in a). With function symbols, termination is lost: from NatNum(0) and NatNum(x) ⇒ NatNum(S(x)), forward chaining will happily generate S(0), S(S(0)), S(S(S(0))), … forever. In that setting forward chaining is complete only in the weaker sense that any entailed fact appears after finitely many iterations.

The natural home of forward chaining is an agent that receives a stream of percepts and must keep its picture of the world up to date. A plant-monitoring agent that is told TemperatureHigh(Reactor3) should immediately conclude whatever that implies — Alarm(Reactor3), NotifyOperator(Shift7) — without waiting to be asked. Similarly, a fraud-detection system asserting a new transaction wants every matching pattern rule to fire straight away. Because the consequences are already materialised, answering a query afterwards is a lookup rather than a search.

The corresponding weakness is irrelevance. Forward chaining does not know what you want. Given a geography knowledge base with rules about borders, populations, currencies and climates, asking "is West a criminal?" would, under naive forward chaining, still derive thousands of facts about rainfall. In the deductive-database literature this is the motivation for goal-directed rewriting (magic sets, discussed below). A second cost is the raw matching work: deciding whether a rule with n premises matches the fact base is equivalent to conjunctive query evaluation, which is NP-hard in the size of the rule — one reason why premise ordering and indexing matter so much.

Backward chaining: goal-driven inference

Backward chaining inverts the direction of travel. It starts with the goal and asks: what would have to be true for this goal to hold? The recursive definition is compact:

  • A goal q succeeds with substitution θ if q unifies with a known fact under θ.
  • Otherwise, q succeeds if there is some rule p₁ ∧ … ∧ pₙ ⇒ q′ (with variables renamed afresh) such that q unifies with q′ under θ, and each of the subgoals p₁θ, …, pₙθ can in turn be proved, with substitutions composed along the way.
  • A conjunction of goals succeeds if the first succeeds with θ₁ and the rest, after applying θ₁, succeed with θ₂; the overall answer is the composition θ₁ ∘ θ₂.

This produces an AND–OR tree. The OR-branching is the choice of which clause to use for a goal; the AND-branching is the requirement to solve all premises of the chosen clause. Standard implementations explore it depth-first with backtracking. Here is the trace for Criminal(West):

Criminal(West)                                    [rule 1, θ = {x/West}]
 ├── American(West)                                 fact (7)                        ✓
 ├── Weapon(y)                                     [rule 5, θ = {x′/y}]
 │     └── Missile(y)                               fact (3) ⇒ θ = {y/M1}           ✓
 ├── Sells(West, M1, z)                            [rule 4, θ = {x″/M1, z/Nono}]
 │     ├── Missile(M1)                              fact (3)                        ✓
 │     └── Owns(Nono, M1)                           fact (2)                        ✓
 └── Hostile(Nono)                                 [rule 6, θ = {x‴/Nono}]
       └── Enemy(Nono, America)                     fact (8)                        ✓

Answer: yes, with bindings {x/West, y/M1, z/Nono}

Several points deserve emphasis. First, substitution propagation: solving Weapon(y) bound y to M1, and that binding was carried into the next conjunct, which therefore became the much more constrained goal Sells(West, M1, z) rather than the open Sells(West, y, z). Composing substitutions as subgoals are solved is what turns backward chaining into a mechanism for computing answers, not merely checking yes/no. A query such as Criminal(p) with a free variable returns the binding {p/West}, and on backtracking would return further solutions if any existed.

Second, memory behaviour. Because the search is depth-first, at any instant the algorithm need only hold the current branch of the proof tree — the stack of open subgoals plus their choice points. Space is therefore linear in the size of the proof, not in the size of the knowledge base or of the deductive closure. This is precisely the opposite of forward chaining, which must materialise the whole closure in memory.

Third, relevance. Only clauses whose head unifies with some goal are ever examined. If the knowledge base also contained ten thousand facts about rainfall, backward chaining would never look at them, because no rainfall predicate ever appears as a subgoal of Criminal(West). For large knowledge bases with narrowly focused questions, this is a decisive advantage.

The price is paid in completeness and redundancy:

  • Infinite loops. Given the rules Path(x, y) ⇐ Edge(x, y) and Path(x, y) ⇐ Path(x, z) ∧ Edge(z, y), a depth-first prover that tries the recursive clause first will expand Path(x,y) into Path(x,z₁) into Path(x,z₂) … and never return. Depth-first search is not complete on infinite trees, so backward chaining is incomplete as a proof procedure even though the definite-clause fragment itself is decidable in the Datalog case. Remedies include iterative deepening, ordering the non-recursive clause first, cycle detection, or tabling (SLG resolution, as in XSB Prolog), which records goals already being solved.
  • Repeated work. The same subgoal may be re-proved many times in different branches. Computing Fibonacci numbers in the naive declarative way causes exponentially many redundant subgoal solutions. Memoisation/tabling converts this to polynomial time at the cost of storing the answer tables — effectively a hybrid that borrows forward chaining's bookkeeping.

Efficiency considerations and pattern matching

Both algorithms spend the overwhelming majority of their time matching rule premises against facts. Five techniques dominate practical systems.

1. Conjunct ordering. The order in which the premises of a rule are tested does not change the logical result, but it can change the amount of work by orders of magnitude. The heuristic is to test the most constrained conjunct first — the one expected to yield the fewest matches — which is the inference-engine analogue of the minimum-remaining-values heuristic in constraint satisfaction. Consider:

Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) ⇒ Sells(West, x, Nono)

If the knowledge base holds 100,000 missiles but Nono owns only four objects, testing Missile(x) first generates 100,000 candidate bindings, each then checked against Owns. Testing Owns(Nono, x) first generates four. A selectivity-aware engine keeps cardinality statistics per predicate and per argument position and reorders accordingly — exactly as a relational query optimiser chooses a join order. Finding the optimal ordering is NP-hard in general, so heuristics are used.

2. Incremental forward chaining. Naive forward chaining re-examines every rule against the entire fact base on every iteration, re-deriving facts it already has. The key observation is that a rule can only produce a new conclusion on iteration k if at least one of its premises was matched by a fact added on iteration k−1. The incremental version therefore maintains an agenda of newly added facts and, for each one, considers only rules containing a premise that unifies with it, and only matches in which that premise is bound to the new fact. This is the standard semi-naive evaluation strategy of deductive databases and typically reduces the work from quadratic to near-linear in the number of derivations.

3. The rete algorithm. Production systems such as OPS5, CLIPS, Jess and Drools take incrementality much further. Rete compiles all rule premises into a single dataflow network in which common prefixes of premise patterns are shared between rules. The network has two main kinds of node:

  • Alpha nodes perform single-pattern tests — "is this fact a Missile?", "is argument 1 equal to Nono?" — and feed matching facts into alpha memories.
  • Beta (join) nodes combine two streams of partial matches, checking consistency of shared variable bindings, and store the resulting partial matches in beta memories.

When a fact is asserted, it is pushed through the network and only the affected partial matches are updated; when a fact is retracted, the corresponding partial matches are removed. Because partial matches are cached rather than recomputed, and because two rules that share the premises Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) share the same join node, the cost of each update is proportional to the change rather than to the size of the rule set. Rete trades memory for time — the beta memories can be large — and the classic rule of thumb is that it pays off when the fact base changes slowly relative to its size, which is exactly the situation in monitoring and business-rule applications. Variants such as Rete II, TREAT and LEAPS adjust that memory/time balance differently.

4. Indexing. Retrieving the facts that could unify with a pattern must not require scanning the whole knowledge base. The minimum useful scheme is predicate indexing: a hash table from predicate symbol (and often arity) to the list of its facts, so that Owns(Nono, x) only touches Owns facts. Beyond that, argument indexing builds a table keyed on constants appearing in particular positions, so Owns(Nono, x) goes straight to the four facts whose first argument is Nono. Prolog systems commonly use first-argument indexing, which is why experienced Prolog programmers arrange the discriminating argument first. For very large or highly structured knowledge bases one can index on a subsumption lattice of patterns, though the lattice itself grows exponentially with the number of arguments, so engineers index selectively on the positions that queries actually constrain.

5. Magic sets. This technique gives forward chaining a dose of goal-directedness, and is the standard answer to the irrelevance problem in deductive databases. The idea is to rewrite the rule set so that each rule carries an extra "magic" premise representing the fact that the corresponding subgoal is actually relevant to the query. Suppose the query is Criminal(West). The rewriting adds a seed fact and filters:

Magic_Criminal(West).                                        -- seed from the query

Magic_Criminal(x) ∧ American(x) ∧ Weapon(y)
      ∧ Sells(x, y, z) ∧ Hostile(z)  ⇒  Criminal(x)

Now the crime rule cannot fire for any individual other than West, because no Magic_Criminal fact licenses it. Further rules propagate magic predicates to the subgoals ("if Criminal(x) is relevant and American(x) holds, then Sells(x, …) is relevant"), mirroring exactly the bindings that backward chaining would have passed down the proof tree. The result is a forward-chaining computation that derives only facts relevant to the query, while retaining forward chaining's set-at-a-time evaluation, its automatic sharing of repeated subcomputations, and its guaranteed termination on Datalog — advantages that depth-first backward chaining lacks. Magic-set rewriting is thus best understood as an automatic compilation of goal-directedness into a bottom-up engine.

Logic programming and Prolog

Prolog is the most widely used embodiment of backward chaining. A Prolog program is a definite-clause knowledge base, written with :- for "if", commas for conjunction, uppercase initial letters for variables, and a terminating period:

criminal(X) :- american(X), weapon(Y), sells(X, Y, Z), hostile(Z).
weapon(X)   :- missile(X).
sells(west, X, nono) :- missile(X), owns(nono, X).
hostile(X)  :- enemy(X, america).

american(west).
missile(m1).
owns(nono, m1).
enemy(nono, america).

?- criminal(X).
X = west.

The execution model is fixed and deliberately predictable: clauses are tried in the order they appear in the program file, and the premises of a clause are solved left to right, with chronological backtracking when a subgoal fails. This is depth-first search of the AND–OR tree with a fixed, declarative-looking but operationally meaningful ordering. The combination is known as SLD resolution. Because the ordering is part of the language definition, the programmer can reason about control as well as about logic — Kowalski's slogan "algorithm = logic + control".

Several pragmatic departures from pure first-order logic follow:

  • No occur check. Sound unification must refuse to bind a variable X to a term containing X, since no finite term satisfies X = f(X). The test costs time linear in the size of the term, so standard Prolog omits it. The consequence is that the query ?- X = f(X). succeeds, creating a cyclic term, and in rare cases unsound conclusions can be drawn. Most systems provide unify_with_occurs_check/2 for when correctness matters; in everyday programming the unsoundness almost never bites.
  • Negation as failure. Prolog's \+ Goal succeeds precisely when the interpreter fails to prove Goal. This is not logical negation; it is an inference under the closed-world assumption, that anything not derivable is false. Under CWA, \+ enemy(france, america) succeeds merely because no such fact or rule exists. The reading is sound for ground, finitely-failing goals in stratified programs, but it is non-monotonic — adding facts can retract previously derivable conclusions — and it interacts badly with unbound variables, so \+ p(X) should generally only be called when X is already bound.
  • Explicit control. Because search is depth-first, clause order can be the difference between an answer and an infinite loop. A left-recursive rule such as path(X,Y) :- path(X,Z), edge(Z,Y). placed before its base case will not terminate; written after, and with the recursion on the right, it usually will. The cut operator ! commits the interpreter to the choices made so far in the current clause, discarding alternative clauses and alternative solutions to goals to its left. Cuts are used to express "if-then-else" (max(X,Y,X) :- X >= Y, !.), to prune provably useless branches for efficiency (a "green" cut), and sometimes to patch logic that would otherwise be wrong (a "red" cut, which changes the program's declarative meaning and is a frequent source of bugs). Other impure facilities — assert/retract, findall, arithmetic via is — similarly trade declarative purity for practicality.

Prolog implementations are fast: programs are compiled to the Warren Abstract Machine, an instruction set designed around unification and backtracking, and good systems achieve millions of logical inferences per second with first-argument indexing, last-call optimisation and clause compilation. Typical application areas include:

  • Natural-language parsing, where definite clause grammars (DCGs) compile grammar rules directly into Prolog clauses and parsing is simply proof search.
  • Expert systems and configuration, where rules mirror domain expertise and explanations can be read off the proof tree.
  • Deductive databases, where Datalog — Prolog without function symbols or impure features — supports recursive views with guaranteed termination; modern descendants include Soufflé and LogicBlox, used for program analysis and business analytics.
  • Automated reasoning, scheduling, constraint solving and theorem proving, often via constraint logic programming (CLP), which replaces plain unification with constraint propagation over domains such as finite integers or reals.

Choosing between forward and backward chaining

Since both algorithms are sound and complete for definite clauses, the choice is an engineering decision about computational cost and usage pattern, not about what can in principle be proved. The deciding question is where the "fan-out" lies: does the knowledge base generate few consequences from many queries, or many consequences from few facts?

CriterionFavours forward chainingFavours backward chaining
Size of fact baseSmall; closure is affordable to materialiseLarge; only a small portion is relevant per query
Number and variety of queriesMany different queries, all answered by lookup once the closure existsFew, narrowly focused queries
Arrival of informationContinuous stream of percepts/events to react toInformation static; questions arrive instead
Response-time requirementPrecomputation up front, then constant-time answersNo precomputation cost; work done on demand
MemoryMust store the whole deductive closureLinear in proof depth
RiskDeriving masses of irrelevant factsNon-termination on loops; repeated subgoals
Typical systemsProduction systems (CLIPS, Drools), real-time monitoring and alarm agents, materialised database views, semantic-web RDFS/OWL reasonersProlog and logic programming, question-answering systems, goal-driven diagnosis, theorem provers

Concretely: a network operations centre with ten thousand incoming SNMP traps per minute and a fixed set of escalation rules should forward chain, because every event must be evaluated anyway and the operator expects alerts without asking for them. A legal-reasoning assistant sitting on a knowledge base of a million statutory facts, asked occasionally whether a particular contract clause is enforceable, should backward chain, because computing the closure of the statute book to answer one question would be absurd. A medical diagnosis system may sensibly do both: forward chain from newly entered observations to flag anything urgent, then backward chain from candidate diagnoses to decide which test to order next.

Hybrids are common and often the right answer. Magic sets give bottom-up engines goal direction; tabling gives top-down engines termination and sharing; rule engines frequently run a forward-chaining "recognise–act" cycle whose actions invoke backward-chained queries. The important conceptual point is that forward and backward chaining are two traversal strategies over the same AND–OR derivation space defined by the definite clauses — one from the leaves up, one from the root down — and the clauses themselves remain a declarative specification independent of which direction you choose to walk them.

Many practical knowledge bases can be written as definite clauses: implications whose premises are conjunctions of positive literals and whose conclusion is a single positive literal (a plain fact is just a clause with no premises). Restricting ourselves to this form gives up some expressive power, but it buys a great deal: inference becomes sound, complete, and tractable enough to run on large rule sets, and the whole process reduces to repeated applications of Modus Ponens with unification.

There are two natural directions to apply those rules. Forward chaining is data-driven: start from the known facts, fire every rule whose premises are satisfied, add the new conclusions, and repeat until the query appears or nothing new can be derived. Backward chaining is goal-driven: start from the query, find rules that could conclude it, and recursively try to prove their premises, backtracking when a branch fails. The two directions derive the same conclusions but do very different amounts of work depending on whether the knowledge base has many facts and few relevant rules, or a single focused question. Backward chaining with depth-first search is also the execution model behind Prolog and logic programming generally.

While watching, pay attention to three things: how unification lets a general rule match specific facts (the pattern-matching step that dominates the cost of inference), how the inference engine avoids redoing work or looping forever, and how control constructs let a programmer influence the search. These are exactly the issues you will weigh when deciding which chaining strategy fits a given task.

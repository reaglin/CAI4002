Introduction

Knowledge engineering is the practical craft behind first-order logic: taking a real domain, such as electronic circuits, family relationships, or a shopping world, and turning it into a knowledge base that an inference engine can reason over. The standard process runs in roughly six steps: identify the task and the questions the system must answer, gather the relevant domain knowledge, settle on a vocabulary of predicates, functions and constants (the ontology), encode the general rules of the domain as axioms, encode the specific problem instance as facts, pose queries and interpret the answers, and finally debug and refine when the answers are wrong or missing.

The hard part is usually not the logic notation but the design decisions. Should "the parent of x" be a function Parent(x) or a relation Parent(x,y)? Should a circuit gate's type be a constant, a predicate, or a term? These choices determine how naturally the axioms can be written and how efficiently queries can be answered, and they are exactly the choices that make knowledge bases easy or painful to debug later.

The videos below support this topic from two directions. The Stanford lectures show how inference actually consumes your axioms and facts, using first-order modus ponens with unification and first-order resolution, so you can see why a badly chosen vocabulary or a missing quantifier makes a query fail. The description logics lecture shows an alternative, more restricted representation language designed specifically so that knowledge about concepts and roles can be modeled and checked reliably. While watching, keep asking: what vocabulary did they commit to, what would a query look like, and what would go wrong if an axiom were omitted?

About this video

This Stanford CS221 lecture covers first-order modus ponens, the inference rule that fires a universally quantified implication against known facts. It is likely to show how substitution and unification match a general rule to specific constants, and how forward inference derives new facts from a knowledge base of definite clauses.

Why this video

It directly illustrates the payoff of Steps 3 and 4: once general axioms and a specific problem instance are encoded, this is the machinery that turns them into answers, and it makes the role of quantifiers and variable bindings concrete.

About this video

The follow-on CS221 lecture introduces first-order resolution, the general proof method that works on clauses rather than just definite clauses. Expect coverage of how first-order sentences are prepared for resolution and how unification is used when resolving complementary literals to answer a query by refutation.

Why this video

Posing a query and interpreting the answer (Step 5) depends on knowing what the prover can and cannot do; this video, from the same well-regarded Stanford course, completes the inference picture beyond modus ponens.

About this video

Prof. Deepak Khemani's NPTEL lecture introduces description logics, a family of decidable fragments of first-order logic built around concepts, roles and individuals. It is likely to cover how terminological knowledge (the general vocabulary and concept definitions) is separated from assertions about specific individuals, and why this restricted language trades expressiveness for tractable reasoning.

Why this video

The concept/role/individual split maps closely onto Step 2 (choosing a vocabulary) and the general-axioms versus problem-instance distinction, giving students a disciplined, widely used alternative view of ontology design from an established academic course series.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos mainly reinforce MO4 and MO5. Choosing predicates, functions and constants and writing quantified axioms is the heart of translating natural language into first-order sentences (MO4), and the description logics lecture adds an ontology-oriented perspective on that same modeling step. The modus ponens lecture shows forward inference over definite clauses, supporting the comparison of chaining strategies in MO5, while the first-order resolution lecture extends the resolution refutation idea from the propositional setting of MO3 into first-order logic. Together they also clarify why a knowledge-based agent's TELL and ASK operations (MO1) behave the way they do once a knowledge base has been engineered.

Introduction

Many practical knowledge bases can be written as definite clauses: implications whose premises are conjunctions of positive literals and whose conclusion is a single positive literal (a plain fact is just a clause with no premises). Restricting ourselves to this form gives up some expressive power, but it buys a great deal: inference becomes sound, complete, and tractable enough to run on large rule sets, and the whole process reduces to repeated applications of Modus Ponens with unification.

There are two natural directions to apply those rules. Forward chaining is data-driven: start from the known facts, fire every rule whose premises are satisfied, add the new conclusions, and repeat until the query appears or nothing new can be derived. Backward chaining is goal-driven: start from the query, find rules that could conclude it, and recursively try to prove their premises, backtracking when a branch fails. The two directions derive the same conclusions but do very different amounts of work depending on whether the knowledge base has many facts and few relevant rules, or a single focused question. Backward chaining with depth-first search is also the execution model behind Prolog and logic programming generally.

While watching, pay attention to three things: how unification lets a general rule match specific facts (the pattern-matching step that dominates the cost of inference), how the inference engine avoids redoing work or looping forever, and how control constructs let a programmer influence the search. These are exactly the issues you will weigh when deciding which chaining strategy fits a given task.

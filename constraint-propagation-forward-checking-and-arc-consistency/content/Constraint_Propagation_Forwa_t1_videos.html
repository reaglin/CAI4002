Introduction

A constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) is a particular way of describing a problem: instead of a single opaque goal test, you state a set of variables, the values each variable may take (its domain), and constraints that say which combinations of values are allowed. Map colouring, Sudoku, timetabling, and the N-queens puzzle are all natural CSPs. Because the structure of the problem is exposed, a solver can do more than blindly try assignments — it can reason about what the constraints imply before committing to a choice.

That reasoning is called inference, or constraint propagation. When you assign a value to one variable, the constraints often rule out values in neighbouring variables' domains; removing those values is domain reduction, and doing it systematically is what we mean by enforcing local consistency. The practical payoff is that propagation detects dead ends early, so backtracking search explores far fewer nodes. This topic sets up the vocabulary and intuition you will need before we study forward checking and AC-3 in detail, including how propagation is interleaved with search and how shrunken domains feed variable- and value-ordering heuristics such as minimum remaining values.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: how a problem is formally specified as variables, domains and constraints; where inference happens relative to the search tree; and what information a reduced domain gives you about which variable to try next. Keep a small example (a map or a 4x4 queens board) on paper and track the domains yourself as the speaker works through the problem.

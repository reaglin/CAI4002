Introduction

So far in this module you have worked with systematic search: backtracking that extends a partial assignment one variable at a time, with forward checking or AC-3 pruning the domains along the way. Local search takes the opposite stance. It begins with a complete assignment in which every variable already has a value — typically one that violates many constraints — and then repairs it, changing one variable at a time until no constraint is broken. This is called a complete-state formulation, and the standard repair rule is the min-conflicts heuristic: pick a variable involved in a conflict and give it the value that leaves the fewest conflicts remaining.

The method is simple, but its behaviour is interesting. Greedy repair can stall in local minima and on plateaus where every move looks equally good, so practical solvers add sideways moves, random restarts, random walk steps, or constraint weighting to escape. Min-conflicts solves n-queens for very large n in roughly constant time regardless of n, which is why local search dominates on large, loosely constrained problems and in online repair settings such as rescheduling an observatory or an airline timetable after a disruption. The trade-off is that it is incomplete: it cannot prove that a problem is unsatisfiable, and it offers no guarantee of termination.

The videos below approach this from two directions. Watch the Stanford lectures for the formal picture — how a CSP is defined, what a complete assignment and a cost or weight function look like, and how iterated local search moves through the space of full assignments. Watch the queens explainer for a concrete, visual problem on which you can hand-trace both systematic backtracking and min-conflicts repair, then ask yourself where each method spends its effort and how you would combine the two by running propagation on the repaired assignment.

About this video

A short explainer that uses the 4-queens version of the classic Eight Queens Problem, introduced by Max Bezzel in 1848, to illustrate constraint satisfaction. It walks through placing queens so that none attack each other and frames the puzzle in CSP terms with variables, domains and constraints.

Why this video

N-queens is the standard benchmark for the min-conflicts heuristic, and the small 4-queens board makes it feasible to trace conflict counts by hand before reasoning about why the same repair method scales to a million queens.

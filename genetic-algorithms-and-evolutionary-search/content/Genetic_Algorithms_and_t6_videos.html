Introduction

So far this module has treated the pieces of a genetic algorithm separately: representation, fitness, selection, crossover, and mutation. This topic assembles them into a working generational loop and asks the practical questions that follow. How large should the population be? What crossover and mutation rates give a sensible balance between exploring new regions of the search space and refining what has already been found? When should the run stop — after a fixed generation budget, when fitness stops improving, or when the population has collapsed onto near-identical individuals?

These choices matter because a genetic algorithm rarely fails loudly. It usually fails quietly, by converging prematurely on a mediocre solution while still reporting steady-looking fitness numbers. Diagnosing that requires looking beyond the best-fitness curve at measures of population diversity, and comparing behaviour across repeated runs rather than trusting a single lucky execution. Benchmarking — running on standard test functions or problem instances and reporting mean, best, and variance over many runs — is how claims about an algorithm are made credible.

The videos below approach these ideas from three angles: diversity measurement in population-based metaheuristics, a complete worked walkthrough of one such algorithm's iteration loop, and a tool-oriented look at automated parameter tuning. While watching, pay attention to what is being measured each generation, how parameters enter the update rules, and what evidence is used to claim that an algorithm has converged. Note that two of the videos use metaheuristics other than the classical GA; the structural lessons about loops, parameters, and diversity transfer directly, but map them back to selection, crossover, and mutation as you watch.

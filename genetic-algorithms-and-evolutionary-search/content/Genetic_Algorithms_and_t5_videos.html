Introduction

Crossover and mutation are the variation operators that actually generate new candidate solutions in a genetic algorithm. Selection decides which individuals get to reproduce, but it is crossover and mutation that determine what the next generation looks like. Crossover recombines material from two parents in the hope of merging useful partial solutions, or "building blocks", while mutation makes small random changes that reintroduce lost genetic material and keep the population from collapsing onto a single solution too early.

The operators you can use depend entirely on how you encoded the chromosome. Binary strings invite single-point, multi-point, and uniform crossover with bit-flip mutation; real-valued vectors suit arithmetic or blend crossover with Gaussian perturbation; permutation encodings, such as a tour in the travelling salesman problem, break under naive crossover because they would produce invalid solutions with repeated or missing cities, so order-based operators (order crossover, PMX, cycle crossover) and swap or inversion mutations are used instead. Behind all of these choices sits the same trade-off: too much disruption and the search becomes random, too little and the population converges prematurely.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: exactly where the cut points or exchange decisions are made in each crossover scheme, how the operator is adapted so that the offspring remain valid for the chosen encoding, and what role the mutation rate plays in maintaining diversity. Try to map each worked example back onto the generational cycle you have already seen, and ask yourself what would happen if the mutation rate were set to zero or to one.

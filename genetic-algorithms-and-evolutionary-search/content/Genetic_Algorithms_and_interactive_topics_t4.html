Selection Strategies

▶

Selection is the stage of the evolutionary cycle where the algorithm decides who gets to reproduce and who survives. Crossover and mutation generate novelty, but they are blind: they have no idea which regions of the search space are promising. Selection is the only component that reads the fitness function and steers the population. For that reason, the selection scheme is usually the single most influential design decision in a genetic algorithm (GA) — two runs identical in every respect except for their selection operator can behave completely differently, one converging crisply on a good optimum and the other either stagnating in a local optimum within ten generations or wandering aimlessly for thousands.

Two distinct selection events occur in a typical generational loop. Parent selection (or mating selection) chooses which individuals contribute genetic material to the offspring. Survivor selection (or replacement) decides which individuals from the combined pool of parents and offspring form the next generation. Some algorithms apply pressure almost entirely at the parent stage (for example, classic generational GAs with fitness-proportionate selection and full replacement), while others apply almost none at the parent stage and all of it at the survivor stage (for example, the (μ + λ) evolution strategy, which selects parents uniformly at random but keeps only the best μ of the combined pool).

Selective pressure is the name given to the strength of the bias that selection applies toward fitter individuals. It is an intuitive notion that can be measured precisely in several ways:

  • Selection intensity: the increase in mean population fitness caused by one selection step, expressed in units of the population's fitness standard deviation.
  • Takeover time: the number of generations required for the single best individual to fill the entire population when only selection operates (no crossover, no mutation). Short takeover times mean high pressure.
  • Reproduction rate or expected number of offspring of the best individual in one generation.

High selective pressure accelerates convergence but narrows the search — the population quickly becomes a cloud of near-copies around whatever looked best early on, which is frequently a local optimum. Low selective pressure preserves variety and keeps many regions under investigation, but progress is slow and, in the limit of zero pressure, the GA degenerates into a random walk. Note also that selection is normally performed with replacement: a single outstanding individual can be chosen as a parent many times in the same generation, which is precisely the mechanism by which good genetic material is amplified — and also the mechanism by which diversity is destroyed if the bias is too strong.

A crucial conceptual point is that selection does not create anything new. It only changes the distribution of existing genetic material. If an allele (a particular gene value) is eliminated from every individual in the population, selection can never bring it back; only mutation, immigration or restart can. This asymmetry is why the long-term behaviour of a GA depends so heavily on how aggressively selection prunes the population in the early generations.

Roulette wheel (fitness-proportionate) selection is the classical scheme described in Holland's original work. Imagine a roulette wheel whose circumference is divided into slices, one per individual, each slice sized in proportion to that individual's fitness. Spinning the wheel once selects one parent. Formally, the probability of selecting individual i is:

p_i = f_i / Σ_j f_j         (for j = 1 .. N)

Implementation is straightforward: compute the cumulative fitness array, draw a uniform random number r in [0, F_total), and walk the array until the cumulative sum exceeds r.

function roulette_select(population):
    total = sum(f(ind) for ind in population)
    r     = uniform(0, total)
    acc   = 0
    for ind in population:
        acc = acc + f(ind)
        if acc > r:
            return ind
    return population[last]       # guards against floating-point drift

The method has well-known weaknesses. First, it requires strictly positive fitness values, because a negative slice makes no geometric sense. A minimisation problem must therefore be transformed, for example with f = C - cost (where C exceeds the worst cost seen) or f = 1 / (1 + cost). Both transformations silently alter the relative spacing of fitness values and hence the selective pressure, which is an easy source of unintended behaviour.

Second, the scheme is dominated by the scale of fitness, not by rank. Consider a population of five individuals:

In the early-run column, individual A is a super-individual: it takes over 60% of the wheel and will be chosen roughly three times in five draws. Within a handful of generations the population consists almost entirely of A's descendants and the search collapses. In the late-run column the opposite pathology appears: fitness values have converged, every slice is nearly identical, and roulette selection degenerates into uniform random selection. The GA loses its ability to discriminate exactly when fine discrimination matters most.

Fitness scaling is the traditional remedy, applied as a pre-processing step before the wheel is built:

  • Linear scaling: f' = a·f + b, with a and b chosen so that the scaled average equals the raw average and the scaled maximum equals C × average, where C is typically 1.2–2.0. This caps the best individual's expected offspring at C. Care is needed to clip negative scaled values near the end of a run.
  • Sigma (σ) truncation scaling: f' = max(0, f − (f̄ − c·σ)), with c around 2. Individuals more than c standard deviations below the mean get zero probability; the rest are measured relative to the population's own spread, so the scheme adapts automatically as variance shrinks.
  • Power law scaling: f' = f^k. With k > 1 pressure increases; with k < 1 it decreases. Varying k over the run gives a simple annealing schedule.
  • Stochastic Universal Sampling (SUS) is a related improvement that keeps proportionate probabilities but reduces sampling noise: instead of N independent spins, one spin places N equally spaced pointers around the wheel, so each individual receives a number of copies within one of its expected value.

Rank-based selection sidesteps the scaling problem entirely by discarding fitness magnitudes and using only the ordering of individuals. The population is sorted, each individual receives a rank, and selection probability is a function of rank alone. A super-individual that is a thousand times better than the runner-up receives exactly the same probability as one that is marginally better, so outliers cannot dominate the wheel. Equally important, when fitness values converge late in the run, the ranks remain fully distinct and selective pressure stays constant rather than evaporating.

The standard linear ranking formula assigns, for rank i running from 1 (worst) to N (best):

p_i = (1/N) · [ η⁻ + (η⁺ − η⁻) · (i − 1) / (N − 1) ]
with  1 ≤ η⁺ ≤ 2   and   η⁻ = 2 − η⁺

Here η⁺ is the expected number of offspring of the best individual and η⁻ that of the worst. Setting η⁺ = 2 gives maximum linear pressure (the worst individual gets probability zero); η⁺ = 1 gives uniform random selection. For a population of 10 with η⁺ = 2, the best individual gets 20% of the probability mass, the median around 10%, and the worst 0% — a predictable, bounded spread that is independent of the raw fitness landscape.

Exponential ranking uses probabilities proportional to c^(N−i) with 0 < c < 1, or to 1 − e^(−i) normalised. This produces a much steeper curve, concentrating probability on the top few ranks, and is useful when the population is large and only the elite tail is of interest. The costs of ranking are modest but real: an O(N log N) sort every generation, and the deliberate loss of information about how much better one individual is than another — which can slow progress when a genuinely outstanding solution deserves rapid amplification.

Tournament selection is today the most widely used scheme in practice. To select one parent, draw k individuals uniformly at random from the population (usually with replacement) and return the fittest of them. Repeat until enough parents have been chosen.

function tournament_select(population, k):
    best = random_individual(population)
    repeat (k - 1) times:
        challenger = random_individual(population)
        if fitter(challenger, best):
            best = challenger
    return best

The tournament size k is a single, intuitive knob for selective pressure. With k = 1 selection is purely random. With k = 2 (binary tournament) the best individual is expected to produce about two offspring — roughly equivalent to linear ranking with η⁺ = 2. Larger k sharpens the bias quickly; the takeover time is approximately ln(N) / ln(k) generations. The probability that an individual of rank i (1 = worst) wins a tournament is (i^k − (i−1)^k) / N^k, showing how probability mass piles up at the top as k grows.

Note the structural consequence: individuals outside the top portion of the population have a vanishing chance of ever winning a tournament against k−1 random opponents, so for large k the bottom of the population is effectively dead weight — present in memory but never contributing genes.

Tournament selection has three practical advantages. It needs no global sorting and no global sum, only pairwise comparisons, so it costs O(k) per selection and parallelises trivially: each worker node can run tournaments on its own local subpopulation with no synchronisation barrier. It works with any comparable fitness measure, including negative values, raw minimisation costs, lexicographic multi-criteria comparisons, and even noisy or Pareto-dominance comparisons, with no transformation required. And k can be varied during a run: starting with k = 2 for broad exploration and ramping to k = 5 or higher in later generations implements a deliberate exploration-to-exploitation schedule. A finer-grained variant, probabilistic tournament, returns the better competitor with probability p (say 0.75) and the worse with probability 1 − p, allowing continuous tuning between k = 1 and k = 2 pressure.

Elitism and survivor selection govern what happens after offspring are produced. In a pure generational GA the entire parent population is discarded and replaced by offspring. Because crossover and mutation are disruptive, the best offspring may be worse than the best parent — so the best-so-far fitness curve can go down from one generation to the next, and an excellent solution discovered in generation 40 may simply be lost. Elitism fixes this by copying the top e individuals unchanged into the next generation. With e ≥ 1, the best fitness in the population is monotonically non-decreasing, which also makes convergence proofs and experimental plots far cleaner.

Elitism must be used sparingly. If e is a large fraction of the population — say 20% of a population of 50 — the surviving elite are repeatedly re-selected as parents, their children resemble them, and within a few generations the population is dominated by copies of two or three genotypes. The effective population size collapses and the GA becomes a slightly noisy hill-climber. A common guideline is to keep one or two elites, or 1–5% of the population, and to combine elitism with a duplicate filter that refuses to admit genotypes already present.

Steady-state GAs deserve particular mention. Because a new offspring enters the population immediately, its genes become available for mating in the very next operation rather than after a whole generation — this makes the effective pressure higher than the parent-selection scheme alone would suggest. Replace-worst is the most aggressive variant; replace-random and replace-oldest are gentler; deterministic crowding pairs each child with its most similar parent and keeps the better of the two, which preserves distinct niches almost for free.

Balancing exploration and exploitation is the unifying theme behind all of these mechanisms. Exploration means sampling widely across the search space to discover new basins of attraction; exploitation means refining what has already been found. Too much exploitation (large k, heavy elitism, aggressive scaling) collapses the population onto a single region; once every individual is nearly identical, crossover produces clones and only mutation can generate novelty, so the GA grinds down to an inefficient random mutation hill-climber. Too much exploration (tiny k, no elitism, high mutation) means good building blocks are never consolidated and the algorithm wastes its evaluation budget on essentially random sampling.

Practical designs therefore schedule the balance over time, mirroring simulated annealing's cooling schedule: begin with low pressure so the population spreads across the landscape, then raise pressure (increase k, increase the scaling exponent, reduce mutation) so that the final generations polish the best region found. Complementary diversity-preserving techniques work in parallel:

  • Fitness sharing divides each individual's fitness by the number of neighbours within a niche radius σ_share: f'_i = f_i / Σ_j sh(d_ij), where sh(d) = 1 − (d/σ_share)^α for d < σ_share and 0 otherwise. Crowded peaks are therefore devalued and the population spreads across multiple optima.
  • Crowding and restricted tournament selection achieve a similar effect through replacement rules based on genotypic similarity rather than fitness modification.
  • Island (multi-deme) models split the population into semi-isolated subpopulations, each running its own selection locally and occasionally exchanging migrants. Each island may converge, but different islands converge to different optima, so global diversity persists. Migration rate and topology become the knobs controlling how quickly good solutions propagate — a low migration rate is effectively a global diversity-preservation mechanism.

Premature convergence is the failure mode that results when these balances go wrong: the population loses diversity and settles on a sub-optimal solution long before the evaluation budget is exhausted. Its signature is unmistakable in diagnostic plots — the gap between best fitness and mean fitness shrinks to near zero, the fitness variance collapses, the average pairwise Hamming distance falls, and the best-so-far curve flattens into a long horizontal line.

The common causes map directly onto the mechanisms described above:

  • A super-individual under fitness-proportionate selection, monopolising the wheel in the first few generations.
  • Small populations, where genetic drift alone eliminates alleles rapidly even without strong selection; the expected time for an allele to be lost by drift scales with population size, so a population of 20 can go uniform in a handful of generations.
  • Overly aggressive elitism or (μ + λ) truncation, which floods the gene pool with copies of a few genotypes.
  • Very large tournament sizes relative to the population, which make the bottom 80% of individuals genetically irrelevant.
  • Low mutation rates combined with any of the above, leaving no mechanism to restore lost alleles.
  • A deceptive or badly scaled fitness function whose steepest gradient points toward a local optimum.

The remedies fall into three families. The first is to reduce the dominance effect: apply sigma or linear fitness scaling so no individual can expect more than about two offspring, or abandon proportionate selection altogether in favour of rank-based or tournament selection, both of which are invariant to fitness outliers. The second is to reintroduce lost genetic material: raise the mutation rate (sometimes adaptively, triggered when a diversity metric drops below a threshold), inject random immigrants — a small percentage of fresh random individuals each generation, replacing the worst — or perform a partial restart that keeps the elite and re-randomises the remainder. The third is to spread the population out structurally: fitness sharing penalises individuals crowded into the same niche; crowding-based replacement keeps distinct niches alive; island models maintain parallel, independently converging subpopulations.

Because corrective action is far more effective before the population has gone uniform than after, diversity should be monitored every generation rather than inferred at the end. Useful, cheap metrics include:

A practical control loop ties these together. For example: compute mean pairwise Hamming distance each generation; if it falls below 10% of the chromosome length while the stagnation counter exceeds 20 generations, halve the tournament size, double the mutation rate and replace the worst 10% of the population with random immigrants, then restore the original settings once diversity recovers. This kind of feedback-driven adaptation is far more robust than fixing a single selection scheme and hoping its pressure suits every phase of the run.

Finally, a worked illustration of why the scheme matters. Consider a 50-city travelling salesman problem with a population of 100, run for 500 generations. With fitness-proportionate selection on f = 1/tour_length, the fitness values of all tours are numerically very close (every random tour has a length within a narrow band), so the wheel is nearly uniform and the GA barely improves — the method degenerates toward random selection from the start. Switch to binary tournament selection, which is invariant to that compression, and the same algorithm converges steadily. Add one elite and the best-so-far curve becomes monotone; raise the tournament size to 7 and convergence is faster but the final tours are typically worse because the population lost its diversity by generation 80. That trade-off — faster versus better — is the whole subject of selection strategy in one experiment, and it is why these comparisons must be judged over repeated runs rather than a single lucky seed.

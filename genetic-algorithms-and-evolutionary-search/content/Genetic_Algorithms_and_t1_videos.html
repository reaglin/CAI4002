Introduction

Evolutionary computation is a family of search and optimisation methods that borrow ideas from biological evolution: a population of candidate solutions is repeatedly evaluated, selected, recombined, and mutated so that better solutions accumulate over time. Instead of reasoning about a problem analytically or following a single path through a search space, an evolutionary algorithm maintains many candidate answers at once and lets competition between them do the work of refinement.

This matters in an introductory AI course because it gives you a second, very different way to think about search. Earlier methods such as hill climbing or beam search track one state (or a few) and move locally; evolutionary methods sample broadly, recombine partial solutions, and are often applied where the objective function is noisy, discontinuous, or only available as a black box. Along the way you will need the standard vocabulary — individual, chromosome, gene, population, generation, fitness — because every later topic in this module builds on it.

As you watch the videos below, keep a few questions in mind. Can you name each stage of the generational cycle and say what it contributes? Where does exploration (searching widely) come from, and where does exploitation (refining what already works) come from? What signals that a run should stop, and what kinds of problems would make you reach for an evolutionary method rather than a classical algorithm? Try to map the terminology each presenter uses onto the terms used in your lecture notes, since small differences in wording are common.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos concentrate on MO1: each presents the generational cycle, so you see selection, crossover, mutation, and evaluation described more than once and in different styles, which helps the sequence stick. The worked example and visual guide also give early, informal exposure to how candidate solutions are encoded and scored, preparing the ground for the later, more rigorous treatment of representations (MO2) and fitness function design (MO3). They are introductory rather than comparative, so they do not settle questions about selective pressure (MO4) or convergence diagnostics across repeated runs (MO5); treat them as the conceptual foundation on which those later topics, readings, and lab exercises will build.

Introduction

So far in this module you have studied the machinery of evolutionary search: populations, chromosome representations, selection, crossover, mutation, and fitness evaluation. This topic asks a different question — what happens when that machinery meets a real problem? Job-shop scheduling, university timetabling, resource allocation, structural and parametric engineering design, and classic combinatorial problems such as travelling salesman or knapsack are the places where genetic algorithms earned their reputation, largely because these problems have huge discrete search spaces, awkward constraints, and no reliable exact method at realistic sizes.

The recurring design decisions are worth naming before you watch anything. How do you encode a schedule or a design so that crossover produces something meaningful? How do you express hard constraints (no two exams in the same room at the same time; a beam must not fail) versus soft preferences (avoid back-to-back classes) inside a single fitness value — by penalty, by repair, or by a representation that cannot express an illegal solution? And how do you know the result is good, given that a GA gives you no optimality guarantee and a different random seed gives a different answer?

The three videos below approach this from three directions: a coded class-scheduling system, a survey of constrained engineering optimisation, and a design-tool demonstration of an evolutionary solver. While watching, keep a running list of (a) what the chromosome is, (b) how constraints are handled, (c) what the fitness function rewards, and (d) what evidence, if any, is offered that the result is actually good. Also notice where other methods appear alongside the GA — hill climbing in the first video, alternative metaheuristics in the second — since comparing GAs with simpler or different search methods is a core part of this topic.

About this video

A walkthrough of a working class-scheduling prototype built in Java with an SQLite backend, where the scheduling engine uses both a genetic algorithm and hill climbing. The video covers the surrounding framework as well as the search itself, so you see how a timetabling problem is set up, stored, and solved rather than just the algorithm in isolation. An accompanying Substack article is linked for readers who want the written detail and code.

Why this video

It is the closest match to the timetabling and resource allocation subtopic, and because it pairs a GA with hill climbing in the same system it directly supports the comparison-with-alternative-methods theme. Seeing the database and framework around the solver is also a useful dose of realism for the practical deployment discussion.

About this video

This video works through constrained engineering optimisation problems solved with metaheuristic algorithms, the standard benchmark family (pressure vessel, spring, beam-type design problems) used to test evolutionary and swarm methods. Expect coverage of how the design variables and constraints are formulated and how a metaheuristic is applied to them, with chaptered sections for navigation.

Why this video

It maps onto the engineering design optimisation subtopic and foregrounds constraint handling, which is exactly the issue your fitness function must solve through penalties or repair. Positioning the GA within a wider family of metaheuristics also gives you vocabulary for comparing methods.

Video 3: View Optimization Using Galapagos For Grasshopper - Intro

ThinkParametric · 8:41 · 2,461 views · 5y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

An introduction to Galapagos, the evolutionary solver built into Grasshopper for Rhino, applied here to a view-optimisation design problem. It demonstrates how a designer defines the variable parameters (the genome) and a single objective to maximise or minimise (the fitness), then lets the solver search the parameter space. This is the opening lesson of a longer ThinkParametric course on the same tool.

Why this video

It shows evolutionary search as an everyday design tool rather than as code, which is a useful contrast and a good illustration of why real-valued parameter encodings suit design problems. Watching the solver run also gives you an intuitive feel for convergence and for the trade-off between search time and solution quality.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these case studies take the abstract generational cycle of MO1 and show it running on real problems, with the Galapagos demonstration making selection, variation, and evaluation visible as the population improves over generations. The scheduling prototype is the best illustration of MO2 and MO5: a timetable needs a permutation- or integer-style encoding, and the presence of hill climbing alongside the GA invites you to judge solution quality by comparison rather than by assuming the GA result is optimal. The constrained engineering optimisation video speaks most directly to MO3, since every benchmark problem there forces a decision about how violated constraints are penalised inside the fitness value. None of the three dwells on selection operators in detail, so for MO4 you should carry the roulette-wheel, rank, and tournament comparison from the earlier lecture material and ask, for each case study, how much selective pressure the problem can tolerate before diversity collapses.

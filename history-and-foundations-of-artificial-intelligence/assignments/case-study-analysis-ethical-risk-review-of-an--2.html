Case Study Analysis: Ethical Risk Review of an AI Deployment

Module: History and Foundations  |  Topic: Ethical Considerations in AI Practice
Estimated time on task: 2–4 hours  |  Weight: 100 points

In one sentence: You will read a short case describing a proposed real-world AI system, identify and evaluate its most serious ethical risks, and recommend specific, governance-grounded changes that a responsible development team should make before launch.

Learning Outcomes for This Assignment

By successfully completing this assignment, you will be able to:

  1. Analyze a proposed AI deployment to identify where bias, opacity, privacy loss, accountability gaps, or workforce disruption arise, and trace each risk to a specific decision in the system's data, model, or deployment design.
  2. Evaluate the severity of the identified risks using stated criteria (likelihood, magnitude of harm, reversibility, and distribution of harm across affected groups).
  3. Apply at least one published responsible-AI governance framework or principle set to the case, mapping concrete case facts to specific framework requirements.
  4. Construct a prioritized set of mitigation recommendations, each justified by evidence from the case and at least one credible external source.

Purpose: Why This Assignment Matters

The history of artificial intelligence is not only a history of technical breakthroughs and funding cycles — it is also a history of systems released into the world before anyone asked who could be harmed by them. Hiring screeners that penalized women's résumés, facial recognition tools with sharply unequal error rates across skin tones, credit models that could not explain their own decisions, and automation programs that reshaped entire job categories all reached deployment because ethical review happened late, informally, or not at all.

Practitioners today are increasingly expected to perform this review as part of ordinary engineering and product work. Whether you become a data scientist, product manager, policy analyst, clinician, auditor, or informed citizen commenting on a public procurement, you will be asked some version of the question this assignment rehearses: What could go wrong with this system, for whom, and what should we do about it before we ship?

The deliverable you produce here — a short structured ethical risk review — mirrors an artifact used in real organizations (variously called an algorithmic impact assessment, model card annex, or responsible-AI review memo). Practicing it on a low-stakes case builds the habit of looking past accuracy metrics to ask about fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, and human consequences.

Key Terms (use these definitions)

Bias (in an AI system)
Systematic error or disparate performance that produces worse outcomes for some groups than others. Sources include unrepresentative training data, proxy variables that encode protected characteristics, historical patterns of discrimination captured in labels, and mismatch between the deployment population and the development population.
Fairness
A normative standard for how outcomes should be distributed across individuals or groups. Note that multiple formal definitions exist (e.g., equal error rates across groups versus equal treatment of similar individuals), and they can conflict — part of your task is to state which notion you are applying and why.
Transparency
The degree to which a system's purpose, data, logic, limitations, and performance are disclosed and intelligible to the people affected by it and to those overseeing it. Includes explainability (can an individual decision be accounted for?) and disclosure (do people know an AI system is being used at all?).
Privacy
Control over the collection, use, retention, inference, and sharing of personal information. Includes risks from data the system infers about people, not only data it was given.
Accountability
The assignment of identifiable responsibility for a system's outcomes, plus the mechanisms — logging, audit, appeal, redress, human review — that make that responsibility actionable when something goes wrong.
Workforce impact
Effects of the deployment on the people who do, supervise, or lose the work being automated or augmented: task displacement, deskilling, intensified monitoring, shifted liability, or changed job quality.
Governance framework
A published, structured set of principles, requirements, or processes for developing and operating AI responsibly. Examples you may use include the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the OECD AI Principles, the EU AI Act's risk-tier approach, UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, IEEE Ethically Aligned Design, or a documented organizational responsible-AI policy.

Verbs defined for this assignment

  • Analyze = break the case into its components (data, model, decision, users, affected parties, oversight) and show how a specific component produces a specific risk. A list of worries is not an analysis; a causal chain from design choice to harm is.
  • Evaluate = judge severity against stated criteria and defend the judgment. Say how bad, for whom, and on what basis.
  • Apply a framework = quote or paraphrase a specific provision of a named framework and connect it to a specific fact in the case. Naming the framework is not applying it.
  • Recommend = propose a concrete, implementable change (who does what, at which stage), not a general aspiration such as "be more transparent."

The Case

Read the case below carefully. All details are fictional but are composites of documented real deployments.

Case: "TriageAssist" at Northvale Regional Health Network

Northvale Regional Health Network operates eleven clinics and two hospitals serving a mixed urban and rural population of roughly 600,000 people. Emergency department wait times have risen sharply, and the network's board has approved a pilot of TriageAssist, a machine-learning system purchased from an external vendor, Callis Analytics.

What the system does. When a patient checks in at the emergency department, TriageAssist produces an "acuity score" from 1 (most urgent) to 5 (least urgent). The score is displayed to the triage nurse alongside the nurse's own assessment. Patients are then placed in the queue according to the final acuity level recorded by the nurse. Northvale's operations director has set a performance target: nurses should agree with the TriageAssist score in at least 85% of cases, and disagreements must be documented in a free-text field.

How it was built. Callis Analytics trained the model on 4.2 million historical emergency visits from a consortium of eighteen hospital systems, predominantly large urban academic medical centers in three states. The training label is "resource intensity" — a composite of total billed charges, number of diagnostic tests ordered, and length of stay — used as a proxy for how sick the patient was. Input features include age, vital signs, chief complaint text, prior visit count within the network, insurance type, home ZIP code, and preferred language.

What is known about performance. Callis reports an overall AUC of 0.88 on a held-out test set. The vendor's documentation does not report performance broken out by patient race, ethnicity, language, insurance status, or rural/urban residence, and Callis considers the feature weights and model architecture proprietary trade secrets. When Northvale's data team asked for subgroup performance figures, Callis offered to run an internal analysis under a non-disclosure agreement but declined to release the underlying results to the hospital's clinical quality committee.

Data handling. Under the contract, de-identified patient records from Northvale flow back to Callis monthly to support "continuous model improvement," and Callis retains the right to use aggregated insights in products sold to other clients. Patients are not separately informed that an algorithmic tool contributes to their triage; the network's general notice of privacy practices, posted in the waiting area and on the website, mentions "quality improvement technologies."

Staffing plan. Northvale's finance office has projected that TriageAssist will let the network reduce triage nursing hours by 15% across the pilot sites within eighteen months. The nurses' union has not been consulted. Several senior triage nurses have told their manager privately that they worry about being blamed if they override a score and the patient deteriorates, and that they are unsure whether their documented disagreements will be used in performance evaluations.

Oversight. The pilot is sponsored by the Chief Operating Officer. No clinical governance body has been assigned formal responsibility for monitoring TriageAssist outcomes. There is currently no process for a patient to learn that the tool was involved in their care, to contest a triage outcome, or to request human-only review.

The Task

Produce a structured ethical risk review of TriageAssist addressed to Northvale's Chief Operating Officer and clinical quality committee. Your review must contain the six sections below, in order, with the given headings.

Section 1 — Case Summary and Scope (about 150 words)

In your own words, state what the system does, who makes the final decision, who is affected, and which one or two decision points in the workflow carry the greatest ethical weight. Do not restate the whole case; identify what matters.

Section 2 — Risk Identification (table)

Produce a table identifying at least four distinct ethical risks, drawn from at least three of the six concern areas (bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, workforce impact). For each risk, give:

  1. A short risk name.
  2. The concern area it belongs to.
  3. The specific case fact that causes it (quote or cite the detail from the case).
  4. Who is harmed, and how.

Hint: Strong responses look hard at the training label, the training population, the 85% agreement target, the proprietary-model clause, the monthly data flow, and the absence of an appeal route.

Section 3 — Risk Evaluation and Prioritization (about 500–650 words)

Choose your three most serious risks from Section 2 and evaluate each against these four criteria:

  • Likelihood — how probable is the harm given the design as described?
  • Magnitude — how severe is the harm to an affected individual?
  • Reversibility — can the harm be detected and undone?
  • Distribution — is the harm concentrated on a group that is already disadvantaged?

Rank the three risks and defend the ranking. Where evidence in the case is incomplete, say explicitly what you are assuming and what information you would need to confirm it. Acknowledge at least one genuine tension or tradeoff (for example, between throughput gains and equity, or between vendor trade-secret protection and auditability).

Section 4 — Governance Framework Application (about 300–400 words)

Select one published governance framework or principle set. Identify three specific provisions, functions, or principles from it and map each to a concrete fact in the TriageAssist case, stating whether the deployment currently meets, partially meets, or fails that provision. Cite the framework properly.

Section 5 — Recommendations (about 350–450 words)

Give four to six prioritized recommendations. Each must specify:

  • The action, stated concretely enough that someone could schedule it;
  • Who is responsible (Northvale, Callis, the clinical quality committee, the union, a regulator);
  • When it must happen (before pilot launch / during pilot / ongoing);
  • The risk from Section 3 it addresses;
  • One anticipated obstacle or cost.

At least one recommendation must address contractual or procurement terms, and at least one must address the people who do the work.

Section 6 — Reflection (about 200–250 words)

Answer in the first person:

  • How did you decide which risks were most serious? What did you notice first, and what did you only see on a second reading?
  • Where did you find the case genuinely ambiguous, and how did you resolve it?
  • What would change your mind about your top-ranked risk?
  • What was the hardest part of this analysis for you, and what would you do differently next time?

Parameters and Constraints

  • Length: 1,400–1,900 words total for the prose sections (Sections 1, 3, 4, 5, 6). The Section 2 table is excluded from the word count, as are the reference list and headings.
  • Sources: Cite a minimum of three credible external sources, at least one of which must be the governance framework used in Section 4, and at least one of which must be peer-reviewed or an authoritative institutional publication. Course readings count toward the total. Blogs and vendor marketing pages do not count toward the minimum, though they may be cited additionally.
  • Citation style: APA 7th edition, in-text citations plus a reference list. Consistent use of another discipline-standard style (IEEE, Chicago) is acceptable if you note your choice at the top.
  • Headings: Use the six section headings exactly as given.
  • Voice: Professional memo register. You may use first person in Section 6 and where you state assumptions elsewhere.
  • Outside research on the case: Northvale and Callis are fictional; do not invent additional facts about them. You may and should bring in real-world evidence about analogous systems (for example, published findings on healthcare cost proxies or clinical risk scores).
  • Submission: Submit through the course site. Your instructor or the learning management system will specify the accepted file format — follow that instruction.

Policy on Generative AI Use

Permitted with disclosure. You may use generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, and similar) for the following purposes:

  • Clarifying unfamiliar terms or concepts from the module;
  • Brainstorming an initial list of risks before you build your own;
  • Locating candidate governance frameworks to then read and verify yourself;
  • Proofreading, grammar, and tightening sentences you wrote.

Not permitted:

  • Generating your risk evaluation, ranking, framework mapping, or recommendations and submitting them as your analysis;
  • Producing the Section 6 reflection, which must be your own account of your own process;
  • Using AI-produced citations without independently locating and reading the source. Fabricated or unverified references will be treated as a sourcing failure and, if extensive, as an academic-integrity matter.

Disclosure requirement. If you use any AI tool, add a short "AI Use Statement" (two to four sentences) at the end of your submission naming the tool, what you used it for, and how you verified or changed its output. Honest disclosure carries no grade penalty. Undisclosed substantive use is handled under the institution's academic integrity policy.

A note on why: the skill being assessed here is your judgment about harm — which risk matters most, for whom, and what to do about it. That judgment is exactly what a general-purpose model cannot supply for a specific organization, and exactly what employers and review boards will expect from you.

Accessibility

  • Write in plain language. Short sentences and defined terms are rewarded, not penalized.
  • Use real heading styles rather than bold text so screen readers can navigate your document.
  • Build the Section 2 risk table as an actual table with a header row. If you prefer, you may present Section 2 as a clearly labeled list instead — this is fully acceptable and will not affect your grade.
  • Provide alternative text for any image, chart, or diagram you include.
  • Ensure adequate color contrast and do not rely on color alone to convey meaning (for example, label priority levels in words, not just red/yellow/green).
  • If you have an approved accommodation — including extended time, an alternative deliverable such as a recorded audio or video review with a transcript, or assistive-technology use — contact the instructor at least 72 hours before the deadline and it will be arranged. You do not need to disclose details of your circumstances.

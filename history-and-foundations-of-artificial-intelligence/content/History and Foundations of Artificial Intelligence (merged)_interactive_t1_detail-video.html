Defining Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence

1

Defining Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence

▶

Artificial intelligence is unusual among technical fields in that its central term resists definition. Chemistry does not need to settle what a molecule "really" is before it can proceed; AI, by contrast, has spent seven decades arguing about what counts as intelligent, and those arguments have shaped research agendas, funding decisions, benchmark design, and public expectation. This topic works through the main definitional positions, the operational tests that have been proposed to settle them, the distinction between narrow and general capability, and the technical vocabulary — agent, percept, performance measure, environment — that lets practitioners talk precisely about systems whose underlying philosophy may be contested. The aim is not to arrive at a single correct definition, but to be able to recognise which definition a given claim, benchmark, or product description is implicitly using.

What do we mean by intelligence? Psychologists, philosophers, and computer scientists have produced hundreds of definitions, and surveys of the literature (Legg and Hutter's 2007 collection is the best known) find that most cluster around a small number of recurring elements. The first is learning from experience: an intelligent system improves its behaviour as a result of exposure to data, feedback, or outcomes rather than requiring every contingency to be specified in advance. A chess program that plays the same way on its ten-thousandth game as on its first is, on this criterion, not learning, however strong it may be. The second is reasoning: drawing conclusions that were not explicitly stored, as when a system that knows "all mammals are warm-blooded" and "a whale is a mammal" concludes that whales are warm-blooded. The third is problem solving: finding a sequence of actions or transformations that converts a current state into a desired one, as in route planning, theorem proving, or scheduling. The fourth is adaptation to novelty: handling situations the designer never anticipated, which is what separates a thermostat from a household robot.

  • Goal-directed behaviour under uncertainty. A second family of definitions foregrounds purposive action in complex or imperfectly known environments. On this view intelligence is not a property of thought at all but of the relationship between an organism or machine and its world: an intelligent system is one that reliably achieves its objectives when the environment is noisy, incompletely observed, or adversarial. Legg and Hutter's own formal proposal — that intelligence is an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments, weighted by environment simplicity — belongs here. Note what this excludes: a calculator achieves its goal perfectly but in exactly one trivially simple environment, so it scores near zero.
  • Perception, language, and generalisation. A third family emphasises the capacities that seem distinctively hard to mechanise: extracting structure from raw sensory signals, using and understanding natural language, and transferring knowledge learned in one domain to another. A child who learns to stack wooden blocks and then applies the same stability intuitions to stacking groceries is generalising in a way that most machine learning systems, which degrade sharply when the data distribution shifts, do not. This family of definitions is why benchmarks such as the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus, which deliberately present tasks the system has never seen, are treated as more informative than benchmarks the system could have memorised.
  • Behavioural rather than structural definition. Crucially, almost every workable definition describes what a system does rather than what it is made of. Nothing in the definitions above mentions neurons, silicon, symbols, or probability distributions. This substrate-independence is deliberate and has a major consequence: radically different architectures can satisfy the same definition. A symbolic expert system encoding ten thousand hand-written rules about bacterial infections, a statistical model fitting coefficients to a million patient records, and a deep neural network with billions of parameters may all qualify as intelligent on a behavioural criterion despite having essentially nothing in common internally. It also means that the question "is it really thinking?" cannot be settled by opening the box.
  • Consequences of the lack of consensus. Because no abstract standard commands agreement, progress in AI is in practice measured by tasks accomplished: defeating a world champion at chess (1997) and at Go (2016), reaching human parity on conversational speech transcription, reducing the error rate on the ImageNet classification benchmark from 26% to under 3% between 2011 and 2017, attaining medal-level scores on olympiad problem sets. This task-based accounting is honest about what has been demonstrated, but it fragments the field: a system can be superhuman on one benchmark and incapable of the simplest common-sense inference immediately outside it. Keep this tension in mind — it recurs in the discussion of weak versus strong AI below.

Framing artificial intelligence: four classic approaches. Russell and Norvig's organising scheme crosses two distinctions. The first asks whether we are interested in thought processes and reasoning or in behaviour. The second asks whether success is measured against human performance or against an ideal standard of rationality — doing the "right thing" given what is known. Crossing these yields four quadrants, each with its own history, methods, and criteria for success.

  Measured against human performance Measured against rationality
Concerned with thought processes Thinking humanly — cognitive modelling; the system should reproduce the steps, errors, and timings of human reasoning. Validated by psychological experiment and brain imaging. Thinking rationally — the "laws of thought"; the system should reason according to correct logical inference. Validated by soundness and completeness proofs.
Concerned with behaviour Acting humanly — the Turing Test tradition; the system's observable behaviour should be indistinguishable from a person's. Validated by human judges. Acting rationally — the rational agent view; the system should select actions that best achieve its goals. Validated by performance measures on defined tasks.
  • Thinking humanly. Here the goal is a computational theory of the human mind, so a program is successful only if its internal processing resembles human processing. Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver (1957) was explicitly evaluated this way: they compared the program's trace of subgoals against the "think-aloud" protocols of human subjects solving the same puzzles, looking for step-by-step correspondence. This approach gave rise to cognitive science as an interdisciplinary field and to cognitive architectures such as SOAR and ACT-R, which are judged by whether they predict human reaction times and characteristic mistakes. Its distinctive demand is that a model which gets the right answer by the wrong route has failed.
  • Acting humanly. This approach sets aside the question of internal mechanism and asks only whether the external behaviour would be accepted as human. It is the tradition of the Turing Test, discussed in detail below, and of conversational agents from ELIZA (1966) onward. Its appeal is that it sidesteps intractable debates about what thought is; its weakness is that human behaviour includes a great deal that is irrational, slow, and error-prone, so imitating humans may mean imitating human failings. An arithmetic system built to act humanly would need to be deliberately slowed down and made to make carrying errors.
  • Thinking rationally. The logicist tradition traces to Aristotle's syllogisms and runs through Boole, Frege, and the development of first-order predicate logic. Its programme is to represent knowledge in a formal notation and derive consequences by provably correct inference rules — if the premises are true and the rules are sound, the conclusions must be true. Logic programming languages such as Prolog, automated theorem provers, and description logics underpinning ontologies (including the OWL standard used in the semantic web) are the engineering descendants. Two obstacles limited this approach as a complete account of AI: the knowledge bottleneck, since informal knowledge is extremely hard to state in formal terms, especially when it is uncertain; and the computational bottleneck, since even decidable fragments of logic can require resources that explode combinatorially with problem size.
  • Acting rationally. This view defines intelligence as doing whatever is expected to achieve one's goals best, given the information available. It is broader than thinking rationally: correct inference is one route to good action, but not the only one — reflexive withdrawal from a hot surface is rational action with no inference at all. It also tolerates uncertainty naturally through decision theory, where the agent maximises expected utility rather than requiring guaranteed correctness. Crucially, it is operational: once a performance measure is specified, two systems can be compared quantitatively.
  • Why the mainstream adopted the rational agent view. Modern AI textbooks, research programmes, and most industrial practice take acting rationally as the organising frame for three reasons. It is measurable: expected utility, regret, cumulative reward, and loss functions give numeric handles on success, whereas "does it really think?" does not. It is domain-independent: the same formalism describes a warehouse robot, an advertising bidder, and a protein-folding predictor. And it is general: logical inference, probabilistic reasoning, search, and learning all fit inside it as tools an agent may use. The main caveat, discussed under bounded rationality below, is that perfect rationality is computationally unachievable in realistic settings.

The Turing Test as an operational definition. In his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Alan Turing proposed replacing the unanswerable question "Can machines think?" with a concrete, decidable procedure. He called it the imitation game. In the standard formulation, a human interrogator sits in one room and communicates by text — Turing specified a teleprinter precisely so that voice, appearance, and handwriting could not give the game away — with two hidden participants, one a human and one a machine. The interrogator may ask anything at all, for as long as the protocol allows. The task is to determine which participant is the machine. The human participant tries to help the interrogator; the machine tries to be mistaken for the human.

  • The pass condition. If the interrogator cannot identify the machine reliably — that is, performs no better than chance, or better than when two humans are compared — the machine is said to pass. Turing's own prediction was that by the year 2000 a machine with roughly 109 bits of storage could fool an average interrogator at least 30% of the time in a five-minute conversation. Note two features of the design: it is comparative (the machine is measured against a human present in the same session, not against an absolute standard) and statistical (a single lucky session proves nothing).
  • Capabilities the test demands. An unrestricted text conversation is a remarkably demanding probe because the interrogator can set any task expressible in words. To pass, a system needs at minimum: natural language processing, to understand and produce fluent language; knowledge representation, to store what it is told and what it already knows; automated reasoning, to answer questions whose answers were never stored and to draw conclusions; and machine learning, to adapt to the pattern of the conversation and to extrapolate beyond its prior experience. An interrogator might simply say, "I am thinking of three objects: a bowling ball, a bag of feathers, and a sheet of paper. I drop them all from a window. Describe what happens to each." Answering well requires physical common sense that no lookup table could plausibly contain.
  • The Total Turing Test. Turing deliberately excluded physical interaction, arguing that the ability to pick up objects was irrelevant to intelligence. Later commentators proposed the Total Turing Test, in which the interrogator can also pass physical objects through a hatch and observe the subject's actions. This adds two further capability requirements: computer vision (and more broadly machine perception) to recognise objects and scenes, and robotics to manipulate them. The total version is a much higher bar — dexterous manipulation of unfamiliar deformable objects remains an open research problem — and it also addresses the objection that language alone can be gamed by a system with no grounding in the world it talks about.
  • Criticisms. The most persistent objection is that the test rewards imitation and deception rather than understanding. Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) convinced some users it understood them using a few dozen pattern-matching rules and the trick of turning statements into questions; PARRY (1972), simulating a paranoid patient, fooled a panel of psychiatrists partly because paranoid evasiveness conceals ignorance well. The successful strategies in the Loebner Prize competitions have historically involved typing errors, feigned moods, and conversational deflection — behaviours that make a system more human-seeming and no more capable. Searle's Chinese Room argument contends that symbol manipulation sufficient to pass the test could nonetheless involve no understanding whatever. A separate practical criticism is that the test is not diagnostic: a failure tells you nothing about which capability was lacking, which is why AI research has largely abandoned it in favour of decomposed benchmarks for translation, question answering, visual recognition, and reasoning. Finally, the test is anthropocentric: a system vastly more capable than a human at planning and inference would fail simply by being too fast, too accurate, and too knowledgeable.
  • Its enduring value. Despite these objections the test remains important as the clearest example of an operational definition: it converts a metaphysical question into a measurable procedure, and in doing so it maps out the capability agenda — language, knowledge, reasoning, learning, perception, action — that still structures the field.

Strong AI versus weak AI. The terminology comes from John Searle's 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs," and it draws a line not between levels of performance but between two philosophical claims about what a program achieves.

  • Weak AI (narrow AI). The weak claim is that machines can be made to act as if they were intelligent within specific, bounded tasks, and that such systems are valuable tools and useful models of cognition. The weak AI thesis makes no commitment about inner mental life. In contemporary usage "narrow AI" has largely displaced "weak AI" and shifted the emphasis from philosophy to scope: a narrow system performs well within the task distribution it was built and trained for, and its competence does not transfer. Note that "weak" does not mean "poor" — a weak-AI system can be radically superhuman. Stockfish plays chess far beyond any person; AlphaFold predicts protein structures at a level that reshaped structural biology. Neither can do anything else.
  • Strong AI. The strong claim is that an appropriately programmed computer would not merely simulate a mind but would have one — genuine understanding, intentionality, and in some formulations conscious experience. This is a claim about mental states, not about behaviour, and that is precisely why it cannot be settled by any behavioural test, including Turing's. Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment — a person who knows no Chinese manipulating Chinese symbols by rulebook well enough to produce perfect answers, yet understanding nothing — is an argument that syntax (symbol manipulation) is insufficient for semantics (meaning). The systems reply, the robot reply, and the brain-simulator reply are the standard counterarguments; the debate remains unresolved and is largely orthogonal to engineering practice.
  • What is actually deployed. Essentially every AI system in commercial or governmental use today is narrow. Web search ranks documents; neural machine translation maps token sequences between languages; recommender systems predict engagement from interaction histories; computer vision systems in radiology flag suspicious regions; fraud detection scores transactions; speech recognisers transcribe audio. Each has a defined input space, a defined output space, and a defined performance measure. Even large language models, whose breadth across text-expressible tasks is striking, remain narrow in the relevant sense: they operate on a fixed modality set, have no persistent goals or embodiment, and degrade unpredictably outside their training distribution. Describing any of these as "a mind" is a category error about which the strong/weak distinction is designed to warn us.
  • Artificial General Intelligence. AGI names the aspiration of human-level competence across arbitrary domains — a system that could learn any intellectual task a person can learn, transferring knowledge between domains and acquiring new skills without redesign. AGI is a claim about capability breadth and should be kept conceptually separate from strong AI, which is a claim about inner mental states. The two are logically independent: one can coherently imagine an AGI with no consciousness at all (sometimes called a "philosophical zombie" case), or a narrowly capable system that nonetheless had some form of experience. Conflating them is one of the most common errors in public discussion.
  • Why the distinction matters practically. It has direct consequences for ethics, law, and marketing. If a system merely processes signals, then responsibility for its outputs rests entirely with the people and organisations that specified, trained, and deployed it — a vendor cannot plead that "the AI decided." If a system genuinely had understanding and interests, questions of moral status and perhaps rights would arise, which is why strong-AI claims are not idle. In the meantime, language that implies understanding — "the system knows your preferences," "it understands your medical history" — can mislead users into over-trusting outputs produced by pattern extrapolation, a phenomenon well documented in automation bias research. Procurement documents, clinical guidance, and regulatory texts increasingly require vendors to state the bounded operating conditions of a system precisely because of this risk.

Rational agents and the working vocabulary. The rational agent framework supplies the shared technical language used throughout modern AI. Its appeal is that it is simple enough to state in a paragraph and general enough to describe any system that takes in information and does something.

  • Agent, sensors, actuators, percepts. An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon that environment through actuators. A single input is a percept; the complete history of everything the agent has perceived is the percept sequence. The agent function maps percept sequences to actions; the agent program is the concrete implementation of that function running on some architecture. For a self-driving car, sensors are cameras, lidar, radar, wheel encoders, and GPS; actuators are steering, throttle, and braking. For a spam filter, the sensor is the arriving message and the actuators are the file-to-inbox and file-to-spam operations. For a trading agent, percepts are price and order-book updates and actions are orders submitted.
  • Performance measure and rationality. A performance measure is an external criterion evaluating sequences of environment states — note "external" and "environment states," not the agent's own satisfaction with its behaviour. A rational agent is one that, for each possible percept sequence, selects the action expected to maximise its performance measure, given the evidence in the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge it has. Four points follow. Rationality is relative to the performance measure, so designing that measure is a design decision with ethical weight: a vacuum robot rewarded for dirt collected may learn to dump dirt and re-collect it; an engagement-maximising feed may learn that outrage holds attention. Rationality is not omniscience — an agent that takes a well-justified action which happens to turn out badly was still rational. Rationality is not perfection, which would require knowing the actual outcome rather than the expected one. And rationality requires information gathering and learning where these are available, because an agent that could reduce its uncertainty cheaply and chooses not to is not maximising expected performance.
  • Environment properties. Task environments are classified along dimensions that determine which techniques are appropriate:
Dimension Meaning Example contrast
Fully vs. partially observable Whether sensors give access to the complete state relevant to the decision Chess (fully observable) vs. poker or driving in fog (partially observable)
Deterministic vs. stochastic Whether the next state is fully determined by the current state and the action A sliding-tile puzzle vs. a robot wheel that sometimes slips
Static vs. dynamic Whether the environment can change while the agent is deliberating Crossword solving (static) vs. collision avoidance in traffic (dynamic)
Episodic vs. sequential Whether current actions affect future situations Classifying each X-ray independently vs. playing a game where early moves constrain later ones
Discrete vs. continuous Whether states, time, percepts, and actions take finitely many values Board games vs. steering angle and throttle position
Single-agent vs. multi-agent Whether other entities' objectives depend on the agent's behaviour Solving a maze vs. negotiating, bidding, or driving among other drivers
Known vs. unknown Whether the agent knows the rules governing outcomes of actions Playing a game with published rules vs. a robot exploring an unmapped site

The hardest real environments are partially observable, stochastic, dynamic, sequential, continuous, multi-agent, and partly unknown — autonomous driving is the canonical example, which is precisely why it has absorbed so much more effort than board games. Classifying an environment before choosing a technique is a practical discipline: a fully observable, deterministic, static, discrete, single-agent problem can be attacked with classical search; introduce stochasticity and you need Markov decision processes; remove full observability and you need belief states and partially observable MDPs; add other strategic agents and you need game-theoretic reasoning.

  • Bounded rationality. Perfect rationality — always computing the genuinely optimal action — is unachievable for any agent in a realistic environment, because the computation is intractable and because the world does not wait. Herbert Simon's response, for which he won the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics, was bounded rationality: real agents operate under limits of time, information, and processing capacity, and therefore satisfice — they search until they find an option that is good enough against an aspiration level, rather than exhaustively comparing all options. AI formalises this in several ways. Chess engines cut off search at a fixed depth and apply a heuristic evaluation rather than searching to checkmate. Anytime algorithms maintain a usable answer at all times and improve it if given more time, so a path planner interrupted after 50 milliseconds still returns a route. Metareasoning treats the allocation of computation as itself a decision problem: is further deliberation worth more than acting now? The practical standard is therefore bounded optimality — behaving as well as possible given the agent's actual computational resources — rather than ideal rationality.
  • Agent structures. The framework also gives a vocabulary for internal organisation, in increasing order of sophistication: simple reflex agents map the current percept directly to an action by condition–action rules (a thermostat, a reflexive emergency brake); model-based reflex agents maintain internal state representing the parts of the world they cannot currently see (a robot tracking a pedestrian who has passed behind a van); goal-based agents search or plan over possible futures to reach a specified goal state; utility-based agents compare outcomes on a continuous scale, trading off speed against fuel against passenger comfort; and learning agents add a performance element, a critic that supplies feedback, a learning element that modifies behaviour, and a problem generator that proposes exploratory actions.
  • Related technical terms. The remaining vocabulary names the techniques agents use. Search is the systematic exploration of a space of states or plans — breadth-first, A*, Monte Carlo tree search — and underpins route finding, scheduling, and game playing. Knowledge representation concerns how facts, relations, and constraints are encoded so they can be reasoned over: logical sentences, semantic networks, frames, ontologies, probabilistic graphical models. Automated reasoning derives new assertions from stored ones. Machine learning infers a function or model from data rather than from explicit programming, and divides into supervised learning (labelled examples), unsupervised learning (structure in unlabelled data), and reinforcement learning (behaviour shaped by reward signals from an environment — the paradigm that most directly instantiates the agent framework). Natural language processing, computer vision, and robotics name the perceptual and physical interfaces. An important point for the rest of the module: these are tools, not rival definitions of intelligence. A single deployed system routinely combines several — a warehouse robot may use vision for perception, a learned model for demand prediction, and search for path planning — which is why the agent vocabulary, rather than any one technique, provides the common frame.

Why definitions matter in practice. It would be easy to treat the definitional debate as philosophical ornament before the real technical content begins. It is not. Definitions have measurable downstream effects on what gets built, what gets funded, what users believe, and how risk is governed.

  • Benchmarks operationalise definitions and steer the field. A benchmark is a definition with a score attached: whoever specifies the test is implicitly asserting what counts as progress. ImageNet defined visual intelligence as assigning one of a thousand labels to a photograph, and in doing so concentrated a decade of research and investment on supervised classification; the consequences — a step change in deep learning, and also a generation of systems with no grasp of three-dimensional structure or causality — flow from that framing. Similarly, benchmarks such as GLUE, SuperGLUE, and MMLU defined language understanding as performance on multiple-choice and classification items, which rewarded systems adept at exploiting statistical regularities and surface cues in the data. When a benchmark saturates, the community must decide whether the underlying capability has been achieved or merely the test has been gamed, a tension captured by Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure. Funding agencies, national AI strategies, and corporate research budgets attach to these scoreboards, so benchmark design is research-agenda design.
  • Overclaiming misleads users and policymakers. Describing a statistical model as "understanding," "knowing," or "deciding" imports connotations of comprehension and accountability that the system cannot support. The practical consequences are documented: automation bias leads operators to accept machine recommendations they would have questioned from a colleague; clinicians may under-scrutinise a flagged scan; a court or caseworker may treat a risk score as objective because it is numerical. Overclaiming also distorts regulation in both directions — inviting rules aimed at science-fiction scenarios while neglecting the mundane failure modes (distribution shift, data quality, feedback loops) that actually cause harm. Precise definitional language is therefore a consumer-protection and governance issue, not pedantry.
  • The AI effect: goalposts that move. There is a recurring pattern, often called the AI effect and summarised in Larry Tesler's quip that "AI is whatever hasn't been done yet": once a capability is achieved, it is reclassified as mere computation. Optical character recognition, chess, route planning, spam filtering, speech transcription, and machine translation were all once paradigm AI problems; each is now described as an ordinary feature. This has two effects worth noticing. It makes the field look perpetually unsuccessful, because its successes are continually subtracted from its scope. And it obscures genuine progress when assessing risk, because capabilities absorbed into infrastructure stop being examined. Recognising the pattern is a reason to prefer explicit, stable criteria over the intuition that "real" intelligence is always one step beyond the state of the art.
  • Honest risk assessment needs the narrow/general boundary. A clear statement of a system's operating envelope — the inputs, populations, and conditions under which its performance was validated — is the foundation of responsible deployment. A dermatology classifier trained predominantly on light-skinned patients is a narrow system whose boundary must be stated, not a general "skin cancer detector." Documentation practices such as model cards and datasheets for datasets exist precisely to force this boundary into writing, and the risk-tiered structure of regulation such as the EU AI Act depends on being able to specify the intended purpose and context of a system. Vague breadth claims make risk assessment impossible, because an unbounded claim has no conditions under which it can be checked.
  • Shared vocabulary enables comparison across paradigms. Symbolic, statistical, and neural approaches have radically different internals, yet the agent vocabulary lets them be compared on common terms: what are the percepts, what are the actions, what is the performance measure, what environment properties are assumed, what resource bounds apply. This is what makes it possible to say that a rule-based expert system and a neural classifier are competing solutions to the same task environment, or to recognise that a hybrid architecture combining a learned perception module with a symbolic planner is coherent rather than confused. Without this shared frame, debates between paradigms become arguments about which internal mechanism deserves the honorific "intelligent" — exactly the unanswerable question Turing tried to replace.

Before we can study how artificial intelligence works, we need to be clear about what we are claiming a machine can do. "Intelligence" is a contested term even among psychologists and philosophers, and the field of AI has never settled on a single definition. Instead, researchers have proposed competing framings: systems that think like humans, act like humans, think rationally, or act rationally. These four classic approaches, together with Turing's imitation game, the weak/strong (narrow/general) distinction, and the rational agent framework, give us the working vocabulary used throughout the rest of this module.

Definitions are not just academic housekeeping. How you define intelligence determines what counts as success, what you measure, and what you can honestly claim in a product description, a research paper, or a policy debate. A system that passes a conversational test is not necessarily one that understands; a system that beats humans at one narrow task is not necessarily on a path to general intelligence. Keeping these distinctions straight is a practical skill.

The videos below do not lecture on philosophy; they give you the modern technical vocabulary that today's definitional debates are conducted in. As you watch, try to map each term you hear onto the framework from the readings: Is this capability about thinking or acting? Is it measured against human performance or against a standard of rationality? Would the system being described count as narrow or general intelligence, and on what evidence?

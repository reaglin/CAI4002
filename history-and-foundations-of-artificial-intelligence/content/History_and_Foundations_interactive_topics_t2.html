Origins and Early History of AI

▶

Artificial intelligence did not begin with computers. It began with a much older question: is reasoning something mysterious and uniquely human, or is it a process that follows rules and could therefore, in principle, be carried out by something other than a human mind? The history traced below is the story of that question being sharpened over roughly two and a half millennia — from Aristotle's syllogisms, through Boole's algebra of thought, to Turing's machines — until it became precise enough to be answered experimentally. By the middle of the twentieth century the question had a name, a research community, a funding base, working programs, and a set of spectacular failures that taught the field lessons it is still absorbing.

Reading this history matters for practical reasons, not merely antiquarian ones. The definitional disputes of the 1950s (is intelligence about thinking or about acting? about imitating humans or about being rational?) still structure how systems are evaluated today. The symbolic paradigm's strengths — explicit knowledge, explainable inference — are precisely what modern statistical systems lack and what regulators now demand. And the boom-and-bust cycle that followed the field's early over-promising is a pattern that has repeated often enough to be worth recognising in advance.

Philosophical and Mathematical Roots

The foundational insight of classical logic is that the validity of an argument depends on its form, not its content. Aristotle's Organon (fourth century BCE) catalogued syllogistic forms such as: all M are P; all S are M; therefore all S are P. Substitute anything you like for S, M and P — mammals, humans, mortals, or nonsense words — and if the premises are true the conclusion must be true. This separation of form from content is the germ of the whole computational idea. It means valid reasoning can be checked mechanically, by pattern-matching against a list of permitted forms, without understanding the subject matter at all. A clerk who knows nothing of biology can verify a biological argument's structure.

Medieval logicians elaborated the syllogistic apparatus, and in the thirteenth century Ramon Llull built the Ars Magna, a set of rotating paper discs inscribed with basic concepts that could be combined systematically to generate propositions. Llull's purpose was theological — he hoped to derive truths that would convert non-believers — but the mechanism is recognisably a combinatorial reasoning device. Leibniz, in the seventeenth century, pushed the ambition much further with two linked proposals: a characteristica universalis, a formal language in which any concept could be expressed unambiguously, and a calculus ratiocinator, a set of manipulation rules for that language. His famous hope was that disputants would one day say "Calculemus" — let us calculate — and settle their disagreement by computation rather than argument. Leibniz lacked the mathematics to deliver this, but he stated the AI research programme with startling clarity two centuries early.

That mathematics arrived in 1847 and 1854 with George Boole's The Mathematical Analysis of Logic and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought. Boole's move was to treat logical propositions as algebraic quantities taking the values 1 (true) and 0 (false), with operations corresponding to AND, OR and NOT. Logical inference then becomes algebraic manipulation. The distributive, commutative and associative laws hold in recognisable forms, and De Morgan's laws give systematic translations between conjunction and disjunction.

Boolean identities that let inference become calculation:

  NOT (A AND B)  =  (NOT A) OR  (NOT B)        De Morgan
  NOT (A OR  B)  =  (NOT A) AND (NOT B)        De Morgan
  A AND (B OR C) =  (A AND B) OR (A AND C)     distribution
  A OR  (A AND B)=  A                          absorption

Modus ponens as an algebraic check:
  "A implies B" is written  (NOT A) OR B
  Given A = 1 and (NOT A) OR B = 1, then 0 OR B = 1, so B = 1.

The significance is hard to overstate. Boole showed that the "laws of thought" could be written as equations, and equations can be solved by procedures. In 1937 Claude Shannon's master's thesis at MIT — often called the most influential master's thesis ever written — observed that Boolean algebra describes exactly the behaviour of switching circuits built from relays: a closed relay is 1, an open relay is 0, relays in series compute AND, relays in parallel compute OR. Logic thereby became implementable in metal. Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) meanwhile extended logic beyond Boole's propositional level to quantifiers and variables, giving first-order predicate logic — the representational language that symbolic AI would adopt wholesale.

In parallel ran a tradition of physical machines that carried out defined procedures. Wilhelm Schickard's calculating clock (1623), Pascal's Pascaline (1642) and Leibniz's stepped reckoner (1673) mechanised arithmetic. Jacquard's loom (1804) used punched cards to control weaving patterns, demonstrating that a machine's behaviour could be encoded on an interchangeable medium — the first separation of program from hardware. Charles Babbage's Difference Engine and, far more ambitiously, his Analytical Engine (designed from 1837) combined a "mill" (processor), a "store" (memory), punched-card input and conditional branching: a general-purpose computer in brass and steam, never completed. Ada Lovelace's 1843 notes on the Analytical Engine contain both the first published algorithm intended for a machine and a philosophical caution — the machine "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything; it can do whatever we know how to order it to perform" — which Turing would later name and rebut as "Lady Lovelace's objection". Elaborate automata such as Jacques de Vaucanson's digesting duck (1739) and the Jaquet-Droz writing boy (1774), a clockwork figure that wrote arbitrary programmed texts with a quill, made the same point theatrically: machinery can produce behaviour that looks purposive.

The final root is the twentieth-century formalisation of computability itself, which grew out of a crisis in the foundations of mathematics. David Hilbert had proposed a programme to place all mathematics on a complete, consistent, mechanically decidable axiomatic footing; his Entscheidungsproblem (decision problem) of 1928 asked for an effective procedure that, given any first-order sentence, would decide whether it was valid. The answers demolished the programme but created computer science.

Church's thesis (or the Church–Turing thesis) holds that these radically different formalisms — lambda calculus, Turing machines, Post systems, general recursive functions — all define exactly the same class of computable functions, and that this class captures everything we could intuitively mean by "effectively calculable". The convergence is the evidence. Its consequence for AI is direct: if any two universal systems compute the same functions, then whatever a brain computes, a computer can compute too — provided the brain's activity is itself an effective procedure, and provided we can discover what that procedure is. Those two provisos are where the entire subsequent argument lives.

Several other threads fed the same confluence around the 1940s. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts (1943) showed that networks of idealised binary neurons could compute any propositional logic function, connecting neurophysiology to Boole. Donald Hebb (1949) proposed that learning consists of strengthening connections between co-active neurons, giving a mechanism for adaptation. Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) analysed goal-directed behaviour in animals and machines in terms of feedback and control. Shannon's information theory (1948) made information a measurable quantity. Taken together, these threads supported a single startling proposition: thinking might be symbol manipulation — that mental states are structured representations, that thought is a rule-governed transformation of those representations, and that the physical substrate performing the transformations is irrelevant to the result. Newell and Simon would later state this formally as the physical symbol system hypothesis: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action.

Alan Turing and the Foundations of Machine Intelligence

Turing's 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" introduced what we now call the Turing machine. Its power lies in its poverty. The machine consists of an unbounded tape divided into cells, a read/write head positioned over one cell, a finite set of internal states, and a transition table. At each step it reads the symbol under the head and, according to its current state and that symbol, writes a symbol, moves the head one cell left or right, and enters a new state. That is all.

A Turing machine that increments a unary number (n ones) to n+1:

 STATE   READ   ->  WRITE   MOVE   NEXT STATE
 -----------------------------------------------
 scan     1     ->    1      R      scan
 scan     _     ->    1      R      halt

Tape before:  _ 1 1 1 _        (represents 3)
Tape after:   _ 1 1 1 1 _      (represents 4)

Turing's argument that this captures all effective computation was psychological rather than mathematical: he analysed what a human "computer" — the job title of a person doing calculation by hand — actually does. Such a person reads finitely many symbols, can distinguish only finitely many states of mind, attends to a bounded region of the paper at any moment, and follows fixed rules. Strip away the inessentials and you have the machine. The second key result was the universal Turing machine: a single machine U that takes as input an encoded description of any other machine M plus M's input, and simulates M. This is the theoretical charter for the stored-program computer and for AI: one physical device, reprogrammed, can exhibit any computable behaviour whatever. Turing then proved the halting problem undecidable — no machine can determine, for all machine-input pairs, whether the computation terminates — settling the Entscheidungsproblem negatively and establishing that there are precise limits to mechanism.

During the Second World War at Bletchley Park, Turing turned theory into operational practice. Building on Polish work by Marian Rejewski, he designed the Bombe, an electromechanical device that searched Enigma rotor configurations. The Bombe's method is instructive as early AI: it worked from a crib (a guessed plaintext fragment such as a routine weather report), propagated the logical consequences of a hypothesised rotor setting through a network of wired relays, and rejected settings the instant a contradiction appeared — a hardware implementation of constraint propagation and proof by contradiction, pruning an astronomically large search space. Turing also devised Banburismus, a sequential Bayesian scoring procedure using log-odds units he called "bans" to rank hypotheses about rotor order. Search with heuristic pruning, and probabilistic evidence accumulation, were thus both demonstrated on real problems of enormous consequence before the first electronic computer ran a program.

In 1950 Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in Mind, opening with "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" and immediately declaring the question "too meaningless to deserve discussion" in that raw form, because it depends on how we choose to define two contested words. He replaced it with an operational substitute, the Imitation Game. In the original version, an interrogator communicates by teleprinter with two hidden participants, a man and a woman, and must determine which is which while the man tries to deceive and the woman tries to help. Turing then asks: what happens if a machine takes the part of the man? The question "can machines think?" is replaced by "can a machine play this game as successfully as a man can?" The modern reading — a machine and a human, with the interrogator identifying which is the machine — is a simplification of Turing's staged formulation, but preserves its logic.

The design choices are deliberate and worth unpacking. The teleprinter channel deliberately excludes appearance and voice, so the test measures verbal behaviour rather than engineering of a lifelike body. The interrogator may ask anything — arithmetic, poetry criticism, chess, personal anecdotes — so no narrow competence suffices. And crucially the criterion is behavioural and comparative: the machine need not be perfect, only as hard to distinguish from a human as another human would be. Turing offered a rough prediction that by around the year 2000, a machine with about 109 bits of storage might fool an average interrogator at least 30% of the time in five minutes of questioning — a prediction about a modest, time-limited version of the test rather than about full human-level intelligence.

The paper's most impressive section anticipates and answers nine objections. Several remain live in contemporary debate.

The final section is arguably the most prophetic. Turing calculated the labour of hand-coding an adult mind and judged it infeasible. His alternative: "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of education one would obtain the adult brain." The child machine would have relatively little initial structure but a strong capacity to be modified. He then sketched the training regime in terms that read as an outline of machine learning: a system of rewards and punishments to reinforce useful behaviours and suppress others; a distinction between fixed rules and modifiable ones; the deliberate inclusion of a random element so that the search for good configurations does not get stuck, noting that random search can outperform systematic search when solutions are scattered; and an acceptance that the teacher will not know what is going on inside the pupil. He also concluded that an intelligent machine would need to take risks and make mistakes: "if a machine is expected to be infallible, it cannot also be intelligent." Turing was writing about reinforcement learning, stochastic search, and the opacity of learned systems in 1950.

The 1956 Dartmouth Conference

In the summer of 1955, John McCarthy — then a young mathematics assistant professor at Dartmouth College — drafted with three collaborators a proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation for a summer research project. Its opening sentence set the agenda for a discipline:

"We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be
 carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover,
 New Hampshire. The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture
 that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can
 in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to
 simulate it."

This is the first documented use of the term artificial intelligence. McCarthy chose it deliberately to mark out new territory: the obvious alternatives — "cybernetics" (associated with Norbert Wiener, whose intellectual gravity McCarthy wished to escape), "automata studies" (too narrow and too dry), "complex information processing" (Newell and Simon's preferred phrase) — each carried commitments he wanted to avoid. He later remarked that nobody liked the name much, but nobody proposed a better one. The label's very boldness helped: it announced an ambition rather than a technique, and it survived because it was memorable.

The four organisers brought complementary standing to the venture:

  • John McCarthy (Dartmouth, then MIT and Stanford) — logician and the project's driving force; would go on to invent LISP, propose time-sharing, formulate the situation calculus and the frame problem, and champion logic-based AI.
  • Marvin Minsky (Harvard Junior Fellow, then MIT) — had built the SNARC in 1951, arguably the first neural-network learning machine, using 3,000 vacuum tubes and surplus B-24 autopilot clutches to simulate 40 synapses; later founded the MIT AI Lab and wrote on frames and the "society of mind".
  • Nathaniel Rochester (IBM) — chief designer of the IBM 701, IBM's first commercial scientific computer, and author of its first assembler; supplied industrial credibility, hardware access, and early neural network simulations.
  • Claude Shannon (Bell Labs) — founder of information theory, author of the 1950 paper on programming a computer for chess, builder of the maze-solving mechanical mouse Theseus; his prestige made the funding request respectable.

The proposal listed seven topics for study, and the list is a remarkably durable table of contents for the field: automatic computers; how a computer can be programmed to use language; neuron nets and how to arrange hypothetical neurons to form concepts; theory of the size of a calculation (essentially computational complexity, before the term existed); self-improvement, meaning machines that learn and modify themselves; abstraction, the formation of concepts from sensory and other data; and randomness and creativity, with the conjecture that creative thinking differs from competent thinking by the injection of controlled randomness guided by intuition.

The workshop itself, held over roughly six to eight weeks in mid-1956, did not run as planned. Rockefeller funded about $7,500 rather than the ~$13,500 requested. Attendance was fluid: participants came and went, and probably only about ten people were present at any time, with a broader cast including Ray Solomonoff (who stayed longest and developed ideas that became algorithmic probability), Oliver Selfridge (pandemonium architectures for perception), Arthur Samuel (checkers), Trenchard More, and the Carnegie Tech pair of Allen Newell and Herbert Simon. There was no shared agenda, little organised discussion, and no collective breakthrough.

The one genuine result was brought in from outside. Newell and Simon, working with the programmer J. C. Shaw at RAND and Carnegie Tech, arrived with the Logic Theorist — a running program that proved theorems. Their reception was mixed; the group was somewhat unimpressed, and Newell and Simon in turn found the others' speculation unrigorous and spent much of their time working apart. Yet the workshop's real products were social and linguistic rather than technical.

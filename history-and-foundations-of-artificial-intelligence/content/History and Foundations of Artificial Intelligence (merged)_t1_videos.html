Introduction

Before we can study how artificial intelligence works, we need to be clear about what we are claiming a machine can do. "Intelligence" is a contested term even among psychologists and philosophers, and the field of AI has never settled on a single definition. Instead, researchers have proposed competing framings: systems that think like humans, act like humans, think rationally, or act rationally. These four classic approaches, together with Turing's imitation game, the weak/strong (narrow/general) distinction, and the rational agent framework, give us the working vocabulary used throughout the rest of this module.

Definitions are not just academic housekeeping. How you define intelligence determines what counts as success, what you measure, and what you can honestly claim in a product description, a research paper, or a policy debate. A system that passes a conversational test is not necessarily one that understands; a system that beats humans at one narrow task is not necessarily on a path to general intelligence. Keeping these distinctions straight is a practical skill.

The videos below do not lecture on philosophy; they give you the modern technical vocabulary that today's definitional debates are conducted in. As you watch, try to map each term you hear onto the framework from the readings: Is this capability about thinking or acting? Is it measured against human performance or against a standard of rationality? Would the system being described count as narrow or general intelligence, and on what evidence?

About this video

This short TED-Ed animation, written by Briana Brownell, introduces the three major approaches to machine learning and explains how computers can derive their own rules for solving problems rather than following rules written by hand. It is a concise conceptual piece aimed at a general audience, with illustrated examples rather than code.

Why this video

Understanding that a system learns its own rules sharpens the question of whether such a system is "thinking" or merely "acting" effectively, which is exactly the axis the four classic approaches are built on. TED-Ed's brevity and clarity make it a good orientation before the heavier conceptual reading.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has moved out of research labs and into hiring, lending, medicine, policing, and everyday consumer products. As that shift happened, questions that once seemed philosophical became practical engineering and management problems: Who is harmed when a model is wrong? Whose data trained it? Can anyone explain why it produced a particular output, and who answers for the result? This topic introduces those questions as a core part of AI practice rather than an afterthought.

You will look at several connected concerns: why ethics became central to the field, how bias enters data and models and what fairness might mean, why transparency and explainability matter for trust and oversight, how privacy and data governance constrain what organizations may collect and use, who is accountable when systems cause harm, how AI reshapes work and society, and what governance frameworks and responsible-practice principles are emerging to manage all of this.

As you watch the videos below, keep two habits in mind. First, note the vocabulary — terms like algorithmic bias, explainability, black box, and AI governance are used precisely in professional settings, so collect definitions you can reuse. Second, resist treating these as abstract virtues: for each issue raised, try to name a concrete application domain where it would bite, what the harm would look like, and what a development team could realistically do about it.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence did not begin with modern chatbots or self-driving cars. Its roots run back through centuries of philosophical argument about whether reasoning can be reduced to rules, and through the mathematical work on logic and computation that made those arguments testable. This topic traces that lineage: from formal logic and the theory of computation, to Alan Turing's proposal that a machine's intelligence could be judged by its behavior, to the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project that gave the field its name and its first research agenda.

The decades that followed produced the symbolic AI paradigm, which treated intelligence as the manipulation of symbols according to explicit rules. Early programs such as the Logic Theorist, the General Problem Solver, and Arthur Samuel's checkers player were genuinely impressive demonstrations, and they encouraged bold predictions about how quickly human-level machine intelligence would arrive. Those predictions did not hold, and understanding why is essential background for the rest of this course: the limits of symbolic approaches, and the funding cuts that followed the unmet promises, shape how we evaluate claims about AI today.

As you watch the videos below, keep a timeline in mind. Note which people and events recur, when the key programs were built, and what each program could and could not do. Pay particular attention to the distinction between rule-based symbolic systems and learning-based approaches, since both were present from the very beginning and the tension between them runs through the entire history of the field.

Defining Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence

1

Defining Intelligence and Artificial Intelligence

▶

Almost every discipline that studies minds — psychology, philosophy, neuroscience, education, animal behaviour — has produced its own definition of intelligence, and none of them has won universal acceptance. This is awkward for a field whose very name contains the word. Artificial intelligence is, in effect, an engineering discipline built on a contested foundation. Rather than being a fatal flaw, this ambiguity has been productive: it has forced researchers to be explicit about what they are trying to build and how they will know when they have built it. This topic examines the competing definitions of intelligence, the four classic ways researchers have framed the goal of AI, the Turing Test as an operational shortcut, the strong/weak AI distinction, and the rational-agent vocabulary that underpins the rest of the module.

What Do We Mean by Intelligence?

If you collect a few dozen published definitions of intelligence and look for overlap, a recurring cluster of capabilities emerges. Most definitions mention learning from experience — the ability to change future behaviour on the basis of past outcomes. A child who touches a hot pan once and avoids it thereafter has demonstrated this; so has a spam filter that reclassifies messages after user corrections. Most also mention reasoning, the drawing of new conclusions from existing information, and problem solving, the construction of a path from a current state to a desired one. Finally, nearly all mention adaptation to novelty: performing well in situations that were not specifically anticipated.

Beyond this common core, definitions diverge according to what their authors care about:

  • Goal-directed behaviour under uncertainty. A widely cited formulation holds that intelligence measures an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments. The emphasis here is on complexity and uncertainty: solving a crossword from a complete dictionary is easier than navigating a crowded railway station where information is incomplete and other actors are unpredictable. On this view, a thermostat is minimally intelligent (one goal, one trivially simple environment) while a foraging animal is far more so.
  • Perception and language. Other definitions foreground the ability to build a useful internal model of the world from noisy sensory data, and to use symbols and language to communicate and to think. This tradition treats language as the clearest external evidence of internal thought, which is precisely why so many AI tests are conversational.
  • Generalisation across domains. A third emphasis is transfer: knowledge acquired in one setting being redeployed in another. A person who learns algebra and then applies proportional reasoning to a cooking recipe has generalised. Psychometrics captures something similar with the notion of a general factor underlying performance across diverse cognitive tests.

Crucially, all of these are behavioural rather than structural definitions. They specify what an intelligent system does, not what it is made of or how it is internally organised. The consequence is what philosophers call multiple realisability: radically different substrates can satisfy the same behavioural description. An octopus with a distributed nervous system, a human with a centralised cortex, a colony of ants exhibiting collective route optimisation, and a silicon system running gradient descent may all qualify as intelligent under some behavioural criterion, despite sharing almost no structural features. This is exactly what makes artificial intelligence conceivable in the first place: if intelligence were defined as "whatever neurons in a primate cortex do," the field would be a contradiction in terms.

The lack of consensus has a practical consequence for how the field measures progress. Because there is no agreed abstract yardstick, AI advances are reported in terms of tasks accomplished: a chess rating, a percentage accuracy on an image dataset, a word-error rate on transcribed speech, a score on a set of exam questions. This is honest in one sense — the numbers are checkable — but it invites confusion, because task performance and general capability can come apart dramatically. A system may exceed human accuracy at identifying skin lesions while being unable to answer the question "is this a photograph of skin?" if the question is phrased unusually.

Framing Artificial Intelligence: Four Classic Approaches

A standard organising device splits definitions of AI along two axes: whether the standard of comparison is human performance or ideal rationality, and whether we are concerned with internal thought or external behaviour. This yields four quadrants.

ApproachStandardFocusCentral questionTypical methods and evidence
Thinking humanlyHumanInternal processDoes the machine reason the way people actually reason?Introspection, psychological experiments, brain imaging, cognitive architectures

Thinking humanly ties AI to cognitive science. The aim is not merely to produce correct answers but to reproduce the human process, including its characteristic errors and timing. A classic example is the General Problem Solver of Newell and Simon, which was evaluated partly by comparing the order of its intermediate reasoning steps with the "think-aloud" protocols of human subjects solving the same puzzle. Modern descendants include cognitive architectures such as SOAR and ACT-R, which attempt to predict human reaction times and error patterns. The scientific payoff is a theory of mind; the engineering payoff is limited, because human cognitive quirks are not always worth copying. Human memory is lossy, human arithmetic is slow, and human probability judgement is systematically biased.

Acting humanly ignores internal mechanism entirely and asks only whether an observer can tell the difference. This is the tradition that produces the Turing Test, discussed in detail below. Its strength is that it requires no theory of thought; its weakness is that indistinguishability from a human is both too demanding (a calculator that answers instantly reveals itself as non-human precisely by being too good) and too permissive (shallow conversational tricks can fool inattentive judges).

Thinking rationally descends from Aristotle's syllogisms through nineteenth-century formal logic to the logicist programme in AI. Here intelligence is identified with correct inference: given true premises, derive only true conclusions. A resolution theorem prover working over first-past-order logic exemplifies the approach. Two obstacles emerged. First, most real-world knowledge is not certain, so strict logical entailment is the wrong tool — hence the later absorption of probability theory into the same quadrant. Second, the qualification problem: writing down all the conditions under which a rule holds ("a bird flies, unless it is a penguin, or injured, or in a cage, or dead, or...") is unbounded. Formal reasoning is also computationally expensive; deciding satisfiability in propositional logic is NP-complete, and full first-order logic is only semi-decidable.

Acting rationally sidesteps the demand for correct reasoning and demands only correct action. A rational agent takes the action expected to achieve the best outcome, or, under uncertainty, the best expected outcome. Logical inference may be one route to that action, but it is not required: a reflex that withdraws a hand from a flame is rational behaviour with no inference at all. This framing is the modern mainstream for three reasons:

  • It is measurable. Specify a performance measure, and you can compare two agents numerically without any dispute about whether either is "really" thinking.
  • It is domain-independent. The same mathematics of expected utility applies to a warehouse robot, an advertising auction bidder, and a medical triage system.
  • It is more general than the human standard. It permits agents that behave better than humans, and does not penalise agents that behave differently while achieving the same or better results.

A concrete contrast: consider designing a system to play Go. A "thinking humanly" project would interview strong players, model their pattern recognition and intuition, and be judged on whether its move preferences mirrored theirs. A "thinking rationally" project would try to prove properties of positions. An "acting rationally" project simply asks: which move maximises the probability of winning, given the resources available? The systems that eventually surpassed human champions took the last route, combining tree search with learned value estimates, and produced moves that human experts initially judged to be mistakes.

The Turing Test as an Operational Definition

In 1950 Alan Turing opened a paper with the question "Can machines think?" and immediately declared it too meaningless to deserve discussion, because it depends on contested definitions of "machine" and "think." His move was to replace the question with an operational one — a concrete procedure whose outcome anyone can observe. This is a general and valuable scientific manoeuvre: rather than arguing about the essence of a property, specify a test.

The procedure, which Turing called the imitation game, runs as follows. A human interrogator communicates by text-only channel with two hidden participants: one human, one machine. The interrogator may ask anything at all, and must decide which respondent is the machine. The text-only restriction is deliberate — it prevents the machine from being penalised for lacking a human voice or appearance, isolating intellectual performance from physical resemblance. If, over a sufficient number of trials, interrogators cannot identify the machine reliably better than chance, the machine is said to pass.

Turing's own prediction was statistical rather than absolute: he speculated that by the year 2000 a machine with roughly 109 bits of storage might fool an average interrogator at least 30% of the time in a five-minute conversation. Note how weak that bar is compared with how the test is usually reported in popular accounts.

What makes the test interesting is not the theatre of deception but the breadth of competence it implicitly demands. To sustain open-ended conversation a system needs, at minimum:

  • Natural language processing — to parse arbitrary input and generate fluent, contextually appropriate output.
  • Knowledge representation — to store facts about the world, including the vast body of unstated commonsense knowledge (that ice melts, that people sleep at night, that you cannot carry a piano in a pocket).
  • Automated reasoning — to answer questions whose answers are not stored but must be derived, and to maintain consistency across a long dialogue.
  • Machine learning — to adapt to the interrogator's style, detect new patterns, and handle topics outside any pre-scripted repertoire.

The interrogator can probe each of these. "I left my ice cream in the car for three hours in August — what should I expect to find?" tests commonsense physics. "Earlier you said you had two sisters; how many siblings does your mother's daughter have?" tests dialogue memory and reasoning. "Please rhyme a couplet about a disappointing sandwich" tests generative flexibility.

Turing's test omits perception and physical action, since the text channel excludes them. The Total Turing Test closes this gap by adding a video channel and a hatch through which the interrogator can pass physical objects. Now the machine must also possess:

  • Computer vision (and broader machine perception) — to recognise objects, scenes, faces, and gestures presented to it.
  • Robotics — to manipulate the objects handed over, and to move through the world.

This variant reflects the argument from embodiment: that much of human concept formation is grounded in sensorimotor experience, so a disembodied text system can at best manipulate symbols whose meanings it never acquired directly. Asking a system to describe a text about grasping a mug is not the same as asking it to grasp one.

Criticisms of the Turing Test are numerous and largely well founded:

  • It rewards imitation and deception. The goal is to appear human, which encourages simulating human weaknesses: hesitating, making typing errors, pleading ignorance of arithmetic. Deliberately performing worse is not progress toward intelligence.
  • It can be gamed by shallow tricks. ELIZA, a 1960s program of a few hundred lines, reflected users' statements back as questions and convinced some users it understood them. Later contest entrants adopted personas — a sarcastic teenager, a non-native speaker — that excuse non-sequiturs and evasions. Success here reveals more about human credulity and the ELIZA effect (our readiness to attribute understanding) than about machine cognition.
  • It is anthropocentric. A system with superhuman capabilities in unfamiliar directions would fail, not because it lacks intelligence but because it is not human-like. We do not test aircraft by checking whether ornithologists mistake them for birds.
  • Behaviour may not entail understanding. Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment imagines a person in a room mechanically following rules to manipulate Chinese symbols, producing perfect answers while understanding nothing. Whether this refutes the test or merely restates the disagreement is itself much debated, but it sharpens the point that passing a behavioural test is not obviously the same as having a mind.
  • It is not useful as an engineering target. No serious research programme tries to pass the Turing Test, because doing so would not tell an engineer which subsystem to improve. Contemporary evaluation instead uses decomposed, quantitative benchmarks.

Related probes have been proposed to preserve the operational spirit while resisting gaming. Winograd schemas are sentence pairs whose pronoun reference flips on a single word — "The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too large/small" — requiring world knowledge rather than surface statistics, and offering no room for persona-based evasion.

Strong AI versus Weak AI

The terms weak AI and strong AI originate in philosophy of mind, not engineering, and the distinction concerns what a working machine would prove, not how well it performs.

Weak AI is the claim that machines can be built to act as if they were intelligent within specific, bounded tasks, and that such systems are valuable tools and useful models of cognition. On this view the machine simulates thought in the way a weather model simulates a storm: the simulation is informative and may be highly accurate, but nothing gets wet inside the computer.

Strong AI is the stronger claim that a suitably programmed machine would not be simulating a mind but would have one — genuine understanding, intentionality, and possibly conscious experience. Searle coined the contrast precisely to attack this position; his target was the idea that running the right program is sufficient for mentality.

Two confusions are worth heading off:

  • "Weak" does not mean "feeble." A weak-AI system can be world-class. A chess engine that defeats every human player is still weak AI, because nobody claims it experiences the game.
  • Strong AI is not the same as Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), although the terms are often conflated in popular usage. AGI is a capability claim: human-level competence across arbitrary domains, with the ability to learn new tasks without redesign. Strong AI is a metaphysical claim about inner life. One could in principle have an AGI that is a "philosophical zombie" — fully general in behaviour, with nobody home.

Virtually every deployed system today is weak AI. A web search ranker optimises click-relevance over documents; it has no model of the user's life. A translation system maps token sequences between languages; it does not know what a "harbour" smells like. A recommendation engine predicts engagement; it has no concept of the viewer's wellbeing unless one is engineered in. A vision system that flags manufacturing defects has no notion of what a factory is for. Each is superb inside its boundary and typically brittle just outside it — the classic failure signature is distribution shift, where a model trained on one hospital's scanners degrades badly on another's.

Why does the distinction matter beyond philosophy?

  • Ethics and moral status. If strong AI were achieved, questions of machine welfare, rights, and the permissibility of shutting a system down would become live. Under weak AI they do not arise, though the ethics of impact on humans remain pressing regardless.
  • Legal responsibility. Weak AI is a product or a tool, so liability follows familiar chains: developer, deployer, operator. Attributing agency to the system itself ("the algorithm decided") can function as a responsibility-laundering device. Clarity about weak versus strong keeps accountability with humans and organisations.
  • Honest marketing and policy. Describing a narrow statistical model as "understanding" a medical record invites over-reliance by clinicians and misdirected regulation by legislators.

Rational Agents and the Working Vocabulary

Because the "acting rationally" framing dominates modern AI, its vocabulary is the standard language of the field and will recur throughout this module.

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon that environment through actuators. A percept is a single sensory input; the percept sequence is the complete history of everything the agent has perceived. An agent function maps percept sequences to actions, and an agent program is the concrete implementation of that function on some architecture.

Agent skeleton:

  percept  <- SENSE(environment)
  memory   <- UPDATE-STATE(memory, percept)
  action   <- CHOOSE-ACTION(memory, goals, performance_measure)
  ACTUATE(action)                 # repeat forever

Examples:
  Vacuum robot : sensors = bump, dirt, cliff   actuators = wheels, suction
  Trading agent: sensors = price feed, order book   actuators = buy/sell orders
  Thermostat   : sensors = thermometer, schedule   actuators = boiler relay

A rational agent selects, for each possible percept sequence, an action expected to maximise its performance measure, given the evidence in the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge it has. Four features of this definition deserve emphasis:

  • The performance measure is external, specified by the designer, and should describe the desired state of the environment, not the agent's internal satisfaction. Rewarding a cleaning robot for "dirt collected" invites it to dump dirt on the floor and re-collect it; rewarding "floor cleanliness over time" does not.
  • Rationality is about expected outcomes, not actual ones. Crossing a road after checking carefully is rational even if a falling aeroplane part happens to strike you. Rationality is not omniscience.
  • Rationality is relative to available information. An agent cannot be faulted for failing to use a percept it never received — though it can be faulted for failing to gather information it could have gathered. This motivates exploration and information-gathering actions.
  • A rational agent should learn, improving its behaviour as its percept history grows, and may need autonomy — the capacity to compensate for incorrect or incomplete prior knowledge.

Origins and Early History of AI

1

Origins and Early History of AI

▶

Artificial intelligence did not begin with computers. It began with a much older question: is reasoning something mysterious and uniquely human, or is it a process that follows rules and could therefore, in principle, be carried out by something other than a human mind? The history traced below is the story of that question being sharpened over roughly two and a half millennia — from Aristotle's syllogisms, through Boole's algebra of thought, to Turing's machines — until it became precise enough to be answered experimentally. By the middle of the twentieth century the question had a name, a research community, a funding base, working programs, and a set of spectacular failures that taught the field lessons it is still absorbing.

Reading this history matters for practical reasons, not merely antiquarian ones. The definitional disputes of the 1950s (is intelligence about thinking or about acting? about imitating humans or about being rational?) still structure how systems are evaluated today. The symbolic paradigm's strengths — explicit knowledge, explainable inference — are precisely what modern statistical systems lack and what regulators now demand. And the boom-and-bust cycle that followed the field's early over-promising is a pattern that has repeated often enough to be worth recognising in advance.

Philosophical and Mathematical Roots

The foundational insight of classical logic is that the validity of an argument depends on its form, not its content. Aristotle's Organon (fourth century BCE) catalogued syllogistic forms such as: all M are P; all S are M; therefore all S are P. Substitute anything you like for S, M and P — mammals, humans, mortals, or nonsense words — and if the premises are true the conclusion must be true. This separation of form from content is the germ of the whole computational idea. It means valid reasoning can be checked mechanically, by pattern-matching against a list of permitted forms, without understanding the subject matter at all. A clerk who knows nothing of biology can verify a biological argument's structure.

Medieval logicians elaborated the syllogistic apparatus, and in the thirteenth century Ramon Llull built the Ars Magna, a set of rotating paper discs inscribed with basic concepts that could be combined systematically to generate propositions. Llull's purpose was theological — he hoped to derive truths that would convert non-believers — but the mechanism is recognisably a combinatorial reasoning device. Leibniz, in the seventeenth century, pushed the ambition much further with two linked proposals: a characteristica universalis, a formal language in which any concept could be expressed unambiguously, and a calculus ratiocinator, a set of manipulation rules for that language. His famous hope was that disputants would one day say "Calculemus" — let us calculate — and settle their disagreement by computation rather than argument. Leibniz lacked the mathematics to deliver this, but he stated the AI research programme with startling clarity two centuries early.

That mathematics arrived in 1847 and 1854 with George Boole's The Mathematical Analysis of Logic and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought. Boole's move was to treat logical propositions as algebraic quantities taking the values 1 (true) and 0 (false), with operations corresponding to AND, OR and NOT. Logical inference then becomes algebraic manipulation. The distributive, commutative and associative laws hold in recognisable forms, and De Morgan's laws give systematic translations between conjunction and disjunction.

Boolean identities that let inference become calculation:

  NOT (A AND B)  =  (NOT A) OR  (NOT B)        De Morgan
  NOT (A OR  B)  =  (NOT A) AND (NOT B)        De Morgan
  A AND (B OR C) =  (A AND B) OR (A AND C)     distribution
  A OR  (A AND B)=  A                          absorption

Modus ponens as an algebraic check:
  "A implies B" is written  (NOT A) OR B
  Given A = 1 and (NOT A) OR B = 1, then 0 OR B = 1, so B = 1.

The significance is hard to overstate. Boole showed that the "laws of thought" could be written as equations, and equations can be solved by procedures. In 1937 Claude Shannon's master's thesis at MIT — often called the most influential master's thesis ever written — observed that Boolean algebra describes exactly the behaviour of switching circuits built from relays: a closed relay is 1, an open relay is 0, relays in series compute AND, relays in parallel compute OR. Logic thereby became implementable in metal. Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) meanwhile extended logic beyond Boole's propositional level to quantifiers and variables, giving first-order predicate logic — the representational language that symbolic AI would adopt wholesale.

In parallel ran a tradition of physical machines that carried out defined procedures. Wilhelm Schickard's calculating clock (1623), Pascal's Pascaline (1642) and Leibniz's stepped reckoner (1673) mechanised arithmetic. Jacquard's loom (1804) used punched cards to control weaving patterns, demonstrating that a machine's behaviour could be encoded on an interchangeable medium — the first separation of program from hardware. Charles Babbage's Difference Engine and, far more ambitiously, his Analytical Engine (designed from 1837) combined a "mill" (processor), a "store" (memory), punched-card input and conditional branching: a general-purpose computer in brass and steam, never completed. Ada Lovelace's 1843 notes on the Analytical Engine contain both the first published algorithm intended for a machine and a philosophical caution — the machine "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything; it can do whatever we know how to order it to perform" — which Turing would later name and rebut as "Lady Lovelace's objection". Elaborate automata such as Jacques de Vaucanson's digesting duck (1739) and the Jaquet-Droz writing boy (1774), a clockwork figure that wrote arbitrary programmed texts with a quill, made the same point theatrically: machinery can produce behaviour that looks purposive.

The final root is the twentieth-century formalisation of computability itself, which grew out of a crisis in the foundations of mathematics. David Hilbert had proposed a programme to place all mathematics on a complete, consistent, mechanically decidable axiomatic footing; his Entscheidungsproblem (decision problem) of 1928 asked for an effective procedure that, given any first-order sentence, would decide whether it was valid. The answers demolished the programme but created computer science.

Church's thesis (or the Church–Turing thesis) holds that these radically different formalisms — lambda calculus, Turing machines, Post systems, general recursive functions — all define exactly the same class of computable functions, and that this class captures everything we could intuitively mean by "effectively calculable". The convergence is the evidence. Its consequence for AI is direct: if any two universal systems compute the same functions, then whatever a brain computes, a computer can compute too — provided the brain's activity is itself an effective procedure, and provided we can discover what that procedure is. Those two provisos are where the entire subsequent argument lives.

Several other threads fed the same confluence around the 1940s. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts (1943) showed that networks of idealised binary neurons could compute any propositional logic function, connecting neurophysiology to Boole. Donald Hebb (1949) proposed that learning consists of strengthening connections between co-active neurons, giving a mechanism for adaptation. Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) analysed goal-directed behaviour in animals and machines in terms of feedback and control. Shannon's information theory (1948) made information a measurable quantity. Taken together, these threads supported a single startling proposition: thinking might be symbol manipulation — that mental states are structured representations, that thought is a rule-governed transformation of those representations, and that the physical substrate performing the transformations is irrelevant to the result. Newell and Simon would later state this formally as the physical symbol system hypothesis: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action.

Alan Turing and the Foundations of Machine Intelligence

Turing's 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" introduced what we now call the Turing machine. Its power lies in its poverty. The machine consists of an unbounded tape divided into cells, a read/write head positioned over one cell, a finite set of internal states, and a transition table. At each step it reads the symbol under the head and, according to its current state and that symbol, writes a symbol, moves the head one cell left or right, and enters a new state. That is all.

A Turing machine that increments a unary number (n ones) to n+1:

 STATE   READ   ->  WRITE   MOVE   NEXT STATE

 scan     1     ->    1      R      scan
 scan     _     ->    1      R      halt

Tape before:  _ 1 1 1 _        (represents 3)
Tape after:   _ 1 1 1 1 _      (represents 4)

Turing's argument that this captures all effective computation was psychological rather than mathematical: he analysed what a human "computer" — the job title of a person doing calculation by hand — actually does. Such a person reads finitely many symbols, can distinguish only finitely many states of mind, attends to a bounded region of the paper at any moment, and follows fixed rules. Strip away the inessentials and you have the machine. The second key result was the universal Turing machine: a single machine U that takes as input an encoded description of any other machine M plus M's input, and simulates M. This is the theoretical charter for the stored-program computer and for AI: one physical device, reprogrammed, can exhibit any computable behaviour whatever. Turing then proved the halting problem undecidable — no machine can determine, for all machine-input pairs, whether the computation terminates — settling the Entscheidungsproblem negatively and establishing that there are precise limits to mechanism.

During the Second World War at Bletchley Park, Turing turned theory into operational practice. Building on Polish work by Marian Rejewski, he designed the Bombe, an electromechanical device that searched Enigma rotor configurations. The Bombe's method is instructive as early AI: it worked from a crib (a guessed plaintext fragment such as a routine weather report), propagated the logical consequences of a hypothesised rotor setting through a network of wired relays, and rejected settings the instant a contradiction appeared — a hardware implementation of constraint propagation and proof by contradiction, pruning an astronomically large search space. Turing also devised Banburismus, a sequential Bayesian scoring procedure using log-odds units he called "bans" to rank hypotheses about rotor order. Search with heuristic pruning, and probabilistic evidence accumulation, were thus both demonstrated on real problems of enormous consequence before the first electronic computer ran a program.

In 1950 Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in Mind, opening with "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" and immediately declaring the question "too meaningless to deserve discussion" in that raw form, because it depends on how we choose to define two contested words. He replaced it with an operational substitute, the Imitation Game. In the original version, an interrogator communicates by teleprinter with two hidden participants, a man and a woman, and must determine which is which while the man tries to deceive and the woman tries to help. Turing then asks: what happens if a machine takes the part of the man? The question "can machines think?" is replaced by "can a machine play this game as successfully as a man can?" The modern reading — a machine and a human, with the interrogator identifying which is the machine — is a simplification of Turing's staged formulation, but preserves its logic.

The design choices are deliberate and worth unpacking. The teleprinter channel deliberately excludes appearance and voice, so the test measures verbal behaviour rather than engineering of a lifelike body. The interrogator may ask anything — arithmetic, poetry criticism, chess, personal anecdotes — so no narrow competence suffices. And crucially the criterion is behavioural and comparative: the machine need not be perfect, only as hard to distinguish from a human as another human would be. Turing offered a rough prediction that by around the year 2000, a machine with about 109 bits of storage might fool an average interrogator at least 30% of the time in five minutes of questioning — a prediction about a modest, time-limited version of the test rather than about full human-level intelligence.

The final section is arguably the most prophetic. Turing calculated the labour of hand-coding an adult mind and judged it infeasible. His alternative: "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of education one would obtain the adult brain." The child machine would have relatively little initial structure but a strong capacity to be modified. He then sketched the training regime in terms that read as an outline of machine learning: a system of rewards and punishments to reinforce useful behaviours and suppress others; a distinction between fixed rules and modifiable ones; the deliberate inclusion of a random element so that the search for good configurations does not get stuck, noting that random search can outperform systematic search when solutions are scattered; and an acceptance that the teacher will not know what is going on inside the pupil. He also concluded that an intelligent machine would need to take risks and make mistakes: "if a machine is expected to be infallible, it cannot also be intelligent." Turing was writing about reinforcement learning, stochastic search, and the opacity of learned systems in 1950.

The Turing Test and Measuring Machine Intelligence

1

The Turing Test and Measuring Machine Intelligence

▶

In October 1950 the journal Mind published a paper by the British mathematician Alan Turing titled Computing Machinery and Intelligence. It opened with a sentence that has framed six decades of argument: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" Turing then did something unexpected — he immediately abandoned that question as unanswerable and replaced it with a different one that could be settled by experiment. That substitution is the intellectual heart of what we now call the Turing test, and it remains the most widely cited attempt to give the phrase "machine intelligence" an operational meaning. This topic examines the reasoning behind Turing's move, the exact procedure of the imitation game, the bundle of technical capabilities that passing it would require, the variants and competitions that have tried to implement it, the philosophical objections it has attracted, and the very different evaluation methods that dominate contemporary AI research.

Turing's Question: Can Machines Think?

Turing's first observation was linguistic rather than technical. To ask whether a machine can "think" requires agreed definitions of both machine and think. The first term is tractable; the second is not. "Thinking" and "intelligence" are folk-psychological words whose meanings shift with context, culture, and rhetorical purpose. Turing noted that settling the question by definition would reduce to a "poll of opinion" about how words are ordinarily used — a survey of linguistic habit, not a scientific result. Worse, any definition of thinking that appeals to inner experience is unverifiable from the outside: we cannot inspect another person's consciousness either, yet we routinely credit other people with thought on the basis of how they behave and what they say.

Turing therefore proposed replacing the abstract question with a concrete, repeatable experiment. Instead of asking "does the machine think?", we ask "can the machine perform, in a controlled test, indistinguishably from a human being?" This is a methodological move of the same kind that psychology made when behaviourism displaced introspection as a research method, and the same kind that physics makes when it replaces "hotness" with a thermometer reading. The advantage is that the new question has a procedure attached: any two investigators can run it and compare results. The cost — as critics have pressed ever since — is that the new question may not be the old question at all.

The consequence of this substitution is a decisive shift from internal mental states to external behaviour. Nothing in the Turing test asks how the machine produces its answers. It does not require neurons, symbols, learning, emotion, or understanding. It asks only whether the observable output stream is discriminable from a human's. Turing defended this with what is sometimes called the "polite convention" argument: in daily life we extend the assumption of mind to other humans purely on behavioural evidence, so refusing to extend it to a machine that behaves identically is a prejudice rather than an inference.

Two pieces of historical context matter. First, Turing's paper predates by six years the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project at which John McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence." Turing was writing before the field existed, before there was a research community, a name, or a funding stream — at a time when there were perhaps a handful of working stored-program computers in the world. His paper is thus not a contribution to AI so much as a founding charter for it, anticipating machine learning ("child machines" trained by reward and punishment), genetic search, and the storage requirements of a general knowledge base.

Second, Turing made a quantitative prediction. He estimated that in about fifty years' time — by roughly the year 2000 — it would be possible to program computers with a storage capacity of around 109 bits (he wrote 109, about 119 MB) so well that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 per cent chance of making the correct identification after five minutes of questioning. Stated the other way round, the machine would fool the interrogator roughly 30 per cent of the time in short exchanges. It is worth being precise about what this prediction is and is not. It is not a definition of passing the test, and Turing never said 30 per cent constitutes success; it is an engineering forecast about a specific, deliberately limited scenario — five minutes, an average (not expert) interrogator, a single machine. Much of the confusion in later "the Turing test has been passed" headlines comes from treating this incidental forecast as the test's official threshold.

How the Imitation Game Works

Turing introduced the test by analogy with a Victorian parlour game. In the original party game, a man (A) and a woman (B) are in one room and an interrogator (C) in another; C must determine which is which using written questions, while A tries to cause a wrong identification and B tries to help C. Turing's modification is simple and radical: replace player A with a machine. The question then becomes whether the interrogator misidentifies as often when the game is played with a machine as when it is played between two humans.

The modern standard formulation, and the one used in most textbooks, is the three-participant version:

  • A human interrogator (the judge) is isolated from two hidden participants.
  • One hidden participant is a human confederate; the other is the machine.
  • The interrogator converses with both in parallel through a text channel and must decide which is which.
  • The machine succeeds if the interrogator's judgements are no better than chance across a sufficient number of trials.

Several design features of the procedure are doing important work and deserve individual attention.

The channel is deliberately impoverished. Turing specified a teleprinter (teletype) link, and today we would use a text chat window. The purpose is to strip away every cue that is irrelevant to thinking: voice timbre, accent, handwriting, facial appearance, reaction to physical stimuli. A machine should not fail the test because it lacks a larynx or a face, and should not pass because it has a convincing synthetic voice. The text channel isolates the variable of interest — the content and structure of linguistic behaviour.

The questioning is unrestricted. The interrogator may ask about any subject whatever. Turing's own illustrative dialogue moves from asking the witness to write a sonnet on the subject of the Forth Bridge, to arithmetic (Add 34957 to 70764), to chess (I have K at my K1, and no other pieces. You have only K at K6 and R at R1. It is your move. What do you play?), which shows how wide the intended range is. A well-designed interrogation probes areas where machines are historically weak: personal autobiography and episodic memory ("what did your grandmother smell like?"), humour and irony, novel metaphor, commonsense physical reasoning ("if I put a sofa on the roof of my car and drive fast, what happens?"), self-contradiction across a long conversation, and questions about the conversation itself ("you said earlier that you were from Leeds — which part?"). This unrestricted scope is what makes the test a candidate for measuring general conversational competence rather than a narrow skill.

The machine is licensed to lie. This is one of the most frequently misunderstood features. The game is imitation, not interrogation under oath, so the machine may deceive in order to appear human. Turing explicitly imagines the machine pausing about thirty seconds before answering an arithmetic question and then giving a slightly wrong answer, precisely because instantaneous perfect arithmetic would betray it. Typing errors, self-corrections, admissions of ignorance, changes of subject, expressions of boredom, and fabricated childhood memories are all legitimate strategies. This reveals something significant about the test's target: it measures resemblance to a human, including human limitations, rather than capability in any absolute sense.

Success is statistical. A machine that fools one judge on one afternoon has demonstrated almost nothing, because a single conversation is a sample of size one from a noisy distribution. Judges vary enormously in skill and scepticism; some human confederates are misidentified as machines (the so-called "confederate effect," repeatedly observed in Loebner Prize transcripts, where terse or eccentric humans are voted machines). Turing's own formulation is explicitly probabilistic — a percentage chance of correct identification — so any serious implementation requires many trials, multiple judges, multiple confederates, and randomised assignment, with results reported as a rate with a confidence interval.

The test is mechanism-neutral. Finally, and by design, the test says nothing about what is inside the box. A system could pass by deep understanding, by statistical pattern completion over a vast text corpus, or — in principle — by an astronomically large lookup table of canned replies. Turing's criterion is indistinguishability of behaviour, not equivalence of mechanism. This is simultaneously the test's greatest strength (it is empirical and implementation-independent) and the opening through which its most famous criticism enters.

Although the test itself is a black-box behavioural criterion, it is useful precisely because passing it would require a very specific set of engineering achievements. This is why introductory AI texts use the Turing test as a map of the discipline's subfields: the competence areas are not arbitrary, they are what an unrestricted text conversation actually demands.

Many commentators therefore distinguish the total Turing test, which adds a video channel and a hatch through which physical objects can be passed. This extension demands two further capability areas:

  • Computer vision (and more broadly machine perception), so the system can identify objects, read scenes, interpret gestures, and answer questions about what it is shown — "how many coins are on the table, and which is the oldest?"
  • Robotics, so the system can manipulate the physical world with human-like dexterity — assembling the objects passed through the hatch, tying a knot, or pouring liquid without spilling.

The total Turing test closes an important loophole: a purely linguistic system can talk about the world without ever having contacted it, and its language may be "ungrounded." Requiring perception and action forces the system's words to connect to sensorimotor experience, which is the core claim of grounded and embodied approaches to intelligence. It also raises the bar dramatically — human-level dexterity remains a harder open problem than human-level conversation, a reversal of what most mid-century researchers expected.

Variations and Practical Implementations

The full Turing test has never been run rigorously at scale, partly because it is expensive and partly because it was never precisely specified. What exists instead is a family of partial implementations and deliberate redesigns, each trading away some of Turing's generality for measurability.

  • The Loebner Prize. Funded by Hugh Loebner and first held in 1991, this was the longest-running attempt at an actual Turing-style competition, held annually until 2019. Judges held parallel text conversations with human confederates and programs, then ranked them for "humanness." A bronze medal and cash prize went each year to the most human-like chatbot; silver (fooling judges in an unrestricted text test) and gold (an audio-visual test) were never awarded. Early contests restricted conversation to a declared topic to keep the task tractable — a compromise Turing would not have accepted. The Loebner Prize is historically important but also cautionary: it was widely criticised for rewarding conversational trickery (canned humour, deflection, personality quirks) and for the recurring embarrassment of human confederates being judged less human than the programs.
  • The reverse Turing test. Here the roles are inverted: a machine must decide whether it is dealing with a human or another machine. The most economically significant implementation is CAPTCHA — "Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart" — used to protect web forms from bots. CAPTCHAs work by exploiting tasks where humans still outperform automated systems: distorted-text reading, image categorisation, or, in reCAPTCHA v3, behavioural signals such as mouse trajectories and interaction timing. The steady erosion of CAPTCHA difficulty as vision models improve is itself a rough longitudinal measure of machine progress.
  • The Winograd Schema Challenge. Proposed by Hector Levesque and colleagues in 2011 as a "Turing test without the deception," this replaces open conversation with multiple-choice commonsense pronoun resolution. Each item is a sentence pair whose only difference is one word, and that word flips the referent of an ambiguous pronoun. For example: "The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too large" versus "...because it was too small." Statistical word association is little help; resolving the pronoun requires knowing that containers must be larger than their contents. The format is objective, machine-scorable, immune to charm and evasion, and yields a single number. Its limitation is that scores on fixed schema sets can be driven up by large-scale pretraining without any guarantee that general commonsense has been acquired — which is why successor benchmarks such as WinoGrande were built to be larger and adversarially filtered.
  • The Lovelace Test. Named for Ada Lovelace, who argued in 1843 that the Analytical Engine could "originate nothing," this family of tests targets creativity rather than imitation. In the version proposed by Bringsjord, Bello and Ferrucci (2001), a system passes if it produces an artefact — a story, a proof, a design — that its own designers cannot explain as the result of the architecture, knowledge base, and training they supplied. A later variant, Lovelace 2.0 (Riedl, 2014), asks the system to meet a human-specified creative constraint ("write a story in which a cat saves a city, without using the word 'hero'"). These tests shift the criterion from resembling a human to producing genuine novelty, and they foreground the awkward fact that a system's designers may be unable to explain its outputs for uninteresting reasons (sheer opacity) as well as interesting ones.
  • The "Eugene Goostman" episode. In June 2014, a University of Reading event marking the sixtieth anniversary of Turing's death announced that a chatbot called Eugene Goostman had "passed the Turing test" by convincing 33 per cent of judges in five-minute conversations. The claim was widely disputed, and the reasons are instructive. The program was designed to pose as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy with limited English, a persona that pre-excuses grammatical errors, ignorance, and evasive or flippant replies as age and language artefacts. The conversations lasted only five minutes, the judges were not expert interrogators, and 33 per cent was treated as a pass threshold on the basis of Turing's incidental 1950 forecast rather than any agreed criterion. The episode is now the standard example of how a poorly specified test invites results that are technically defensible and scientifically empty.

The Chinese Room and Other Criticisms

The best-known objection to the Turing test's underlying logic is John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment, published in 1980 in Behavioral and Brain Sciences. Searle asks us to imagine a monolingual English speaker locked in a room. Slips of paper bearing Chinese characters are posted in through a slot. The man cannot read Chinese; to him the characters are meaningless squiggles. But he has an enormous collection of rule books, written in English, that tell him which squiggles to write out in response to which incoming squiggles. He follows the rules mechanically and posts his answers back. To a native Chinese speaker outside, the answers are indistinguishable from those of a fluent, intelligent Chinese speaker. The room passes a Chinese-language Turing test.

Searle's point is that the man plainly does not understand Chinese. He is manipulating symbols according to their shapes, with no access to their meanings. And yet, Searle argues, this is exactly what a digital computer does: a program is a set of formal rules for transforming symbol tokens on the basis of their form. The conclusion is that syntax alone is insufficient for semantics — running the right program, however perfectly, cannot by itself constitute understanding, intentionality, or consciousness. This is an attack not on whether machines can pass the test, but on what passing would prove. Searle labelled the position he was attacking "strong AI" (the programmed computer is a mind) as distinct from "weak AI" (the computer is a useful model of mind).

The literature contains a long list of replies; the most influential is the systems reply. It concedes that the man does not understand Chinese but denies that this is the relevant subject. The appropriate candidate for understanding is the whole system — man plus rule books plus paper plus filing system plus the procedures that bind them. Analogously, no individual neuron in a Chinese speaker's brain understands Chinese; understanding is a property of the organised whole, not of its components. Searle's counter-move is to have the man internalise the entire rule book and do all the work in his head, so that he is the whole system and still, he insists, understands nothing. Critics respond that our intuitions about what an imaginary person who has memorised a language-sized rule system would or would not understand are exactly the intuitions we should not trust. Other replies include the robot reply (add sensors and effectors so the symbols are causally grounded in the world — essentially an appeal to the total Turing test) and the brain simulator reply (if the program simulates neuron-by-neuron activity, denying understanding commits you to denying it in brains too). The debate remains unresolved, which is itself significant: it indicates that the dispute is conceptual rather than empirical, and that no experiment of the Turing kind can settle it.

Independent of Searle, the test attracts several practical and methodological criticisms:

  • It rewards deception and the imitation of human flaws. Because the goal is to be mistaken for a human, the optimal strategy includes simulating ignorance, slow arithmetic, typing errors, mood, and evasion. A system that is a better reasoner than any human would have to hide that fact to pass. A test that penalises superhuman competence is a poor measure of intelligence, and it channels engineering effort into conversational camouflage — the documented history of chatbot competitions, from ELIZA's reflective questioning in 1966 to PARRY's paranoid deflections and Goostman's teenage flippancy.
  • It is anthropocentric. The test treats human-like behaviour as the only valid form of intelligence. This excludes by construction any intelligence that is real but differently shaped: a system with perfect recall, distributed attention, non-human sensory modalities, or reasoning styles no human uses. Strong AI versus Weak AI

1

Strong AI versus Weak AI

▶

Few distinctions in artificial intelligence carry as much conceptual weight — or are as frequently mangled in popular discussion — as the split between weak (narrow) AI and strong AI, also called artificial general intelligence (AGI). The distinction is not primarily about how powerful a system is, how much money it cost to train, or how impressive its outputs look. It is about the kind of achievement a system represents. A weak AI system is an engineered artefact that produces intelligent-seeming behaviour within a bounded domain; a strong AI system would be a general learner and, on the strongest readings of the claim, a genuine mind. The terminology descends from the philosopher John Searle, who in 1980 coined "strong AI" for the thesis that an appropriately programmed computer is a mind with genuine understanding, and "weak AI" for the more modest thesis that computers are useful tools for simulating and studying mental processes. Every deployed system in existence today — every fraud detector, translation engine, radiology triage model, and chatbot — sits on the weak side of that line, however sophisticated it appears.

Understanding this boundary matters practically as well as philosophically. It shapes what you should expect from a procurement decision, how you should read a vendor's marketing copy, how you interpret a headline claiming a model "understands" physics, and how you reason about long-range policy questions such as machine rights or existential risk. What follows examines each side of the distinction in detail, the philosophical arguments that give it its bite, the actual status of contemporary systems, and the reasons many researchers now prefer to think in terms of a continuum rather than a clean dichotomy.

Defining weak (narrow) AI. A weak AI system simulates intelligent behaviour rather than possessing intelligence in any general sense. The system is constructed — by programming, by training on data, or by some combination — to map inputs to outputs in a way that a human observer would judge competent. Nothing in the architecture requires that the system grasp what the inputs mean. A convolutional network that labels an image "golden retriever" has learned a statistical association between arrangements of pixel intensities and a token in its label set. It has no concept of a dog as a living animal that breathes, ages, belongs to someone, or might bite. Its competence is an artefact of the correlation structure in its training distribution, and it is real competence in the engineering sense: the labels are useful and often correct. But it is competence without comprehension.

The defining structural feature of weak AI is that performance is bounded by the task the system was trained or programmed to handle. The boundary is usually sharp and often surprising. A speech recogniser trained on North American broadcast English degrades badly on Glaswegian or Nigerian accents it never encountered. A credit-scoring model built on pre-pandemic repayment data produces systematically miscalibrated risk estimates once employment patterns shift. A chess engine of superhuman strength cannot play draughts, cannot explain why it moved, and cannot notice that the board has been set up illegally unless someone wrote that check for it. The engineering term for this is distribution shift: the system's guarantees hold only while the world it meets resembles the world it was fitted to.

  • Spam filters classify messages as wanted or unwanted using lexical features, sender reputation, and header patterns. They are constantly retrained because spammers adapt, and a filter that is excellent in January may be mediocre by June.
  • Recommendation engines predict which item a user will click or watch next by exploiting patterns of co-consumption across millions of users. They optimise engagement, not the user's interests, and they have no model of what a film is about.
  • Image classifiers assign labels to visual input and underpin applications from medical screening to agricultural pest detection. They can be fooled by adversarial perturbations imperceptible to a human eye — powerful evidence that their internal representation is not the human one.
  • Speech recognisers transcribe audio to text. Transcription is not understanding: a system may render "recognise speech" perfectly and still have no notion of what recognition or speech are.
  • Game-playing programs such as Deep Blue, AlphaGo, and AlphaZero reach or exceed the best human play in their domains. AlphaGo's famous Move 37 against Lee Sedol was creative by any reasonable behavioural standard, yet the program could not be asked to plan a dinner party, and knew nothing of the tournament it was winning.

Because the competence is task-bound, transfer to an unrelated problem generally requires retraining or redesign by human engineers. There is no button that converts a protein-folding predictor into a legal-document summariser. Even within a family of related tasks, transfer is a deliberate engineering act: fine-tuning a vision model on a new dataset, adapting a language model with domain-specific corpora, rebuilding a feature pipeline. The intelligence that performs the transfer lives in the human team, not in the artefact. This is precisely the capacity that strong AI is defined by possessing.

It follows that success for weak AI is measured by task metrics rather than by evidence of comprehension. Practitioners report accuracy, precision and recall, F1 score, area under the ROC curve, word error rate, BLEU or ROUGE scores, mean average precision, perplexity, regret, or Elo rating. Every one of these quantities is defined relative to a dataset or an opponent pool. None of them is a measure of understanding, and none of them claims to be. A system that reaches 99.2% accuracy on a benchmark has demonstrated a statistical property of its input–output mapping over a particular sample — a genuinely valuable thing to know, and an entirely different thing from knowing that the system grasps its subject matter.

Weak AI, schematically:

    bounded task T  -->  engineered system S  -->  metric M on test set D
                          (trained/programmed by humans for T)

    Change T  =>  humans must rebuild or retrain S
    High M    =>  evidence about behaviour on D, not about understanding

Defining strong AI. Strong AI, or artificial general intelligence, is defined by the removal of exactly the boundary that characterises weak AI. A strong AI would learn any intellectual task a human can learn, without task-specific engineering. Handed a rulebook for an unfamiliar game, a research paper in an unfamiliar field, or a broken appliance and a toolkit, it would do what a capable adult does: form a model of the situation, identify what it does not know, acquire the missing knowledge, and act. The generality is the point. A system that is superhuman at a thousand tasks but requires a human team to add the thousand-and-first is still, on this definition, a collection of narrow systems.

The claim typically goes further. In Searle's original and strongest formulation, strong AI asserts that the system actually understands, rather than merely behaving as though it does. This is a claim about the system's internal states having genuine semantic content — about its representation of "water" being about water, not merely a symbol that participates in water-related input–output regularities. Philosophers call this property intentionality or aboutness, and it is not obviously reducible to any pattern of behaviour, which is why the strong AI thesis is contested rather than empirically settled.

Some formulations extend further still, to self-awareness, intentionality, and subjective experience — the claim that there is something it is like to be the system, that it has a point of view, that it can suffer or flourish. This is the hardest version of the thesis and connects directly to the philosophy of mind's "hard problem" of consciousness. It is worth separating these strands, because they come apart:

A system could conceivably be fully general without being conscious, or (less plausibly) conscious without being general. Careless discussion collapses these, which is one reason debates about whether a given model "is AGI" so often stall: the participants are asserting and denying different propositions.

On the empirical question, the position is straightforward: no existing system meets these criteria by any widely accepted standard. Contemporary models are impressive, broad, and commercially transformative, and none of them learns arbitrary new domains autonomously, maintains reliable long-horizon goals, or gives us any reason to attribute understanding beyond sophisticated pattern completion. Meanwhile, predictions of when or whether strong AI will arrive vary enormously. Surveys of published AI researchers have repeatedly produced aggregate forecasts spanning many decades, with large fractions of respondents placing meaningful probability both on arrival within twenty years and on arrival beyond the century — and a minority holding that it is not achievable at all on current computational paradigms. The historical record counsels humility in both directions: Herbert Simon predicted in 1965 that machines would be capable of any human work within twenty years, while sceptics in the 1970s confidently declared computer chess and machine translation impossible. Both were wrong, in opposite directions, and for instructive reasons.

The philosophical stakes. Why does any of this matter beyond terminology? Because the weak/strong distinction is the point where engineering meets the philosophy of mind, and the outcome shapes what we owe machines and what we may claim on their behalf.

The position friendliest to strong AI is functionalism: the view that mental states are individuated by their causal-functional roles rather than by the material that realises them. On this view, pain just is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage, typically causes avoidance behaviour, and typically interacts with beliefs and desires in characteristic ways. If a silicon system instantiates the same functional organisation as a human brain, it thereby has the same mental states — the substrate is irrelevant, just as a clock keeps time whether it is driven by a pendulum or a quartz crystal. Functionalism underwrites the intuition behind thought experiments such as gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement: if each silicon neuron reproduces the input–output behaviour of the biological one it replaces, at what point would the lights go out, and why?

The most celebrated counter-argument is Searle's Chinese Room, which presses the claim that syntax alone cannot generate semantics. Imagine a person who speaks no Chinese sealed in a room with an enormous rulebook. Chinese characters arrive through a slot; the person looks up the shapes, follows the rules mechanically, and passes out other characters. To Chinese speakers outside, the room gives fluent, apparently intelligent replies. Yet the person inside understands nothing — they manipulate uninterpreted shapes. Searle's conclusion is that running a program, which is by definition formal symbol manipulation, cannot be sufficient for understanding, because the symbols have no meaning for the system; any meaning is projected onto them by observers. Defenders reply with the systems response (the person is only one component; understanding is a property of the whole room, rulebook and all), the robot response (embed the system in a body with sensors and effectors so its symbols are causally grounded in the world), and the brain simulator response (a program simulating neuron-by-neuron activity of a Chinese speaker's brain would seem to leave nothing out). The debate has run for four decades without resolution, which is itself informative: this is not a question that a benchmark result can close.

That last point has a sharp corollary for testing. The Turing test evaluates observable behaviour and therefore cannot settle the strong AI question. Turing's imitation game deliberately replaced the intractable question "can machines think?" with an operational, behavioural substitute. That was a methodological virtue for its purpose, but it means a passing grade is silent about what is happening inside. A system could pass by being a mind, or by being an enormous lookup table (Ned Block's "Blockhead" argument), or by exploiting human credulity — as the crude 1966 ELIZA program did, eliciting emotional disclosures from users who knew they were talking to a script. Conversely, a genuinely understanding system might fail for lacking human-typical ignorance, human reaction times, or the inclination to deceive an interrogator about its nature.

The practical stakes escalate if the strong thesis is ever satisfied. If strong AI were achieved, questions of machine moral status and rights would follow. An entity with interests, preferences, and the capacity for suffering would plausibly belong in the moral circle, and that would raise very concrete problems: would deleting a trained model be killing? Would running it on demand for commercial purposes be forced labour? Could it own property, consent to experiments, or refuse to be modified? These questions are currently hypothetical, but the history of moral-circle expansion suggests that the practices we establish before the question becomes urgent tend to persist afterwards.

Finally, the debate influences how much weight we give to claims that a system "knows" or "believes" something. Engineers and journalists routinely use intentional vocabulary — the model "thinks", "knows", "wants", "tries" — because it is a compact and predictively useful shorthand, what Daniel Dennett called adopting the intentional stance. There is nothing wrong with the shorthand as long as everyone remembers it is one. It becomes dangerous when it migrates into safety cases, legal filings, or clinical documentation: "the system knows the patient is allergic to penicillin" is a claim about a data field and a retrieval mechanism that can silently fail, not about a state of knowledge that can be relied upon the way a colleague's knowledge can.

Where today's systems actually sit. Modern foundation models — large models pre-trained on broad data and adapted to many downstream uses — complicate the picture without changing the verdict. A single large language model will draft contracts, write and debug code, translate between dozens of languages, summarise research, tutor a student in algebra, and generate plausible poetry. That breadth is genuinely new; no previous artefact spanned so many tasks with one set of weights. Nevertheless, the behaviour derives from patterns in training data — from next-token prediction over a corpus comprising a large fraction of the accessible written record, refined by fine-tuning and human feedback. Breadth of training corpus produces breadth of behaviour. It does not, by itself, produce the open-ended autonomous learning that defines generality.

Several concrete deficits mark the gap. Such systems lack persistent goals: absent external scaffolding, a language model does not carry an intention across sessions, notice that yesterday's plan is failing, or pursue an objective over weeks. They lack grounded experience of the world: their representation of "heavy", "sour", or "three metres away" is derived from co-occurrence statistics of words rather than from sensorimotor contact, which is the symbol-grounding problem in contemporary dress. And they lack reliable self-correction: a model's expressed confidence is often poorly calibrated against its actual accuracy, so it will assert a fabricated citation, a non-existent legal precedent, or a wrong arithmetic result in the same fluent register it uses for correct claims. Real cases exist of practitioners sanctioned by courts for filing briefs containing invented case law generated in exactly this way.

Failures on simple reasoning or novel situations reveal the absence of genuine comprehension more clearly than successes on hard benchmarks reveal its presence. Characteristic failure modes include:

  • Surface-form sensitivity — rewording a problem, changing the names of the people in it, or altering the numeric values can flip a correct answer to an incorrect one, indicating reliance on familiar templates rather than on the problem's structure.
  • Distractor vulnerability — inserting an irrelevant but topical clause into a word problem measurably degrades accuracy, which should be impossible for a solver that has genuinely modelled the task.
  • Benchmark contamination — a model may score highly on a published test simply because that test, or material closely resembling it, appeared in its training data, inflating apparent understanding.
  • Brittleness on trivially novel puzzles — variants of classic riddles with the twist removed often elicit the memorised answer to the original rather than the obvious answer to the variant.
  • Inconsistency — the same factual question asked in two forms can produce two contradictory answers within one conversation, with no sign that the system notices the conflict.

These are exactly the errors one expects from an extremely capable pattern-completer and exactly the errors one does not expect from an understander. For this reason, broad capability across many tasks is better described as "broad narrow AI" than as general intelligence: the coverage is wide, but the underlying mechanism is the same distributional pattern-matching, and the mechanism's failure signature is visible at every edge.

Complicating matters, marketing language frequently blurs the line. Product copy speaks of systems that "reason", "understand your business", or "think through problems"; the term "AGI" is deployed in fundraising materials and roadmap announcements where it functions as an aspiration rather than a claim about current capability. Anthropomorphic interface design — first-person pronouns, apologies, simulated deliberation — amplifies the effect on users. Critical evaluation therefore requires disciplined questions:

  • What is the exact task, and what is the evaluation set? Could that set have appeared in training data?
  • What happens under distribution shift — different phrasing, different demographics, different domain, adversarial input?
  • Does the system report calibrated uncertainty, and is that calibration measured?
  • Is the claim of "understanding" doing any work beyond reporting a benchmark score?
  • What human scaffolding — prompt engineering, retrieval pipelines, output filtering, human review — is required for the demonstrated behaviour, and is it visible in the demo?

A spectrum rather than a binary. Although the weak/strong contrast is conceptually clean, treating it as a switch that is either off or on obscures real variation among systems. A more useful picture positions systems along a continuum by how many domains they handle and how far they transfer learning — the second dimension being the more diagnostic. Transfer distance asks: how far from the training distribution can the system go while remaining competent, and how much new data or human intervention does adaptation require? A system that masters a new task from a handful of examples has travelled further towards generality than one that matches its performance only after a million labelled instances and a rebuilt pipeline.

AI Winters and Cycles of Progress

1

AI Winters and Cycles of Progress

▶

The history of artificial intelligence is not a smooth upward curve. It is a sequence of surges and collapses in which bold technical demonstrations attract extraordinary funding and public attention, fail to generalise beyond the conditions that produced them, and are followed by a sharp withdrawal of money, confidence and even the name "artificial intelligence" itself. These withdrawal phases are known as AI winters, a term coined by researchers in the mid-1980s by analogy with "nuclear winter" — a warning issued, notably, at a time when the field was still booming. Understanding this pattern matters for two reasons. First, it explains why particular techniques dominated particular decades: the technology of any era is shaped as much by what funders would pay for as by what was scientifically promising. Second, it supplies a diagnostic vocabulary for evaluating present-day claims, since the rhetorical structure of an AI boom has remained remarkably stable across seventy years.

The boom-and-bust pattern in AI history

Each cycle follows a recognisable sequence. It opens with a genuine technical breakthrough — a program, an architecture or a hardware capability that does something no machine has done before. Because the breakthrough is real, the enthusiasm it generates is not irrational; the error occurs in the next step, when success on a constrained problem is extrapolated into a prediction about unconstrained human-level capability. Herbert Simon's 1965 statement that "machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do" and Marvin Minsky's 1970 remark that a machine with "the general intelligence of an average human being" was three to eight years away are the canonical examples, but structurally identical claims accompanied expert systems in 1984 and large language models in the 2020s.

Ambitious public predictions attract funding surges from three sources that behave differently. Governments and militaries fund on the basis of strategic anxiety and can commit very large sums quickly, but they also review programmes and can terminate them abruptly. Industry funds on the basis of expected product revenue and withdraws when the revenue does not appear. Venture capital funds on the basis of expected resale value and is the most volatile of the three. When all three align, a boom is intense; when all three reverse within a short window, the result is a winter rather than a mere slowdown.

The turning point in every cycle has been the same: systems that work on demonstration problems fail to scale. The demonstration problem is chosen, often unconsciously, to suit the method — a blocks world with ten objects, a chess endgame, a medical domain with a few hundred rules. Real environments differ not in degree but in kind: they contain open-ended sets of objects, unstated background assumptions, noisy inputs and adversarial users. When the gap becomes publicly visible, confidence collapses faster than it accumulated, because the funders were never evaluating the science directly — they were evaluating the reputation of the field.

Crucially, the quiet periods are not empty. Backpropagation was popularised in 1986, during the gap between winters; convolutional networks, reinforcement learning theory, probabilistic graphical models, support vector machines and statistical machine translation were all developed or matured during the second winter and its aftermath, when "AI" was an unsayable word in grant applications. The groundwork for each resurgence was laid in the trough of the preceding one. Historians usually identify two major winters — roughly 1974–1980 and 1987–1993 — with smaller local contractions (machine translation in 1966, connectionism after 1969) that affected sub-fields rather than the whole discipline.

The first wave of optimism (1956–1973)

The field acquired its name at the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project, whose proposal asserted that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." The confidence had an empirical basis. Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw's Logic Theorist (1955–56) proved thirty-eight of the first fifty-two theorems of Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica, and found a proof of Theorem 2.85 shorter and more elegant than the published one. Their subsequent General Problem Solver (1957) generalised the approach through means–ends analysis: represent the current state and the goal state, measure the difference between them, and select an operator known to reduce that kind of difference, recursing when an operator's preconditions are unmet.

GPS-style means-ends analysis (schematic)

solve(current, goal):
    if current == goal: return SUCCESS
    d  = largest_difference(current, goal)
    op = select_operator_that_reduces(d)
    if preconditions(op) not satisfied in current:
        solve(current, preconditions(op))      # recursive subgoal
    current = apply(op, current)
    return solve(current, goal)

Other landmark programs reinforced the impression of rapid progress: Arthur Samuel's checkers player, which improved by self-play; Daniel Bobrow's STUDENT, which solved algebra word problems stated in English; Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA, whose pattern-matching conversations were taken seriously by users; and Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (1970), which accepted complex English instructions about a simulated world of coloured blocks and answered questions about its own reasoning. What these systems shared was a carefully bounded universe. SHRDLU's competence depended on the fact that its world contained a handful of blocks with a handful of properties; the linguistic and inferential machinery did not survive contact with a larger domain.

The funding environment was unusually permissive. From 1963, ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office under J.C.R. Licklider funded MIT's Project MAC, Stanford and Carnegie Mellon with block grants of millions of dollars per year and almost no specified deliverables — Licklider's stated policy was to "fund people, not projects." This produced foundational infrastructure (time-sharing, networking, laboratory culture) but also insulated researchers from the discipline of demonstrating measurable progress toward stated goals, which made the eventual reckoning harsher.

The central technical misjudgement was the underestimation of combinatorial explosion. Search-based methods that succeed on small instances degrade exponentially as the problem grows. If each state offers b legal moves and a solution lies d moves away, exhaustive search examines on the order of b^d states.

Faster hardware shifts this table by a constant factor and therefore buys only a few extra levels of depth — it cannot defeat an exponential. Heuristics help enormously in specific domains, but a general-purpose heuristic that prunes reliably across arbitrary problems is, in effect, the intelligence one was trying to build. Simon and Newell had effectively assumed that generality and tractability could be achieved simultaneously; the 1960s showed that they trade off against one another.

The failure of machine translation made the limits publicly visible earliest. Post-war MT projects, heavily funded on Cold War grounds to translate Russian scientific literature, relied on bilingual dictionaries plus syntactic rearrangement rules. They foundered on ambiguity that can only be resolved with world knowledge: "the box is in the pen" requires knowing that a child's playpen, not a writing pen, can contain a box; "time flies like an arrow" admits several grammatical parses that are semantically absurd. The US National Academy of Sciences' ALPAC report (1966) concluded that MT was slower, less accurate and more expensive than human translation and saw no near-term prospect of improvement, effectively ending US government MT funding for two decades. It was the template for later, broader retrenchments.

Connectionist research suffered its own early contraction. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) attracted extravagant press coverage, but Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969) proved that a single-layer perceptron cannot compute functions that are not linearly separable — the exclusive-or being the standard example — and expressed pessimism about extending the analysis to multi-layer networks. Multi-layer networks can represent XOR, and an effective training method for them (backpropagation) was already known in other forms, but the combination of the book's authority, the absence of a widely adopted training algorithm and the scarcity of compute redirected funding toward symbolic AI for roughly fifteen years.

The first AI winter (1974–1980)

In 1973 the UK Science Research Council published Sir James Lighthill's review of artificial intelligence research. Lighthill divided the field into category A (advanced automation), category C (computer-based studies of the central nervous system) and category B (building robots and bridging the two), and argued that category B had produced nothing of value and that results in A and C had been "wholly disappointing" relative to promises. His central technical charge was precisely the combinatorial explosion: methods that worked on toy problems could not be scaled, and there was no evidence that scaling was merely a matter of time. Following a televised debate with John McCarthy, Donald Michie and Richard Gregory, the report led the UK to concentrate AI funding in a small number of institutions, closing or shrinking groups elsewhere; Edinburgh's celebrated robotics programme was a notable casualty.

In the United States the retrenchment was driven less by a single document than by policy change and disappointing deliverables. The Mansfield Amendment (1969, reinforced in subsequent appropriations) required Defense Department funding to support "mission-oriented direct research" with an identifiable military application, which ended Licklider-style undirected block grants. DARPA's Speech Understanding Research programme, which had set a specific performance target, produced Carnegie Mellon's Harpy — technically impressive, but not the flexible speech system the sponsors believed they had commissioned — and was cancelled in 1974. Money did not vanish from computing; it moved toward projects that could state what they would deliver, by when, and to whom.

Two infrastructural constraints compounded the intellectual crisis. Computing power and memory were minuscule by later standards: a DEC PDP-10, a mainstay of AI laboratories, typically offered a few hundred kilowords of memory and a few million instructions per second. Hans Moravec later observed that AI programs of the era were starved of the raw cycles their algorithms needed by something like six orders of magnitude. Systems that were conceptually sound could not be run at a useful size, which made it impossible to distinguish a bad idea from a good idea on inadequate hardware.

Second, there were no large digital datasets. The statistical, data-driven alternative that eventually superseded hand-coded rules was not merely unfashionable in 1975 — it was unavailable. There were no machine-readable corpora of tens of millions of words, no labelled image collections, no logs of user behaviour. The Brown Corpus (1964) contained about one million words and was considered a major resource. Since learning from data was impossible at scale, knowledge had to be entered by hand, and the economics of hand-entry would define the next boom and the next collapse.

The cultural consequence was rebranding. Researchers seeking grants avoided the discredited label and described their work as informatics, knowledge-based systems, machine learning, pattern recognition, cognitive systems or advanced information processing. This is a recurring feature of winters rather than a one-off: the same manoeuvre reappeared after 1993. It matters methodologically, because it means the apparent disappearance of AI from funding records overstates the true collapse in research activity.

The expert systems boom and second winter (1980–1993)

The second boom rested on a genuine strategic insight, articulated by Edward Feigenbaum: general reasoning methods are weak, but a program equipped with a large body of domain-specific knowledge can outperform a general problem solver and sometimes rival human specialists. Expert systems separated a knowledge base of IF–THEN rules from an inference engine that chained them, with an explanation facility that could report which rules fired.

Simplified MYCIN-style rule

IF   the stain of the organism is gram-positive
AND  the morphology of the organism is coccus
AND  the growth conformation of the organism is chains
THEN there is suggestive evidence (0.7) that the identity
     of the organism is streptococcus

DENDRAL inferred molecular structures from mass spectrometry data; MYCIN diagnosed bacterial infections and recommended antibiotics, performing comparably to specialists in evaluation, though it was never deployed clinically; PROSPECTOR was credited with identifying a molybdenum deposit. The commercial exemplar was Digital Equipment Corporation's XCON (originally R1), which configured VAX computer orders, growing from roughly 750 rules to well over 10,000 and reportedly saving DEC around US$25–40 million a year. By the mid-1980s hundreds of corporations had AI groups and the market for AI hardware, software and services was valued in the billions.

Geopolitics amplified the surge. In 1982 Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry launched the Fifth Generation Computer Systems project, a ten-year, roughly ¥57 billion programme to build massively parallel machines running logic programming for knowledge processing. Coming after Japanese success in automobiles and memory chips, it was read in the West as a bid for computing leadership and produced defensive responses: the United States formed the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation (MCC) consortium and DARPA launched the Strategic Computing Initiative (approximately US$1 billion over a decade); the United Kingdom created the Alvey programme (about £350 million), partially reversing the Lighthill cuts; the European Community established ESPRIT. Much of this money was allocated on competitive rather than scientific grounds, which meant it was vulnerable to the same competitive logic when the threat failed to materialise.

Four structural weaknesses then converged.

  • Brittleness. An expert system had no model of the world outside its rule set and no sense of its own boundaries. Given an input just outside its domain it did not degrade gracefully or decline to answer; it produced a confident, wrong conclusion. A medical system that reasons about infectious disease will happily diagnose an infection in a patient whose actual problem is a fracture, because nothing in the rule base represents the concept of a fracture — or, indeed, of a patient. Human experts, by contrast, fall back on general knowledge and common sense at the edges of their specialism. Douglas Lenat's Cyc project (from 1984) was an explicit attempt to codify that common-sense substrate by hand, and its enormous cost illustrated the size of the problem.
  • The knowledge acquisition bottleneck. Rules had to be elicited from human experts by knowledge engineers through interviews, protocol analysis and iterative refinement. Experts are often unable to articulate tacit, pattern-based judgement; two experts disagree; and the engineer must translate informal statements into a formalism with consistent certainty factors. Throughput of a few rules per expert-day was typical, so a 10,000-rule system represented many person-years before a line of maintenance was written. Nothing about this process became cheaper with faster hardware.
  • Maintenance cost. Knowledge decays. Tax codes change annually, product catalogues change quarterly, clinical guidelines change continually. Because rules in a large base interact in ways no one fully understands, adding or amending one rule can silently alter conclusions elsewhere, so every change requires regression testing against a body of cases. XCON's rule base required a dedicated maintenance team, and for many organisations total cost of ownership eventually exceeded the savings. This is the economic mirror of brittleness: hand-coded knowledge has a high fixed cost of creation and a high recurring cost of upkeep, and it does not amortise across domains.
  • The hardware collapse. Much of the boom's capital was tied up in specialised LISP machines from Symbolics, LISP Machines Inc. and Xerox — single-user workstations with tagged architectures and hardware support for garbage collection, costing tens of thousands of dollars each. By 1987 general-purpose workstations from Sun and Apollo, and later commodity 386-class PCs, ran good optimising LISP compilers fast enough to make the specialist machines pointless. The market, worth roughly half a billion dollars, essentially disappeared in a single year; Symbolics and its competitors failed or were absorbed. Because the LISP machine vendors were the visible face of the AI industry, their collapse was read as the collapse of AI itself, and corporate AI groups were disbanded across the sector.

DARPA's Strategic Computing Initiative was reviewed critically and redirected toward nearer-term goals; the Fifth Generation project concluded in 1992 without achieving its commercial aims, its parallel logic-programming bet having been overtaken by cheap sequential hardware and, later, by statistical methods. By 1993 "AI" had again become a word to avoid in proposals, and successful systems were described as decision support, business rules engines, data mining or simply software. Notably, much expert-system technology survived under those names — rule engines remain embedded in insurance underwriting, credit scoring, tax software and clinical decision support to this day. The winter destroyed a market and a label more completely than it destroyed the technology.

Modern Breakthroughs and Current Trends

1

Modern Breakthroughs and Current Trends

▶

The history of artificial intelligence can be read as a long argument about where intelligent behaviour should come from. For its first three decades, the dominant answer was knowledge authored by humans: experts wrote down the rules, and the machine applied them. The modern era rests on a different answer: knowledge extracted from data. The machine is given examples, an objective, and enough computation, and it induces the rules itself. This single shift — from programming behaviour to learning behaviour — produced almost every capability people now associate with AI, from spam filters and speech transcription to protein structure prediction and conversational assistants. The sections below trace that shift, the technical enablers that made it possible, the milestones that marked it publicly, and the directions currently pulling the field forward.

From rule-based systems to machine learning

A rule-based or "expert" system encodes human expertise as explicit symbolic statements — typically IF condition THEN conclusion production rules — and applies an inference engine to chain them together. MYCIN, built at Stanford in the 1970s to recommend antibiotic therapy, contained roughly 600 such rules; XCON, used by Digital Equipment Corporation to configure VAX computer systems, eventually grew to several thousand. These systems genuinely worked inside narrow, well-formalised domains, and they had a real virtue: every conclusion could be traced back through the rules that produced it.

Their weakness was structural rather than accidental. Three problems recurred:

  • The knowledge acquisition bottleneck. Every rule had to be elicited from a human expert by a knowledge engineer, debugged, and maintained. This scaled linearly with human labour, not with data or hardware.
  • Brittleness. Rule systems degraded catastrophically rather than gracefully at the edges of their competence. A medical system that had never been told about a rare interaction did not become slightly less confident; it produced a confidently wrong answer.
  • Tacit knowledge. Many tasks resist articulation entirely. No expert can write the rules that distinguish a photograph of a cat from a photograph of a dog, or that map an acoustic waveform to the word "because". Humans perform these tasks reliably without being able to explain how.

Machine learning reframes the problem. Instead of specifying the mapping from input to output, the engineer specifies a family of candidate mappings (the model class), a measure of how badly a candidate performs (the loss function), and a procedure for searching the family for a low-loss candidate (the optimiser). Tom Mitchell's classic formulation is useful here: a program learns from experience E with respect to task T and performance measure P if its performance at T, as measured by P, improves with E. Concretely, for handwritten digit recognition, T is classifying images into ten classes, E is a corpus of labelled digit images, and P is accuracy on images the system has never seen.

Three learning paradigms became dominant, distinguished chiefly by the kind of feedback signal available.

Two hybrids are worth naming because they dominate current practice. Semi-supervised learning exploits a small labelled set alongside a large unlabelled one. Self-supervised learning manufactures supervision from the data's own structure — masking a word and predicting it, or predicting the next frame of a video — which is technically supervised training with no human annotation cost. This trick is the foundation of every modern large language model.

Through the 1990s and 2000s, applied AI was overwhelmingly statistical rather than neural. Support vector machines, introduced in their modern form by Cortes and Vapnik in 1995, were prized because they optimised a convex objective (so training reliably found the global optimum), came with margin-based generalisation theory, and could model non-linear boundaries through the kernel trick — implicitly mapping data into a high-dimensional space by replacing inner products with a kernel function such as K(x, z) = exp(-γ‖x − z‖²). They dominated text categorisation and handwriting recognition. Meanwhile, decision tree ensembles proved almost unreasonably effective on the tabular, heterogeneous data typical of business and scientific problems. Random forests (Breiman, 2001) average many deep trees trained on bootstrap samples with randomly restricted feature subsets, reducing variance; boosting methods (AdaBoost, then gradient boosting, then XGBoost and LightGBM) instead fit trees sequentially, each new tree targeting the residual errors of the ensemble so far. Gradient-boosted trees remain the default winning approach on tabular Kaggle competitions to this day.

These methods shared a limitation: performance depended overwhelmingly on feature engineering. An SVM cannot classify raw pixels well, so practitioners spent their effort hand-designing descriptors — SIFT and HOG for vision, MFCCs for audio, TF-IDF n-grams for text. The learning algorithm was a thin layer on top of human-designed representations.

Crucially, the statistical turn was practical as well as intellectual. Digitisation supplied the raw material: the UCI Machine Learning Repository standardised benchmark datasets from 1987; the Penn Treebank gave computational linguistics a million syntactically annotated words; TREC and later the Netflix Prize provided large, realistic evaluation corpora. Once data existed at scale, learning from it became cheaper and more accurate than paying experts to author rules — a Canadian Institute-style speech recognition system trained on thousands of recorded hours simply outperformed any hand-written phonological rule base. This economic reversal is what allowed AI to recover from the funding contractions of the AI winters. The field stopped promising general reasoning and started delivering measurable, narrow, commercially valuable improvements: credit-card fraud detection, postal address reading, web search ranking, recommendation, and machine translation. Investment returned because results were auditable against benchmarks rather than against rhetoric.

The deep learning revolution

Deep learning uses neural networks with many stacked layers of non-linear transformation, trained end to end by gradient descent using backpropagation to compute derivatives of the loss with respect to every weight. None of the core ideas were new in 2012 — backpropagation was popularised in 1986, convolutional networks in 1989 — but a conjunction of larger datasets, GPU computation, and a handful of training tricks converted a marginal research area into the dominant paradigm.

The inflection point is usually dated to the 2012 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, where AlexNet — a convolutional network trained on two GPUs by Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton — achieved a top-5 error of about 15.3% against roughly 26% for the best hand-engineered runner-up. A gap of that size in a mature benchmark reorganised the field within eighteen months.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) work because they build the right inductive biases for images directly into the architecture:

  • Local receptive fields. Each unit sees only a small patch, reflecting the fact that visual structure is local — edges and textures do not depend on distant pixels.
  • Weight sharing. The same small filter is convolved across the whole image, so a vertical-edge detector learned in one corner works everywhere. This gives approximate translation equivariance and cuts the parameter count by orders of magnitude relative to a fully connected layer.
  • Hierarchical composition. Early layers respond to oriented edges and colour blobs; middle layers to motifs such as corners, textures and wheel-like circles; later layers to object parts and whole objects. Visualisation studies confirmed this progression empirically.

Successive architectures deepened this idea: VGG (2014) showed that stacking many 3×3 convolutions works well; Inception introduced multi-scale parallel filters; ResNet (2015) added residual skip connections, y = F(x) + x, which let gradients flow directly through the network and made 50-, 101- and even 1000-layer models trainable. ResNet's 3.57% top-5 ImageNet error crossed below commonly cited estimates of human performance on that benchmark (around 5%). CNNs then propagated into object detection (R-CNN, YOLO), semantic segmentation (U-Net, still the workhorse of biomedical imaging), and medical diagnostics.

Sequential data required different machinery. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) maintain a hidden state h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t) updated at each time step, giving them, in principle, unbounded memory. In practice, repeated multiplication during backpropagation through time caused gradients to vanish or explode, so plain RNNs could not learn long-range dependencies. Long Short-Term Memory units (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) solved this with a gated cell state — input, forget and output gates controlling what is written, retained and read — and the simpler GRU followed. Gated RNNs powered the first genuinely usable large-vocabulary speech recognisers on mobile phones and the sequence-to-sequence encoder–decoder translation systems deployed by Google in 2016, which cut translation error substantially overnight.

Recurrence still had two flaws: it forced all information about the source sequence through a single fixed-length vector, and it was inherently sequential, so it could not be parallelised across time. Attention, introduced by Bahdanau and colleagues in 2014, fixed the first by letting the decoder compute, at each output step, a weighted average over all encoder states, with weights reflecting relevance. Translating "the agreement on the European Economic Area was signed in August 1992", the decoder can attend to "August 1992" precisely when it needs to emit the date, regardless of how far back it lies. The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" then removed recurrence entirely, producing the transformer — discussed below, because its significance is as much computational as representational.

The deepest consequence of all this was the collapse of feature engineering as a discipline. Consider the change in a speech pipeline:

Stage Classical pipeline (pre-2012) Deep learning pipeline
Input processing Hand-designed MFCC features, delta and delta-delta coefficients Raw or lightly processed spectrogram / waveform
Acoustic modelling Gaussian mixture models over phone states Learned by the network
Sequence modelling Hidden Markov model with expert-authored pronunciation lexicon Learned by the network
Training Each component trained separately, then combined Single end-to-end objective (e.g. CTC or cross-entropy)
Where human effort goes Designing features and linguistic resources Designing architecture, curating data, tuning training

This is representation learning: the model discovers the intermediate abstractions that make the task solvable, rather than receiving them. Word embeddings illustrate the point vividly — word2vec and GloVe, trained only on co-occurrence statistics, place words in a vector space where semantic relations appear as geometric ones, so that vec("king") - vec("man") + vec("woman") lands near vec("queen"). Nobody specified gender or royalty as a dimension; the geometry emerged from prediction. The same principle explains transfer learning: features learned on ImageNet transfer usefully to skin-lesion classification, because early-layer edge and texture detectors are generic. A hospital with 5,000 labelled images can fine-tune a pretrained ResNet and reach accuracy that would have required millions of images from scratch.

Finally, open-source frameworks industrialised the process. Theano (2007) and Caffe (2014) pioneered GPU-backed automatic differentiation; TensorFlow (2015) brought production tooling; PyTorch (2016) won research mindshare with an imperative, define-by-run interface that made debugging feel like ordinary programming; Keras supplied a high-level API; Hugging Face Transformers turned pretrained models into a two-line import. The practical effect was enormous: a graduate student could reimplement a state-of-the-art architecture in an afternoon, and a published result could be reproduced by downloading weights rather than reconstructing a lab. Consider how little code a working image classifier now requires:

import torch, torchvision
from torch import nn

model = torchvision.models.resnet50(weights="IMAGENET1K_V2")
model.fc = nn.Linear(2048, 4)          # adapt to 4 local classes

loss_fn   = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
optimiser = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)

for images, labels in train_loader:    # fine-tune on local data
    optimiser.zero_grad()
    loss = loss_fn(model(images), labels)
    loss.backward()                    # autodiff computes all gradients
    optimiser.step()

Every hard part — the derivative of every operation, GPU kernel dispatch, a pretrained hierarchy of visual features — is supplied by the ecosystem.

The three enablers: data, compute, and algorithms

It is a useful discipline to explain modern AI as the product of three multiplying factors rather than one breakthrough. Remove any one and the others deliver little: the algorithms of 1990 on today's hardware and data would still work reasonably well, but today's algorithms on 1990's hardware and data would fail outright.

Data. Digitisation and the web created corpora of a scale no annotation project could have funded. ImageNet, assembled from 2007 by Fei-Fei Li's group, used Amazon Mechanical Turk to label roughly 14 million images across 20,000+ categories organised by WordNet — a decisive demonstration that benchmark construction is itself a research contribution. Common Crawl archives petabytes of web pages; Wikipedia supplies encyclopaedic text in hundreds of languages; GitHub supplies billions of lines of code; LibriSpeech supplies a thousand hours of transcribed audio. Just as important, self-supervised objectives made unlabelled data directly usable, so the effective training set became "most of the public internet" rather than "whatever we could afford to annotate". The costs of this shift are also real and now well documented: web-scraped corpora carry the biases, toxicity, copyright entanglements and factual errors of their sources, and data curation, filtering and deduplication have become central research problems rather than housekeeping.

Compute. Training a deep network consists overwhelmingly of dense matrix multiplications, which are embarrassingly parallel. A CPU optimised for low-latency serial execution offers tens of cores; a GPU designed for rasterising graphics offers thousands of simple arithmetic units and very high memory bandwidth. Once CUDA (2007) exposed GPUs to general-purpose programming, the effective cost of a training run fell by one to two orders of magnitude — AlexNet's decisive result was in a real sense a CUDA result. Specialised accelerators followed: Google's Tensor Processing Units, built around large systolic-array matrix units with reduced-precision arithmetic; NVIDIA tensor cores; and a range of inference-focused chips. Mixed-precision training (FP16/BF16 with selective FP32 accumulation) roughly doubled throughput again, and distributed strategies — data parallelism, tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, sharded optimiser states — allowed single runs to occupy thousands of chips for weeks. Analyses of the field estimate that compute used in notable training runs grew with a doubling time of roughly 6 months during the 2012–2018 period, vastly outpacing Moore's law, driven by spending and parallelism as much as by transistor density.

Algorithms. Deep networks were historically considered untrainable, and the fix was an accumulation of specific, unglamorous techniques:

  • Activations. Replacing saturating sigmoid/tanh units with the rectified linear unit ReLU(x) = max(0, x) kept gradients from vanishing and sped convergence several-fold.
  • Initialisation. Xavier/Glorot and He initialisation scale initial weights by layer width so that activation variance neither explodes nor collapses with depth.
  • Optimisers. Stochastic gradient descent with momentum, then adaptive methods — AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam (2014) and AdamW — maintain per-parameter learning rates, making training far less sensitive to hyperparameter guesswork. Learning-rate schedules (warm-up, cosine decay) matter nearly as much as the optimiser itself.
  • Regularisation. Dropout randomly zeroes units during training, approximating an ensemble and discouraging co-adaptation; weight decay penalises large weights; early stopping halts training when validation loss turns; data augmentation (crops, flips, colour jitter, mixup, SpecAugment) multiplies effective dataset size; label smoothing tempers overconfidence.
  • Normalisation. Batch normalisation (2015) standardises layer inputs using batch statistics, permitting far higher learning rates and much deeper stacks; layer normalisation, which normalises across features within a single example, became standard in transformers because it is independent of batch size and sequence position.
  • Residual connections. Skip paths make identity mapping the default, so adding depth cannot easily make optimisation worse.

The transformer deserves separate treatment because it resolved the compute–algorithm interaction. Its core operation, scaled dot-product attention, computes for each position a query, key and value vector and mixes values weighted by query–key similarity:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (Q K^T) / sqrt(d_k) ) V

Because every position attends to every other position in a single matrix operation, the whole sequence is processed in parallel. This has two consequences. First, the path length between any two tokens is constant rather than proportional to their distance, so long-range dependencies are learned far more easily than by an RNN. Second, and decisively for scaling, training saturates modern accelerators: a transformer converts available FLOPs into learning efficiently, whereas an RNN idles waiting for the previous time step. Multi-head attention runs several attention functions in parallel subspaces so different heads can specialise (syntactic agreement, coreference, positional patterns), and because attention itself is order-agnostic, positional encodings — sinusoidal originally, rotary or learned in later systems — inject sequence order. The quadratic cost in sequence length remains the architecture's main weakness and motivates ongoing work on sparse, linear and state-space alternatives such as Mamba. Transformers subsequently absorbed vision (Vision Transformer), audio, protein sequences and reinforcement learning, becoming an unusually general-purpose substrate.

Cloud infrastructure supplied the final piece by converting capital expenditure into operating expenditure. Before roughly 2010, training at scale required owning a cluster, which effectively restricted the frontier to well-funded universities and corporate labs. Rentable GPU instances, managed training services and free hosted notebooks (Colab, Kaggle kernels) meant a two-person startup, a student in a country with no supercomputing centre, or a small hospital research group could access the same hardware by the hour. This democratisation is genuinely partial, however: renting eight GPUs for a fine-tuning job is accessible, while the tens of thousands of accelerators needed to pretrain a frontier model from scratch are not, which is precisely why access to compute has become a strategic and regulatory concern.

Landmark milestones

Public perception of AI has been shaped less by papers than by a handful of dramatic, legible demonstrations. Each is worth examining for what it actually proved — and what it did not.

Application Domains of AI

1

Application Domains of AI

▶

Artificial intelligence stops being an abstraction the moment it is embedded in a hospital workflow, a payments network, a delivery vehicle, a classroom platform, or a design studio. Each of these settings imposes its own data conditions, error tolerances, regulatory constraints, and human expectations, and each one reveals a different face of the same underlying technology. A model that is celebrated as "superhuman" on a benchmark dataset may be unusable in a clinic because it was never validated on the local patient population; a fraud detector with an excellent overall accuracy may still be commercially useless if its false-positive rate blocks too many legitimate customers. Studying application domains therefore does two things at once: it shows what AI is genuinely good at, and it exposes the boundaries of that competence with far more precision than any general argument about machine intelligence could.

A useful way to read the domains below is to ask four questions of each deployment. First, what task is actually being automated — is it perception, prediction, ranking, generation, or control? Second, what is the cost structure of errors — is a mistake an annoyance, a financial loss, or a fatality? Third, who is accountable when the system is wrong? And fourth, what is the role of the human — is the system advisory, supervised, or fully autonomous? These questions recur in every sector, and the answers explain most of the difference between AI projects that succeed and those that quietly fail after a pilot.

AI in Healthcare and Medicine

Medicine has been an early and intense adopter of machine learning because much of clinical work involves pattern recognition over high-dimensional data — precisely the task at which modern deep learning excels. Medical imaging is the flagship example. Convolutional neural networks and, more recently, vision transformers are trained on large labelled archives of radiographs, CT and MRI volumes, digitised pathology slides, and dermatoscopic photographs. The output is typically a probability score or a heat-map-style localisation indicating where an anomaly may lie: a suspicious pulmonary nodule on a chest CT, microcalcification clusters on a mammogram, mitotic figures in a histology tile, or an asymmetric pigmented lesion on skin. Well-known deployment patterns include triage (reordering a radiologist's worklist so that likely-positive studies are read first), second-reader support (the model reviews studies a human has already cleared, flagging possible misses), and quantification (automatically measuring tumour volume or cardiac ejection fraction more consistently than manual tracing). Crucially, in almost every regulated market these tools are positioned as computer-aided detection for clinician review, not as autonomous diagnosticians. The model narrows attention; a licensed clinician still makes and signs the diagnosis.

Predictive analytics on electronic health records (EHRs) works on a very different data type: sparse, irregular, time-stamped tabular streams of vital signs, laboratory results, medication orders, nursing notes, and billing codes. Gradient-boosted trees and recurrent or transformer-based sequence models are trained to answer questions such as: which inpatients are likely to meet sepsis criteria in the next six hours; which discharged patients are at high risk of 30-day readmission; which ward patients are deteriorating and should be escalated to intensive care. Early-warning scores of this kind can genuinely shorten the time to antibiotics in sepsis, which is directly linked to survival. They also illustrate a characteristic failure mode: a sepsis alert with high sensitivity but modest positive predictive value produces so many alarms that nursing staff begin to ignore them — "alert fatigue" — and the clinical benefit evaporates even though the model's statistics look acceptable. Deployment quality, not only model quality, determines outcomes.

Drug discovery uses machine learning further upstream, where the search space is astronomically large and laboratory experiments are slow and expensive. Virtual screening models rank millions of candidate molecules by predicted binding affinity to a target protein, predicted solubility, or predicted toxicity, so that chemists synthesise only the most promising few. Generative models propose novel molecular structures conditioned on desired properties. The most celebrated advance is in protein structure prediction: systems in the AlphaFold family predict three-dimensional protein folds from amino-acid sequence with accuracy that in many cases approaches experimental methods, collapsing a problem that once consumed years of crystallography into hours of computation. This has changed the starting point for structure-based drug design across biology. It has not, however, removed the need for wet-lab validation, animal studies, or clinical trials — the expensive, slow, failure-prone end of the pipeline remains intact, which is why AI has accelerated discovery far more visibly than it has accelerated approvals.

The limitations in healthcare are severe and well documented. Models inherit the demographics of their training archives. A dermatology classifier trained overwhelmingly on images of light skin performs measurably worse on darker skin tones, because the visual presentation of erythema, melanoma, and inflammatory conditions differs; a pulse-oximetry-derived feature set can encode a known measurement bias; a cardiovascular risk model trained in one country's health system may transfer poorly to another population with different baseline prevalence. There is also dataset shift in the mechanical sense: change the scanner vendor, the slide-staining protocol, or the image reconstruction kernel, and performance can drop sharply even for the same disease in the same population. Models can also learn shortcuts — famously, detecting a chest drain or a portable-scanner marker that correlates with illness rather than the pathology itself, which works beautifully in the test set and fails in a screening clinic where those cues are absent.

Finally, regulation and liability constrain autonomy. Clinical AI is generally regulated as a medical device, requiring evidence of safety and effectiveness for a defined intended use and patient population; approvals are narrow, and substantially modifying a model can require new evidence, which complicates continuous learning from live data. Liability is unsettled: if a supervised model misses a cancer that a radiologist then also misses, responsibility may fall on the clinician who signed the report, the hospital that procured the tool, or the manufacturer whose labelling defined its limits. Because no actor wants to absorb that uncertainty, the prevailing model remains "AI proposes, clinician disposes," and fully autonomous medical AI is confined to a handful of tightly bounded tasks (for example, autonomous screening for diabetic retinopathy in specified settings).

AI in Finance and Business

Finance was an early adopter of machine learning for three reasons: the data is digital and abundant, outcomes are measurable in currency, and the feedback loop is fast. Fraud and anomaly detection is the canonical use case. A card network may authorise tens of thousands of transactions per second, each of which must be scored in milliseconds. Models combine supervised learning on labelled historical fraud with unsupervised anomaly detection for novel attack patterns, using features such as amount relative to the cardholder's history, merchant category, geolocation velocity (a card physically present in two cities minutes apart), device fingerprint, and graph features linking accounts, devices, and beneficiaries. The class imbalance is extreme — genuine fraud may be well under one percent of transactions — so raw accuracy is meaningless and evaluation uses precision–recall trade-offs tuned to business cost: each undetected fraud costs the issuer money, but each wrongly declined purchase costs customer goodwill. Fraud is also adversarial: attackers adapt to the detector, so models require continual retraining, which is a structural difference from a domain like radiology where the underlying pathology does not strategically respond to being detected.

Credit scoring uses applicant attributes, bureau history, and increasingly behavioural or transactional data to estimate probability of default. Machine-learning scorecards can extend credit to "thin-file" applicants whom traditional models reject, which is a genuine inclusion benefit. But this is also where the fairness problem bites hardest: a model may not use a protected attribute such as race or gender directly, yet still learn proxies — postcode, shopping categories, device type, the school attended — that reconstruct it statistically, a phenomenon called proxy discrimination. Because consumer lending law in many jurisdictions requires that an applicant who is denied credit be given the specific principal reasons for the decision, an unexplainable gradient-boosted ensemble is not merely inconvenient; it is potentially non-compliant. This drives practical use of interpretable-by-design scorecards, monotonicity constraints, and post-hoc explanation methods such as SHAP values, together with disparate-impact testing across demographic groups.

Algorithmic and high-frequency trading uses AI in a fundamentally different way: not to advise a human but to act autonomously at machine timescales. Execution algorithms slice large orders to minimise market impact; statistical arbitrage models predict short-horizon relative price movements; market-making systems continuously quote both sides of a book while managing inventory risk. Latency is measured in microseconds, so decisions cannot involve a person, and risk control must itself be automated through position limits, kill switches, and circuit breakers. The systemic hazard is that many autonomous agents optimising on correlated signals can interact to produce instability — the 2010 "flash crash" is the standard illustration of liquidity evaporating within minutes through interacting automated behaviour.

Natural language processing converts unstructured business text into tradable or actionable signals: sentiment and tone extracted from news wires, event extraction from regulatory filings (a change in auditor, a going-concern warning, a new legal proceeding), summarisation of earnings-call transcripts including analysis of management language, and automated screening of contracts or know-your-customer documentation. Large language models have made these pipelines far easier to build, while adding the risk that a plausible-sounding but hallucinated extraction propagates into a decision. In broader business operations, the same technology underpins demand forecasting, dynamic pricing, churn prediction, recommendation, supply-chain optimisation, and customer-service automation.

AI in Transportation and Robotics

Autonomous driving and mobile robotics are best understood as a layered pipeline, because the engineering problem is not a single model but an integrated stack in which errors compound. The perception layer takes raw sensor data and produces a structured model of the world: where the drivable surface is, what objects exist, what class they belong to, where they are moving and how fast. Sensor fusion is central because each modality has complementary failure modes, as summarised below.

Fusion can be late (each sensor produces detections that are then reconciled by tracking and probabilistic filtering) or early/mid-level (raw or feature-level data from several sensors is fed into one neural network that outputs a unified scene representation such as a bird's-eye-view occupancy grid). Modern stacks lean towards learned fusion with temporal tracking, producing object tracks with predicted future trajectories — because the planner does not only need to know that a cyclist is there, but where the cyclist will be in three seconds.

The prediction, planning, and control layers then turn that world model into motion. Behaviour prediction estimates the intentions of other agents (will that pedestrian at the kerb step out? is the vehicle drifting left about to change lanes?). A behavioural planner selects a manoeuvre — follow lane, change lane, yield at an unprotected left turn, creep forward at an occluded junction. A motion planner generates a smooth, kinematically feasible trajectory that respects comfort limits and safety margins, often by optimising a cost function over sampled candidate paths. Finally, a low-level controller (classically a PID or model-predictive controller) issues steering, throttle, and brake commands to track that trajectory despite tyre slip and actuator lag. Note the hybrid character: learning dominates perception and prediction, while classical optimisation and control theory still dominate the safety-critical actuation layer, precisely because its behaviour can be bounded and analysed.

In warehouse and industrial robotics, the same components appear with a friendlier environment. Autonomous mobile robots navigate fulfilment centres using SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping), fiducial markers, or floor-embedded codes, with fleet-level schedulers resolving traffic and assigning tasks — a large-scale multi-agent coordination problem. Robotic picking uses learned grasp-pose detection from depth images to handle the enormous variety of consumer items, a task where humans remain better at deformable, cluttered, or fragile objects; this is an instance of Moravec's paradox, in which dexterous manipulation proves harder to automate than symbolic reasoning. Collaborative robots ("cobots") share space with people, using force-torque sensing, speed-and-separation monitoring, and vision-based human pose tracking so that they slow or stop when a worker enters a zone. Predictive maintenance models on vibration and thermal data extend the same pattern-recognition capability to the machinery itself.

The dominant obstacle to full autonomy is the long tail of edge cases. Driving is statistically dominated by ordinary situations that a competent stack handles easily, but the distribution has an extremely long tail of rare events: a mattress falling from a truck, a police officer hand-signalling against a red light, a horse on a motorway, a mobility scooter crossing at night in the rain, reflections of a traffic light on a wet tanker, a flooded underpass, unmapped roadworks with contradictory temporary markings. Each is individually improbable, but collectively such events occur constantly across a large fleet, and no training set can enumerate them in advance. Because a human driver in many jurisdictions travels on the order of a hundred thousand miles between injury crashes and far more between fatal ones, statistically demonstrating that an autonomous system is safer than a human requires hundreds of millions of miles of representative driving — which is why the field relies on simulation, scenario libraries, adversarial scenario generation, and structured safety-case arguments rather than road mileage alone.

This leads directly to verification standards. Safety-critical deployment is governed by frameworks such as functional-safety standards for electrical and electronic systems (ISO 26262-style hazard analysis and risk assessment, with integrity levels driving design rigour) and "safety of the intended functionality" standards addressing hazards arising from performance limitations rather than component faults. Requirements include redundancy and graceful degradation (a minimal-risk manoeuvre if a sensor fails), deterministic real-time behaviour, exhaustive requirement traceability, independent assessment, and continuous field monitoring. None of this maps neatly onto a statistical model whose behaviour is defined by weights learned from data and which cannot be exhaustively specified — the core research problem of assuring machine learning in safety-critical systems. Compare the norms: a web application ships when tests pass and can be rolled back after failure; a braking-intervention function must be argued to be safe before exposure, because its first failure may be irreversible.

AI in Education

Adaptive learning platforms treat instruction as a sequencing problem. As a learner answers questions, the system updates an estimate of their ability or mastery and chooses the next item accordingly — harder if they are succeeding, easier or remedial if they are not, and spaced for review if the estimate suggests forgetting. Techniques range from item-response theory and computerised adaptive testing (where each item is chosen to be maximally informative about the learner's ability, so a reliable estimate is reached in far fewer questions than a fixed test) to knowledge-tracing models over prerequisite graphs and spaced-repetition schedulers. The practical benefit is efficiency: students do not grind through material they have mastered, and do not stall on material for which they lack prerequisites.

Intelligent tutoring systems go further by maintaining an explicit student model — a representation of what the learner knows, misunderstands, and is likely to do next. Classical cognitive tutors combine a domain model (an expert solver that can produce correct steps), a student model (Bayesian knowledge tracing or similar, estimating mastery of each fine-grained skill), and a pedagogical model (rules about when to hint, when to withhold help, and when to escalate difficulty). Because the domain model can solve the problem itself, the tutor can diagnose which step went wrong and deliver targeted feedback on the specific misconception — for example, recognising that a student subtracting fractions has added denominators rather than finding a common one, and responding to that error rather than merely marking the answer wrong. Evaluations of well-designed tutors in mathematics and physics have shown learning gains comparable to substantial additional instructional time, which is the strongest empirical case for AI in education.

Automated assessment tackles scale. Auto-grading of code (running unit tests, static analysis, plagiarism and similarity detection) and of short constructed responses allows a class of thousands to receive immediate formative feedback. Essay scoring models trained on human-rated samples can predict a rubric score and generate comments about structure, coherence, or evidence use. The known weaknesses are important: such models often score surface features (length, lexical sophistication, discourse markers) that correlate with quality rather than judging the argument itself, which means they can be gamed by verbose or formulaic writing and can misjudge unconventional but excellent responses. The defensible use is therefore formative feedback and drafting support, or high-volume screening with human moderation of borderline and appealed cases, rather than unsupervised high-stakes grading.

Large language models have added a general-purpose conversational layer: a student can request an explanation at a chosen level, ask for a worked analogue of a problem, get code debugged, practise a language dialogue, or have a dense text summarised. The pedagogical promise is Socratic tutoring available on demand; the pedagogical risk is that the model supplies the answer rather than developing the capability, short-circuiting the productive struggle that drives learning. Tuned "tutor mode" configurations that withhold final answers and ask guiding questions are an attempt to address this. Models also hallucinate — inventing citations, misstating theorems, producing subtly wrong code — which is particularly dangerous for a novice who lacks the domain knowledge to detect the error.

The concerns in education cluster into three groups. Academic integrity has been destabilised because fluent text and working code can be generated in seconds, while AI-detection tools are unreliable and are known to flag non-native writers disproportionately; institutions have responded by redesigning assessment towards process evidence, oral defence, supervised work, and drafting histories rather than relying on detection. Data protection is unusually stringent because learners are often minors, and platforms accumulate fine-grained behavioural records — keystroke timings, attention proxies, error patterns — that can be used for profiling well beyond the original educational purpose; special regimes for children's data restrict collection, retention, and secondary use. Finally, over-reliance is a genuine skills risk: if feedback is always instantaneous and answers always available, learners may not develop self-monitoring, tolerance for difficulty, or independent judgement, and instructors may lose the diagnostic insight they would have gained from reading student work themselves.

AI in Creative and Generative Work

Generative systems differ from the predictive systems discussed so far in a fundamental way: there is no single correct output, so correctness is replaced by a mixture of plausibility, usefulness, and taste. Large language models, trained to predict the next token over vast text corpora and then aligned with instruction tuning and human preference feedback, now perform drafting, summarisation, translation, rewriting for audience and register, structured extraction, and editing. Typical professional workflows treat the model as a fast first-draft generator or a tireless editor: producing a release note from a change log, condensing a hundred-page report into a briefing, translating documentation into a dozen languages for human post-editing, or rephrasing a paragraph for a lay audience.

Diffusion models dominate image generation. Their training procedure adds noise progressively to real images and teaches a network to reverse that process; at generation time the model begins from pure noise and denoises step by step towards an image, conditioned on a text embedding so that the result matches a prompt. Extensions give practitioners control: inpainting replaces a region of an existing image, image-to-image translation restyles a photograph, depth- or pose-conditioning constrains composition, and lightweight fine-tuning teaches a specific character, product, or house style. In practice this has been adopted fastest for pre-final assets — mood boards, storyboards, concept art, marketing variants, texture generation, background replacement — where iteration speed matters more than perfect fidelity. Related systems generate music, speech, and video with similar architectures.

Code generation assistants apply the same next-token machinery to source code, using the surrounding file, imports, related files, and natural-language comments

Ethical Considerations in AI Practice

1

Ethical Considerations in AI Practice

▶

Ethics is no longer a peripheral concern bolted onto artificial intelligence after the engineering is finished. It has become a core design discipline, sitting alongside accuracy, latency and cost as a property that determines whether a system is fit to deploy at all. The reason is straightforward: AI systems now make or shape consequential decisions about credit, employment, medical treatment, policing, immigration, insurance and education, and they do so at a speed and volume that no human bureaucracy could match. This topic examines the substantive ethical problems that arise in practice — bias, opacity, privacy intrusion, diffuse accountability and labour displacement — and then surveys the governance frameworks and working methods that organisations use to manage them.

Why Ethics Became Central to AI

Four structural features of modern AI explain why ethical questions have moved from philosophy seminars into engineering sprints.

Scale amplifies harm. A biased human loan officer might process thirty applications a day and affect a few thousand people over a career. A single credit-scoring model deployed at a national bank can process millions of applications in a week, applying exactly the same flaw to every one of them with perfect consistency. Consistency is normally a virtue; when the rule being applied consistently is wrong, it becomes a mechanism for industrialised harm. In 2019, the Apple Card was widely reported to have offered substantially lower credit limits to women than to men with comparable or identical financial profiles, including within married couples filing jointly. Whatever the underlying cause, the episode illustrates the point: the defect was not confined to one branch office, it was replicated across the entire customer base instantaneously. Scale also compresses the timeline for detection — by the time a pattern of harm is visible in complaint data, hundreds of thousands of decisions may already have been made.

Machine learning induces patterns; it does not reason from principles. A rule-based expert system encodes propositions that a human author consciously wrote down: IF applicant_age < 18 THEN reject. A supervised learning model, by contrast, is handed a corpus of past decisions and instructed to find whatever statistical regularities predict the labels. It has no concept of justice, relevance or desert. If the historical record shows that a particular group was systematically declined, the model will treat that as signal, because from a purely predictive standpoint it is signal. The model is optimising a loss function, not pursuing a normative goal. This is the fundamental gap between prediction and legitimacy, and it cannot be closed by more data or larger models alone.

Opacity makes harms harder to detect and contest. A rule-based system can be audited by reading it. A large neural network cannot. When a decision emerges from the interaction of hundreds of millions or billions of weights, there is typically no human-readable rule to point to, no single feature to blame, and no simple counterfactual to offer the person affected. The practical consequence is that a person denied a mortgage may be unable to identify what they would have to change to succeed next time, and a regulator may be unable to determine whether a protected characteristic influenced the outcome. Contestability — the ability of an affected person to challenge a decision — depends on some account of the reasoning, and opacity removes it.

Ethical failure is also legal, reputational and financial failure. Framing ethics as a purely moral overlay understates the institutional stakes. The Dutch childcare benefits scandal (toeslagenaffaire), in which a risk-scoring system helped wrongly accuse roughly 26,000 families of fraud and drove many into severe financial distress, contributed to the resignation of the entire Dutch government in January 2021. Amazon abandoned an experimental CV-screening tool after discovering it penalised CVs containing the word "women's". Regulators have since acquired hard powers: under the EU AI Act, penalties for prohibited practices reach up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, and GDPR penalties reach 4%. Ethical diligence is therefore continuous with risk management, not separate from it.

Bias and Fairness in AI Systems

"Bias" in AI is used in two distinct senses, and confusing them causes endless argument. Statistical bias means a systematic deviation of an estimator from the true value. Social bias means a systematic disadvantage imposed on a group in a way that is morally or legally objectionable. A model can be statistically unbiased with respect to the historical data and still be socially biased, precisely because the historical data recorded unjust practice.

Historical bias arises when the world the data describes was itself discriminatory. Predictive policing systems trained on arrest records are a canonical example: arrests measure police activity as much as criminal activity, so neighbourhoods historically subject to heavy patrolling generate more records, the model directs more patrols there, and the resulting arrests confirm the prediction. This is a feedback loop that manufactures its own evidence. Note that no data-collection error occurred — the records are accurate. The problem is that the measured quantity (arrests) is a biased proxy for the quantity of interest (offending).

Sampling and representation bias arises when the training set underrepresents populations or contexts in which the system will be used. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru's 2018 "Gender Shades" study evaluated commercial facial analysis products and found error rates below 1% for lighter-skinned men but above 30% for darker-skinned women — a direct consequence of benchmark datasets dominated by light-skinned faces. The same pattern recurs in medicine: dermatology models trained largely on light skin misclassify lesions on darker skin; pulse oximeters, though not AI, illustrate the analogous calibration problem in sensing. Related failure modes include measurement bias (the recorded feature imperfectly captures the concept, as when healthcare spending is used as a proxy for healthcare need, understating need among groups that historically received less care) and deployment bias (a model built for one context is used in another, such as a triage tool validated in a teaching hospital deployed in rural clinics).

Proxy variables defeat the naive remedy of simply deleting protected attributes. Because societies are residentially and economically segregated, postcode correlates strongly with ethnicity; shopping patterns, device type, name, first language, secondary school attended and even typing cadence can all carry information about protected characteristics. A model with sufficient capacity will reconstruct the protected attribute from its correlates — a phenomenon sometimes called redundant encoding. "Fairness through unawareness" therefore offers very little protection, and it has the perverse effect of making disparate impact harder to measure, since the organisation no longer holds the demographic data needed to test for it.

Competing fairness definitions cannot be jointly satisfied. This is a mathematical result, not a matter of insufficient effort. The ProPublica investigation of the COMPAS recidivism tool in 2016 argued that Black defendants were roughly twice as likely to be falsely flagged as high risk, while the vendor responded that the tool was equally calibrated — a given risk score meant the same reoffending probability regardless of race. Both claims were true. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, and independently Chouldechova, proved that when base rates differ between groups, calibration, equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates cannot all hold simultaneously except in degenerate cases. Choosing a fairness criterion is therefore an irreducibly normative act that should be made explicitly, documented, and justified with reference to the harms that matter most in the specific domain.

Demographic (statistical) parity Selection rates are equal across groups Equal access to a benefit such as interviews or loans Ignores genuine differences in the outcome variable; may force unqualified selections

Mitigation operates at three stages. Pre-processing improves the data: targeted collection to fill representation gaps, reweighting underrepresented strata, relabelling where historical labels are known to be contaminated. In-processing modifies training: adding a fairness penalty to the loss function, adversarial debiasing in which a second network tries to predict the protected attribute from the representation and the main network is trained to defeat it. Post-processing adjusts outputs: group-specific decision thresholds, or reject-option classification in which borderline cases are routed to human review. Cutting across all three is disaggregated evaluation: never report a single aggregate accuracy figure. Break performance down by gender, age band, ethnicity, disability status, language, geography and relevant intersections, because an overall accuracy of 95% can conceal 60% accuracy for a subgroup that constitutes 3% of the test set. Finally, bias is not a defect you fix once. Populations shift, behaviour adapts to the model, and data pipelines change upstream without notice, so drift monitoring and periodic re-audit after deployment are indispensable.

Transparency and Explainability

Transparency is the broader property — disclosure of what a system is, what data it used, who built it and how it is evaluated. Explainability is narrower: producing an account of why a particular output was generated. A system can be transparent without being explainable (you may know exactly which architecture and dataset were used and still not know why case 4,712 was rejected), and conversely a vendor may supply plausible explanations while concealing the training data entirely.

The technical difficulty is real. A contemporary deep network distributes its decision across an enormous parameter space, with no module corresponding to a legible rule. There is no line to read. Two families of response have emerged. Post-hoc explanation methods approximate the model's behaviour locally or attribute credit to inputs: LIME fits a simple interpretable model in the neighbourhood of one prediction; SHAP assigns each feature a contribution value derived from cooperative game theory; saliency and attention maps highlight influential pixels or tokens; counterfactual explanations state the smallest change that would flip the outcome ("your application would have succeeded with an annual income of £34,000 rather than £31,500"). Counterfactuals are often the most useful to an affected person because they are actionable. But post-hoc methods have a known weakness: they explain a surrogate, not the model itself, and can be unstable or even manipulated to look innocuous.

Inherently interpretable models are the alternative. Cynthia Rudin has argued forcefully that for high-stakes decisions, organisations should stop explaining black boxes and instead build models that are interpretable by construction — sparse scoring systems, short decision lists, generalised additive models. The frequently assumed accuracy–interpretability trade-off is often smaller than expected on structured tabular data, and where a 0.5% accuracy gain is bought with total opacity in a sentencing or child-protection context, the trade is difficult to defend. A simple points-based clinical score that a doctor can sanity-check and override may produce better real-world outcomes than a marginally superior model that clinicians either blindly trust or quietly ignore.

A right to explanation. GDPR Article 22 restricts decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, and Articles 13–15 together with Recital 71 establish rights to meaningful information about the logic involved and to contest the decision and obtain human intervention. Legal scholars dispute how strong this right is in practice, but the direction of travel is clear, and sectoral rules go further: US lenders must supply adverse action notices with specific reasons, and the EU AI Act imposes explanation duties for high-risk systems. Ethically, the case rests on contestability and dignity: a person subject to a consequential decision is owed reasons they can examine and challenge, not merely an outcome.

Documentation practices institutionalise transparency without requiring mathematical interpretability. Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) accompany a released model with a concise statement of intended use, out-of-scope uses, training data provenance, evaluation results disaggregated by relevant groups, ethical considerations and known limitations. Datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2018) do the same for data: motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, consent status, recommended and discouraged uses. Privacy and Data Governance

Machine learning is data-hungry, and the data is frequently about people. Four distinct privacy problems recur.

Purpose drift and the limits of consent. Data is typically collected under a specific purpose — delivering a service, processing a transaction, supporting a clinical episode — and consent, where it exists, is tied to that purpose. Later, the same data is attractive as training material. The Royal Free NHS Trust's 2015–16 transfer of 1.6 million patient records to DeepMind for an acute kidney injury application was found by the UK Information Commissioner to lack an appropriate legal basis, because patients could not reasonably have expected their records to be used that way. Consent mechanisms are also weak in practice: notices are long, choices are bundled, defaults are permissive, and no individual can meaningfully anticipate all future inferential uses of their data. GDPR's purpose limitation principle exists precisely to constrain this drift, and data minimisation — collect only what is necessary — is the complementary discipline.

Models leak. A trained model is a lossy but sometimes surprisingly faithful compression of its training data. Membership inference attacks determine whether a particular record was in the training set, which alone can be disclosive if the dataset is "patients treated for HIV". Model inversion reconstructs representative inputs, such as recognisable face images, from model outputs. Memorisation is acute in large language models: Carlini and colleagues demonstrated extraction of verbatim training sequences, including names, phone numbers and addresses, from production-scale models, with memorisation increasing with model size and with the number of times a sequence appeared in the corpus. The practical implication is that releasing a model can constitute a form of data release, and should be governed accordingly.

Anonymisation is fragile under aggregation. Removing names and identifiers does not make a record anonymous if the remaining attributes are jointly unique. Latanya Sweeney showed in the late 1990s that ZIP code, date of birth and sex alone uniquely identify a large majority of the US population, and used this to re-identify the Massachusetts governor's medical record in a "de-identified" release. The Netflix Prize dataset was re-identified by correlating anonymous film ratings with public IMDb reviews. Aggregated fitness-tracker heatmaps published by Strava in 2018 revealed the layout and patrol routes of military bases. The general lesson is that privacy is a property of a dataset in its informational environment, not an intrinsic property of the file, and each additional linkable dataset erodes it.

Privacy-preserving techniques reduce but do not eliminate these risks:

  • Federated learning trains a shared model by sending model updates rather than raw data from devices or institutions; useful for mobile keyboards and multi-hospital collaborations, though gradients themselves can leak information and usually require additional protection.
  • Differential privacy adds calibrated noise so that the presence or absence of any single individual changes the output distribution by at most a bounded factor, quantified by the privacy budget ε. It provides a rigorous, composable guarantee; the cost is accuracy, borne disproportionately by underrepresented subgroups, creating a genuine privacy–fairness tension.
  • Synthetic data generated from a fitted model can support development and testing, but a generator trained without privacy guarantees can reproduce outliers almost verbatim, so synthesis is not automatically privacy-preserving.
  • Secure computation — homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, trusted execution environments — allows computation on data that is never exposed in plaintext, at substantial computational cost.

Surveillance and proportionality. Some applications are privacy-problematic even when technically well governed. Live facial recognition in public space, emotion inference, workplace keystroke and productivity monitoring, and predictive risk scoring of welfare claimants all impose diffuse costs on people who never consented and cannot opt out. The relevant tests are legality, necessity and proportionality: is there a clear legal basis, is the intrusion necessary for a legitimate aim, and is the harm to civil liberties proportionate to the benefit? Chilling effects matter — people behave differently when they believe they are watched, which affects assembly, protest and free expression. The EU AI Act's prohibitions on untargeted facial-image scraping, social scoring and most real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces reflect a judgement that certain uses fail the proportionality test regardless of accuracy.

Accountability and Responsibility

When an AI system contributes to harm, it is often unclear who is answerable. Helen Nissenbaum's "problem of many hands" describes this: responsibility is distributed across data collectors, model developers, integrators, procuring organisations, operators and regulators, and each can plausibly point elsewhere.

Illustrative cases. In the 2018 Uber test-vehicle fatality in Tempe, Arizona, the perception system detected the pedestrian but cycled between classifications and the emergency braking function had been disabled to reduce erratic behaviour; the safety driver was streaming video. The National Transportation Safety Board distributed criticism across the driver, the company's deficient safety culture, the regulator and the pedestrian. The safety driver was later charged; the company was not criminally prosecuted. In medicine, if a diagnostic model misses a malignancy, is the clinician negligent for trusting it, negligent for overriding it in a different case, or entitled to rely on a regulator-approved device? The vendor may disclaim liability through terms of use; the hospital may argue it used an approved tool as intended. Without explicit allocation, the person harmed faces a wall of mutual deferral.

Meaningful human oversight is the most commonly proposed safeguard and the most commonly hollowed out. Placing a human "in the loop" achieves nothing if that human lacks the time, information, authority or incentive to dissent. Well-documented failure modes include automation bias (humans over-trust machine output, especially when it is confident and numerically precise), moral crumple zones (Madeleine Elish's term for operators who absorb blame for failures of a system they could not realistically control), and rubber-stamping under throughput targets. Oversight is meaningful only when the reviewer sees the inputs and an explanation, has enough time per case, is measured on decision quality rather than volume, experiences no penalty for overriding the system, and is periodically tested with cases where the model is known to be wrong. Designing the override path and then measuring how often it is used is a practical diagnostic: an override rate of zero is a warning sign, not a success metric.

Audit trails and logging make retrospective investigation possible. A defensible log records the model version and its hash, the input features as presented, the output score and any threshold applied, the identity of the human reviewer and their action, the timestamp, and the data sources consulted. The EU AI Act requires automatic record-keeping for high-risk systems for exactly this reason.

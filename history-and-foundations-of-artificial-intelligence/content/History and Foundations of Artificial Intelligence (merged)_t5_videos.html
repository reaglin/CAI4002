Introduction

Artificial intelligence has never advanced at a steady pace. Its history is better described as a series of cycles: a breakthrough or a bold demonstration generates excitement, funding and press coverage inflate expectations far beyond what the technology can deliver, and when the promised results fail to arrive, money and credibility drain away. The periods of retreat that followed the booms of the 1960s and the 1980s are known as the AI winters, and they reshaped which research directions survived and which were abandoned.

This topic traces that pattern through four phases: the first wave of optimism after the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, the first winter of roughly 1974 to 1980, the expert systems boom and the second collapse of the late 1980s and early 1990s, and the renewal driven by cheap compute, large datasets and statistical learning. In each case there are both technical causes (combinatorial explosion, brittle knowledge bases, limits on hardware and data) and economic or political causes (funding reviews, defense priorities, collapsing hardware markets) that are worth separating in your own notes.

As you watch, pay attention to who made the promises, who paid for the research, and what specific evidence triggered the loss of confidence. Try to identify at least two technical limitations and two economic or institutional factors for each downturn, and ask yourself which of today's claims about AI capability would sound familiar to a researcher in 1973 or 1988.

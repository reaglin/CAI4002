Introduction

Almost every AI system you have used — a spam filter, a route planner, a chess engine, a chatbot — was built to do a bounded set of tasks. That is what people mean by weak or narrow AI: competence within a domain, with no requirement that the system understand anything or transfer its skill elsewhere. Strong AI, often discussed today under the label artificial general intelligence (AGI), is the much larger aspiration of a system that can learn and reason across arbitrary domains the way a person can, and — in the stronger philosophical reading — one that genuinely understands rather than merely simulates understanding.

This distinction matters in this course for two reasons. First, it is a classification tool: you should be able to look at a real deployed system and say where it sits and why, using capability criteria rather than marketing language. Second, it carries philosophical weight. Arguments like Searle's Chinese Room turn on whether behaving intelligently is the same thing as being intelligent, and that question shapes how we talk about machine consciousness, moral status, and risk.

As you watch, keep three questions open. What criteria does each speaker actually use to separate narrow from general systems? Do they treat the boundary as a hard line or as a spectrum of increasing generality? And where do they place today's large language models — a point on which thoughtful people genuinely disagree. Note the vocabulary differences between sources too; terms like narrow, general, and superintelligent are not used identically everywhere.

This IBM Technology video takes the discussion one step past AGI to artificial superintelligence — hypothetical systems that would exceed human capability across essentially all domains.

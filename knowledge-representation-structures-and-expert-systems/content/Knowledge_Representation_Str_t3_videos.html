Introduction

Logic-based representations are precise, but they say little about how knowledge is organized. Frames and scripts take a different approach: they bundle everything typically known about an object, situation, or event into a single named structure made of slots (attributes) and fillers (values). A "restaurant" frame, for example, might carry slots for cuisine, price range, and opening hours, while a "restaurant script" captures the usual sequence of entering, ordering, eating, and paying. These structures are the bridge between raw symbolic logic and the chunked, stereotyped knowledge people actually use when they understand a story or make a quick judgment.

In this module the ideas matter for two reasons. First, frames are the direct ancestor of the object-oriented and ontological representations used in modern knowledge engineering: slots have facets (metadata such as type restrictions, allowed values, or attached procedures), frames are arranged in is-a hierarchies, and child frames inherit values from parents. Second, frames introduce default reasoning — a filler that holds unless contradicted — which makes the resulting inference non-monotonic: adding new facts can retract earlier conclusions. That behavior is a key contrast with the classical logic you have already seen.

As you watch the videos below, keep a few questions in mind. What exactly sits in a slot, and what distinguishes a slot from a facet? How does inheritance flow down a frame hierarchy, and what happens when a default is overridden? Where do frames give you efficient, well-organized retrieval, and where do they struggle to express things logic handles easily? Try sketching your own frame system for a small domain while you watch — the travel-planning example in particular is easy to imitate.

About this video

The core lecture on frames as slot-and-filler structures. It should cover how a frame names a concept and attaches slots with values, the use of facets and slot metadata, default fillers, and the organization of frames into is-a hierarchies where properties are inherited by more specific frames. Expect discussion of how inference in such a system works by matching, filling, and inheriting rather than by theorem proving.

Why this video

This video maps almost one-to-one onto the central subtopics — slots, facets, defaults, and inheritance — and presents them in the standard AI-textbook terminology you will be expected to use in assessments.

A worked example that builds a frame system for the domain of travel planning. It should show how a domain is decomposed into frames (trips, legs, transport modes, bookings and the like), how slots and defaults are chosen, and how specific instances inherit from more general frames when a plan is instantiated.

Why this video

Seeing a complete small knowledge model built end to end is the fastest way to learn the modelling decisions behind frames, and it gives you a template to imitate when you construct your own domain model.

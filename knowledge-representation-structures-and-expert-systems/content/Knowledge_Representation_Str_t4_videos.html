Introduction

A production rule system represents knowledge as a collection of condition-action rules — statements of the form IF (some pattern holds in working memory) THEN (assert, modify, or retract some fact, or take some action). The rule base holds the long-term knowledge, working memory holds the current facts about the problem at hand, and an inference engine repeatedly runs the recognize-act cycle: match rule conditions against working memory, collect the rules whose conditions are satisfied into a conflict set, select one using a conflict resolution strategy (such as specificity, recency, refractoriness, or explicit priorities), and fire it. This simple loop is the engine behind classic expert systems, and it is the bridge between the declarative structures you studied earlier (semantic networks, frames, ontologies) and actual automated reasoning.

The two directions of control matter most here. Forward chaining is data-driven: you start from known facts and keep firing rules until no new conclusions can be drawn, which suits monitoring, configuration, and diagnosis from streaming observations. Backward chaining is goal-driven: you start from a hypothesis, find rules that conclude it, and recursively try to establish their premises, which suits diagnostic question-and-answer consultations where asking the user is expensive. You should be able to trace both by hand over a small rule base and say which one is cheaper for a given problem shape.

The two videos below are supporting rather than central. Neither one walks through the recognize-act cycle directly; they sit on either side of rule-based reasoning. The fuzzy logic video shows what happens when crisp IF-THEN conditions are replaced by graded membership — a natural extension of rule syntax that reappears in the uncertainty part of this module. The machine learning video contrasts hand-authored rules with rules that are induced from data, which is exactly the trade-off at the heart of the strengths-and-limitations subtopic (the knowledge acquisition bottleneck, brittleness, and the cost of hand-coding expertise). Watch them with the rule-based framework in mind, and keep asking: where is the rule base, what fires, and who wrote the rules?

About this video

From MATLAB's technical education series, this video introduces fuzzy logic and the construction of a fuzzy inference system (FIS). Based on its title and description, it is likely to cover membership functions and degrees of truth, the way fuzzy IF-THEN rules are written over linguistic variables, and how a FIS combines rule outputs to produce a usable result. It positions fuzzy logic as a design tool rather than an abstract theory.

Why this video

It is the closest available treatment of rule syntax and structure: fuzzy inference systems are explicitly rule bases of IF-THEN statements, so the video reinforces how conditions and actions are written while showing what changes when conditions become graded rather than crisp. MATLAB is a reliable technical producer, and the content also previews the fuzzy logic material in the uncertainty portion of this module.

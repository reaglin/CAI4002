Introduction

Having built up the machinery of knowledge representation — semantic networks, frames, production rules, inference engines, and uncertainty handling — we now step back and ask the practical questions. Where were these systems actually deployed, how did anyone know they worked, and why did an industry built on them largely fade away? This topic surveys classic expert systems such as MYCIN, DENDRAL, XCON and PROSPECTOR in domains like medical diagnosis, chemical analysis, and equipment configuration, alongside contemporary descendants in compliance checking, business rules engines, and decision support.

Evaluation is the part students most often skip. A knowledge base can be internally consistent (verification) and still give advice no expert would endorse (validation), so we look at blinded evaluations against human experts, test case libraries, and the ongoing problem of maintaining a rule base as domain knowledge drifts. We also confront the two limitations that shaped the field's history: the knowledge acquisition bottleneck, the slow and expensive business of extracting tacit expertise from busy specialists, and brittleness, the tendency of these systems to fail without warning just outside their narrow competence.

While watching, keep two threads in view. First, notice how evaluation is designed — what counts as evidence that a system is good enough to use on real problems. Second, track where human effort is spent: if most of the cost is in hand-encoding knowledge, you can see why machine-learned and hybrid neuro-symbolic approaches became attractive, and why rule-based reasoning still survives where explanation and auditability matter.

About this video

A short history of MYCIN, the Stanford rule-based system that diagnosed bacterial infections and recommended antibiotic therapy from the early 1970s onward. The video's framing points to the blinded evaluation in which MYCIN's recommendations were compared against those of human physicians, so expect discussion of how such a study was set up and what the results showed. It likely also touches on why, despite favorable results, the system was never deployed in clinical practice.

Why this video

It is the canonical worked example for both Classic Expert Systems and Their Domains and Validation, Verification, and Evaluation, showing what a rigorous blinded evaluation of a knowledge-based system actually looks like.

Video 2: Why the first AI boom collapsed

First Principles · 7:29 · 45 views · 1mo ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

This video traces the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck from its naming in 1977 through to the collapse of the first commercial AI industry in the late 1980s. It should explain why extracting and encoding expert knowledge proved far more expensive and slower than early commercial projections assumed, and how that economic reality undermined companies built around expert system tooling. Expect it to connect the technical limitation to the broader boom-and-bust pattern often called the AI winter.

Why this video

It covers The Knowledge Acquisition Bottleneck and the structural limitations subtopic together, giving students the historical and economic context for why symbolic expert systems gave way to statistical and learned approaches.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos place the module's technical content in a real-world frame. MYCIN's evaluation story ties directly to MO4, since a consultation that produces advice a physician can scrutinize depends on the interplay of knowledge base, inference engine, working memory, and explanation facility, and it also motivates MO5 by showing a system that had to reason under diagnostic uncertainty using certainty factors. The two knowledge acquisition videos illuminate the practical cost side of MO1 and MO2: whatever representation scheme you choose, someone must populate it, and the expressiveness and structure of that scheme determine how hard elicitation and ongoing maintenance will be. Together they help you judge when a rule-based approach of the kind you apply in MO3 is genuinely the right tool and when its brittleness makes another method preferable.

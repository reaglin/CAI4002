Introduction

Real expert systems rarely work with facts that are simply true or false. A physician may be fairly confident but not certain of a diagnosis; a sensor reading may be noisy; a term like "high fever" or "tall" has no sharp boundary. This topic surveys the main formal ways AI systems represent and reason with that messiness: certainty factors (the ad hoc confidence scores used in MYCIN-style rule systems), Bayesian probability and belief networks, Dempster-Shafer theory of evidence, and fuzzy logic for vague or imprecise concepts.

Each approach makes different assumptions about what uncertainty is. Bayesian methods treat it as probability over well-defined events and require priors and conditional distributions. Dempster-Shafer separates "evidence against" from "no evidence either way," which is useful when knowledge is incomplete. Fuzzy logic is not about probability at all — it models degrees of membership in vaguely defined categories. A central practical issue is propagation: when uncertain conclusions feed into further rules, confidence can degrade or combine in ways that must be computed consistently and then explained to the user.

As you watch the videos below, keep two questions in mind. First, what exactly does each number mean in that framework — a probability, a belief mass, a degree of membership, or a heuristic confidence score? Second, what does the method need from you as a knowledge engineer (data, priors, membership functions, rules), and would that be available in a domain you care about? Those two questions are the basis for choosing and justifying an uncertainty approach.

Video 1: What is a Bayesian network?

Norman Fenton · 3:04 · 55,116 views · 3y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

A very short introduction to Bayesian networks, built around the classic "Asia" example that links risk factors, diseases, and symptoms in a small diagnostic network. Expect to see how nodes represent variables, how directed links encode dependence, and how observing evidence updates belief in other nodes. It is an overview rather than a derivation of the underlying math.

Why this video

Norman Fenton is a well-known researcher and author on Bayesian networks in risk and decision analysis, and at roughly three minutes this gives you the core idea of belief networks quickly before you work through propagation in more detail.

About this video

An introduction to fuzzy logic and how it is used to build a fuzzy inference system. The video is likely to cover degrees of membership versus crisp true/false values, membership functions for vague terms, fuzzy if-then rules, and the steps of fuzzification, rule evaluation, and defuzzification back to a usable output.

Why this video

From MATLAB's technical education team, this is an accurate and carefully illustrated treatment that directly addresses the "fuzzy logic and imprecise concepts" subtopic and shows how uncertainty flows through a complete inference chain.

About this video

A short, accessible explainer on Bayesian reasoning featuring statistician Sir David Spiegelhalter. It introduces the idea of starting from a prior belief and revising it as evidence arrives, with everyday illustrations rather than heavy formalism.

Why this video

It gives the conceptual intuition behind probabilistic updating — the reasoning that belief networks automate — from a highly credible statistician and a reputable producer, which helps when you later argue why a Bayesian approach does or does not suit a domain.

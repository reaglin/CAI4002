Semantic Networks and Ontologies

▶

Semantic networks and ontologies form the structural backbone of symbolic knowledge representation. Where production rules capture how to act, graph-based representations capture what exists in a domain and how things relate. The central intuition is simple and very old: meaning can be modelled as a network of concepts connected by named relationships. Quillian's original semantic memory model of the 1960s was built to simulate how humans retrieve word meanings; sixty years later the same intuition drives industrial knowledge graphs with billions of edges. Between those two endpoints lies a long engineering story of making the informal boxes-and-arrows diagram precise enough for a machine to reason over reliably.

This topic traces that story. It begins with the raw graph formalism and its reasoning mechanisms, moves to the inheritance machinery that makes graphs compact (and nonmonotonic), then to formal ontologies that add logical commitment, to the simpler cousins of ontologies (taxonomies, controlled vocabularies, thesauri), to the description logics that give ontologies a decidable semantics, and finally to the Semantic Web stack — RDF, RDFS, OWL, SPARQL — that lets these structures be published, merged, and queried across organisational boundaries.

Graph-Based Knowledge Representation

A semantic network is a labelled, directed graph. Nodes stand for things we wish to talk about; arcs stand for binary relations between them. The vocabulary of node types is usually richer than it first appears, and distinguishing the types carefully is the first discipline a knowledge engineer must learn.

  • Concept or class nodes denote categories: Mammal, Invoice, FebrileIllness. They denote sets of possible individuals, not any particular individual.
  • Individual nodes denote particular entities: Clyde, Invoice-20394, PatientSmith.
  • Attribute-value nodes denote literals or fillers: Grey, 38.7C, 1200kg. In many systems these are terminal nodes with no outgoing arcs.
  • Event or situation nodes are used to reify n-ary relations. Because arcs are binary, a three-place fact such as "Dr. Lee prescribed amoxicillin to Smith on 3 May" cannot be a single arc. It is modelled as an event node Prescription-17 with arcs agent → DrLee, drug → Amoxicillin, patient → Smith, date → 2024-05-03. Reification is the standard workaround for the binary-arc limitation and it recurs in RDF as "blank nodes" or intermediate resources.

Arcs carry labels that name the relation. A small set of relations recurs across almost every domain:

A worked fragment makes the structure concrete. Consider a small zoological network written in an indented textual form:

Animal        has-property: alive
   ^ is-a
Bird          has-property: can-fly = true
              has-part:     feathers
   ^ is-a            ^ is-a
Penguin                     Eagle
  can-fly = false           habitat = mountains
   ^ instance-of
Opus (an individual)
  colour = black-and-white
  located-in = Antarctica

Reasoning by traversal. Inference in a semantic network is graph search. Three mechanisms dominate:

  • Link following / inheritance retrieval. To answer "Is Opus alive?", follow instance-of from Opus to Penguin, then climb is-a arcs to Bird and then Animal, where alive is stored. The answer is yes, obtained without ever storing "Opus is alive" explicitly.
  • Path-based inference. Transitive relations compose along paths. From Trunk part-of Elephant and Elephant located-in Africa a system may (with care) derive facts about the location of trunks. Path semantics must be declared: not all relation compositions are valid, and naively chaining part-of with has-property produces nonsense ("a human's hand is mortal" is arguable; "a human's hand is a citizen" is not).
  • Spreading activation and intersection search. Quillian's original retrieval method activates two or more nodes simultaneously, propagates activation outward along arcs with decreasing strength, and reports the nodes where the activation fronts meet. Asking for the connection between Fire and Smoke might intersect at a Combustion node; asking the relation between Nurse and Hospital might intersect at Employment. This is an associative, similarity-flavoured inference rather than a logically sound one, and it is the ancestor of modern graph-embedding similarity measures and of "related searches" features.

Strengths. The graph form is extraordinarily readable. A domain expert with no logic training can usually validate a drawn network, which shortens the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck. Retrieval of "everything related to X" is a cheap local operation — all the arcs incident on a node — whereas in a flat logical database it requires scanning formulas. The representation is also economical: a property asserted once at a high node serves thousands of descendants.

The critical weakness: unstandardised link semantics. Woods' famous 1975 critique, "What's in a Link?", observed that the same diagram can mean different things in different systems. Consider the arc Elephant → Grey labelled colour. Does it mean:

  • all elephants are grey (universal quantification),
  • typical elephants are grey (a default, defeasible),
  • some elephant is grey (existential),
  • the concept Elephant has the attribute "grey" as part of its definition (definitional/necessary-and-sufficient), or
  • grey is a permitted value in the colour slot (a type restriction)?

The picture cannot tell you. Similarly, an is-a arc has historically been overloaded to mean subclass, instance-of, part-of, role-playing ("Clyde is-a circus performer"), and even similarity. Without a declared semantics, two systems exchanging a network will silently draw different conclusions, and automated reasoning becomes unreliable. The entire subsequent history of the field — structured inheritance networks, KL-ONE, description logics, OWL — can be read as the project of fixing exactly this ambiguity by assigning each link a model-theoretic meaning.

Is-A Hierarchies and Property Inheritance

The is-a backbone is the most important structure in any semantic network, because it is the channel through which properties flow. Two distinct link types must be kept apart, a distinction novices frequently collapse:

  • Subclass (is-a / subsumption) relates two classes and is transitive: if Penguin is-a Bird and Bird is-a Animal then Penguin is-a Animal.
  • Instance-of (membership) relates an individual to a class and is not transitive in the same way. Opus instance-of Penguin plus Penguin is-a Bird gives Opus instance-of Bird — but you may never conclude Opus is-a Bird in the subclass sense, because Opus is not a category.

A classic error illustrates why this matters. "Clyde is-a Elephant" and "Elephant is-a EndangeredSpecies" must not combine to yield "Clyde is-a EndangeredSpecies" — Clyde is an animal, not a species. The mistake arises from using one arc label for two relations. Formal systems force the distinction (OWL uses rdfs:subClassOf versus rdf:type).

Inheritance and compactness. Inheritance means a node acquires, by default, the attribute–value pairs stored on its ancestors. If Animal carries breathes = true, mortal = true, needs-food = true, then every one of ten thousand descendant species inherits them from a single storage site. The saving is not merely in memory; it is in maintenance. Correcting an error at the ancestor corrects it everywhere, which is the knowledge-base analogue of avoiding duplicated code.

The retrieval algorithm is a simple upward search:

function GET-VALUE(node, attribute):
    if attribute is stored locally on node:
        return local value
    for each parent P of node, in precedence order:
        v = GET-VALUE(P, attribute)
        if v is not null: return v
    return null        # attribute unknown

Default inheritance and overriding. Rigid inheritance — "every property of an ancestor holds absolutely of every descendant" — is too brittle for real domains, which are full of typical cases and exceptions. Default inheritance says an inherited value holds unless the descendant states otherwise. The textbook case:

NodeLocally storedEffective value of can-flyWhy
Animal—unknownNothing stored or inherited
Birdcan-fly = truetrueLocal default
Eagle—trueInherited from Bird
Penguincan-fly = falsefalseLocal value overrides ancestor
Opus (instance of Penguin)—falseNearest ancestor with a value is Penguin
InjuredEaglecan-fly = falsefalseOverride at a lower, more specific class

The governing principle is specificity: the value from the most specific class that supplies one wins. This is sometimes called the "shortest path" or "inferential distance" criterion, though path length is a poor proxy and the proper criterion is subsumption-based specificity.

Nonmonotonicity. Classical logic is monotonic: adding premises never removes conclusions. Default inheritance breaks this. Suppose the knowledge base contains only "Tweety is a bird". The system concludes can-fly(Tweety) = true. Now add one fact — "Tweety is a penguin" — and the previous conclusion must be retracted. Knowledge grew, conclusions shrank. This is nonmonotonic reasoning, and it carries concrete engineering consequences:

  • Conclusions must be tagged with their justifications so they can be withdrawn; this is the role of a truth-maintenance system (TMS) or justification-based dependency network.
  • Anything derived from a defeated default must also be re-examined. If the system booked a flight cage for Tweety on the basis of flight capability, that downstream decision is now suspect.
  • Order of assertion can affect intermediate states, so systems should recompute rather than cache defaults carelessly.
  • Formal accounts of this behaviour include Reiter's default logic, circumscription, and the closed-world assumption; inheritance networks are a restricted, computationally cheap fragment of these.

Multiple inheritance and conflict. When a node has more than one parent, it may inherit contradictory values. The canonical "Nixon diamond" illustrates it:

Quaker  -- pacifist = true          Republican -- pacifist = false
     ^ is-a                               ^ is-a
            \                           /
                     Nixon (individual)

Neither parent is more specific than the other, so specificity gives no answer and the network is ambiguous: two equally defensible extensions exist. Systems resolve such conflicts by policy, and the policy must be declared:

  • Explicit precedence ordering on parents — e.g. in a frame system, the first parent in the a-kind-of list wins. Simple, deterministic, but arbitrary.
  • Linearisation algorithms such as the C3 method used in Python's method-resolution order, which compute a single consistent total order over all ancestors and preserve local precedence.
  • Sceptical reasoning — conclude nothing when parents conflict, and flag the clash for a human or for a more specific rule. Safer in medical or legal applications.
  • Credulous reasoning — accept any one consistent extension, possibly carrying all alternatives forward as hypotheses.
  • Explicit override at the child — the knowledge engineer asserts Nixon.pacifist = false directly, removing the ambiguity by fiat. This is usually the most honest fix.
  • Prohibit the structure — many ontologies restrict themselves to single inheritance for the primitive taxonomy and express cross-cutting characteristics as separate properties or as defined classes.

A practical design heuristic follows: cross-cutting dimensions (life stage, legal status, role, condition) usually should not be is-a parents at all. Instead of making Employee and Student both superclasses of WorkingStudent, model Person with hasRole arcs to role instances. This prevents combinatorial explosion of the hierarchy and sidesteps most inheritance conflicts.

Formal Ontologies

An ontology, in the computing sense, is a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualisation (Gruber's definition, and still the standard one). Each word earns its place: formal means machine-interpretable with a defined semantics; explicit means the constraints are stated rather than assumed; shared means it represents consensus among a community, not a single modeller's private view; conceptualisation means it captures the abstract model behind a domain, not one database schema.

Levels of ontology. Ontologies are layered by generality, and reuse flows downward:

  • Upper (foundational, top-level) ontologies define domain-neutral categories: Object versus Process, Continuant versus Occurrent, Quality, Role, Function, SpatialRegion, TemporalRegion. Established examples include BFO (Basic Formal Ontology, heavily used in biomedicine), DOLCE (grounded in cognitive and linguistic analysis), SUMO, and UFO. Their value is that they settle hard modelling questions once — is a disease a process or a disposition? is an organisation an object or a collective? — so that independently built domain ontologies make compatible choices.
  • Mid-level / core ontologies cover concepts common to many domains: units of measure, time intervals, geospatial features, organisations, provenance. Examples include QUDT, OWL-Time, GeoSPARQL and PROV-O.
  • Domain ontologies specialise the above for a field: SNOMED CT and the Gene Ontology in life sciences, FIBO in finance, schema.org for web content, ISO 15926 for process plants.
  • Application ontologies are narrow, task-specific, often short-lived, and tuned to the needs of a single system.

A specialisation chain might run: Continuant → MaterialEntity → Artifact → Equipment → RotatingEquipment → Pump → CentrifugalPump, with the upper three levels taken from a foundational ontology and the lower levels contributed by the engineering domain.

Interoperability. The strongest practical argument for ontologies is semantic integration. Two hospitals record a lab result; one calls the field HGB, the other Haemoglobin_g_dL. A shared ontology maps both to the same concept with an explicit unit and an explicit specimen type, so a research query returns correct results across both sites. Without such agreement, integration is done by hand-written, brittle, pairwise mappings — n(n−1)/2 of them for n systems, versus n mappings to a common ontology. Ontologies also make the meaning of an integration explicit and auditable, rather than buried in ETL code.

Services ontologies provide.

  • Consistency checking — detecting that the knowledge base entails a contradiction, e.g. an individual asserted to be both a Pump and a Valve when those are declared disjoint, or an individual with three values for a functional property.
  • Automated classification — the reasoner computes the subsumption hierarchy rather than requiring it to be drawn by hand. If HighRiskPatient ≡ Patient ⊓ ∃hasDiagnosis.CardiacCondition and MyocardialInfarction ⊑ CardiacCondition, then any patient with an MI is automatically classified as high-risk. SNOMED CT relies on this: its hundreds of thousands of concepts are organised by a reasoner, not by editors.
  • Inference of implicit facts — transitive property closure, inverse properties, symmetric relations, inherited restrictions, and type inference all add facts that were never asserted. Asserting P101 installedIn UnitA and declaring installedIn to have inverse contains yields UnitA contains P101 for free.
  • Query answering over inferred knowledge — queries are answered against the deductive closure, so a request for "all rotating equipment in Unit A" retrieves pumps, turbines and compressors without the query author enumerating subclasses.

Ontology engineering as a process. Building an ontology is a software-engineering activity with its own lifecycle. Methodologies such as METHONTOLOGY, On-To-Knowledge, NeOn and the widely taught Ontology Development 101 share a common skeleton:

  • Scoping and requirements. Decide the purpose, the intended users, and the boundaries. The standard technique is to write competency questions — concrete queries the finished ontology must be able to answer, such as "Which pumps installed before 2015 have had more than two seal failures?" Competency questions double as acceptance tests: if the model cannot answer one, something is missing; if a class answers no question, it may be out of scope.
  • Reuse survey. Before modelling, look for existing ontologies to import or align with. Reinventing a units-of-measure or time ontology is wasted effort and destroys interoperability.
  • Term elicitation from experts. Interviews, document and form analysis, card sorting, laddering ("what is this a kind of?", "what kinds of this are there?"), and protocol analysis of experts solving real cases. The output is a glossary of candidate terms with informal definitions, separated into things that will become classes, things that will become properties, and things that are really values.
  • Conceptualisation and formalisation. Organise terms into an is-a hierarchy, decide class-versus-individual for each term, define properties with domains and ranges, add restrictions and disjointness axioms, then encode in a formal language such as OWL.
  • Evaluation and iteration. Run a reasoner to check consistency and to inspect the inferred hierarchy for surprises (an unexpected subsumption is usually a modelling bug). Check against the competency questions with real SPARQL queries. Apply pitfall catalogues — OOPS! lists common errors such as creating synonymous classes, missing disjointness, confusing a class with its name, or cycles in the hierarchy. Have domain experts review natural-language renderings of the axioms.
  • Maintenance and versioning. Publish under stable URIs, deprecate rather than delete terms, record change rationale, and manage releases, because downstream consumers depend on the vocabulary remaining stable.

Two recurring modelling decisions deserve emphasis. First, class or individual? Treat something as a class if you need to talk about its members; as an individual if it is the finest grain you will ever reason about. "Boeing 747" is a class if you track specific airframes, an individual if you only catalogue aircraft models. Second, what is a subclass? The test is strict set inclusion: every instance of the subclass must necessarily be an instance of the superclass. "Pump is-a Equipment" passes. "Pump is-a Maintenance" fails — the relation is subjectOf, not subsumption. Most beginner ontologies are full of is-a links that are really part-of, instance-of, or topical association.

Taxonomies and Controlled Vocabularies

Ontologies sit at the expressive end of a spectrum of knowledge organisation systems. The weaker members of the family are enormously useful in their own right, and often cheaper to build and maintain.

Taxonomy versus ontology. A taxonomy captures only subsumption: it tells you that CentrifugalPump is narrower than Pump, and nothing else. An ontology adds arbitrary relations (Pump hasComponent Impeller, Pump installedIn ProcessUnit) and logical constraints (Pump and Valve are disjoint; every Pump has exactly one ratedFlow). The practical consequence is that a taxonomy supports navigation and roll-up reporting, while an ontology supports deduction and consistency checking. Many successful projects deliberately stop at a taxonomy: if the only requirement is "let users browse and filter documents", the extra cost of axiomatisation buys nothing.

Controlled vocabularies. The point of restricting terminology is to collapse the many ways humans name the same thing. A fault-reporting system that allows free text will accumulate seal leak, leaking seal, mech seal leaking, MS leak and seal failure (leak) as five unrelated strings; no reliability analysis can count them. A controlled vocabulary designates one preferred term, lists the rest as non-preferred entry terms pointing to it (USE / UF relations), and forbids anything else. Benefits: consistent indexing, reliable retrieval, comparable statistics, and simpler downstream integration. Costs: governance effort, a change-request process, and the risk of a vocabulary that lags the language of its users.

Thesauri. A thesaurus (standardised by ISO 25964 and expressible in SKOS) extends a controlled vocabulary with inter-concept links:

  • BT / NT (broader term / narrower term) — the hierarchical backbone, e.g. Analgesics NT Opioid analgesics.
  • RT (related term) — associative, non-hierarchical links, e.g. Anaesthesia RT Operating theatres. These support "see also" navigation and recall-oriented query expansion.
  • USE / UF (used for) — synonym control, e.g. Myocardial infarction UF Heart attack.
  • Scope notes — human-readable guidance on exactly when to apply a term, which is the main defence against inconsistent indexing.

Note the important semantic difference from an ontology: thesaurus BT/NT is a documentation relation meaning "is indexed under", not strict set inclusion. Hospitals may be NT of Health facilities, but Surgery might also be NT of Medicine in a sense that is topical, not subsumptive. Treating SKOS broader as if it were OWL subClassOf is a classic source of bad inference when thesauri are migrated to the Semantic Web.

Consistent classification criteria. A well-formed taxonomy divides each level by a single criterion — what ontologists call maintaining one facet per level of division. A fault taxonomy that lists, as siblings, MechanicalFault, ElectricalFault and FaultDuringStartup mixes a system criterion with a timing criterion; an electrical fault occurring at startup belongs in two places at once and reporting becomes incoherent. The repair is faceted classification: keep separate dimensions separate.

FacetPermitted values
Affected subsystemmechanical, electrical, instrumentation, software
Operating phasestartup, steady-state, shutdown, standby
Severityminor, major, critical
Detection methodoperator observation, alarm, scheduled inspection, condition monitoring

Each incident then receives one value per facet, giving unambiguous, mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive categories at every level and enabling cross-tabulated analysis that a single tangled tree cannot support. Other quality rules follow from the same discipline: sibling categories should be mutually exclusive, the set of children should exhaust the parent (add an explicit "other" only when genuinely necessary), hierarchies should be no deeper than users will tolerate browsing, and a node should normally have between two and roughly a dozen children — a single child indicates a pointless level, fifty children indicate a missing level of division.

Entry point for indexing, search and tagging. In knowledge-management practice these artefacts earn their keep at the point where content meets users. A taxonomy drives faceted navigation in a document repository or e-commerce catalogue; a controlled vocabulary populates the tagging interface so that contributors cannot invent new labels; a thesaurus expands a user's query from heart attack to the indexed term myocardial infarction and then down the NT branches, raising recall without flooding results. Auto-classification tools match text against vocabulary terms to suggest tags, and the same structures provide the labels for supervised text classifiers. When an organisation later builds an ontology or knowledge graph, the existing taxonomy is usually the seed for its class hierarchy — which is why investing in consistent classification criteria early pays off twice.

Description Logics

Description logics (DLs) are the family of formal languages created to give semantic networks and frames a precise, decidable logical foundation. They are, in essence, carefully chosen fragments of first-order logic: expressive enough to describe structured concepts, restricted enough that key inference problems are guaranteed to terminate. They are the direct formal ancestor of OWL.

TBox and ABox. A DL knowledge base is split into two parts:

  • The TBox (terminological box) holds general knowledge about concepts: definitions and subsumption axioms. It is the schema level — the vocabulary and its logical structure. Example axioms: Mother ≡ Woman ⊓ ∃hasChild.Person, Penguin ⊑ Bird, Man ⊑ ¬Woman.
  • The ABox (assertional box) holds facts about named individuals: concept memberships and role fillers. Example assertions: Woman(mary), hasChild(mary, john), Person(john).

Given that TBox and ABox, a reasoner infers Mother(mary) — never asserted, but entailed. Some systems add an RBox for axioms about roles themselves (transitivity, inverses, role hierarchies such as hasMother ⊑ hasParent).

Concept constructors. Complex concepts are built from atomic concept names (Person, Document) and role names (hasChild, authorOf) using a fixed set of operators:

Two traps are worth highlighting. First, ∀R.C is trivially satisfied by anything with no R-fillers at all: a childless person vacuously satisfies ∀hasChild.Female. Modellers who want "has children and all of them are female" must write ∃hasChild.⊤ ⊓ ∀hasChild.Female. Second, DLs make the open-world assumption: what is not stated is unknown, not false. If the ABox does not say Mary has a child, the reasoner does not conclude she is childless. This is the right default for web-scale and integrated data, but it surprises people used to databases, where the closed-world assumption applies. It also explains why DL reasoners cannot find "missing data" errors — that job needs closed-world validation tools such as SHACL.

Standard reasoning tasks.

  • Subsumption — does C ⊑ D follow from the TBox? Is every MarriedPerson necessarily a Person? This is the fundamental service.
  • Satisfiability / consistency — can a concept have any instances at all without contradiction, and is the whole knowledge base contradiction-free? An unsatisfiable class (equivalent to ⊥) is almost always a modelling bug, typically caused by a disjointness axiom colliding with a restriction.
  • Classification — compute the complete subsumption lattice of all named concepts, producing the inferred hierarchy. This is how a reasoner discovers that CheeseAndTomatoPizza belongs under VegetarianPizza without anyone drawing that link.
  • Instance checking and instance retrieval — is individual a a member of concept C, and which individuals are members of C? This answers queries over the deductive closure, not merely the asserted data.
  • Equivalence and disjointness checking — are two concepts necessarily co-extensive, or necessarily non-overlapping?

All of these reduce to satisfiability testing in logics with full negation: C ⊑ D holds exactly when C ⊓ ¬D is unsatisfiable. Implementations therefore concentrate on one highly optimised procedure, usually a tableau algorithm that attempts to build a model and reports subsumption when every attempt yields a clash.

The expressiveness/complexity trade-off. DL research is organised around naming languages by the constructors they include, because each addition costs computation. The base language ALC provides intersection, union, negation, and both restrictions; letters are then appended — S for ALC with transitive roles, H role hierarchies, O nominals, I inverse roles, N number restrictions, Q qualified number restrictions, (D) datatypes. OWL 2 DL corresponds to SROIQ(D).

The crucial design philosophy is that expressiveness is deliberately sacrificed to preserve decidability and tractability. Restrictions to binary roles, to tree-shaped models, and on how role chains may interact are not oversights; they are what keeps reasoning guaranteed to terminate. Worst-case complexities also look worse than practice: real ontologies are far from worst case, and heavily optimised reasoners classify large ontologies in seconds. Nevertheless, a project that only needs subsumption over conjunctive definitions should choose the EL profile and enjoy polynomial performance rather than pay for expressiveness it will never use.

Reasoners. Several mature implementations automate these tasks and plug into editors such as Protégé through the OWL API:

  • HermiT — uses a hypertableau calculus that reduces the nondeterminism of classic tableaux; strong general-purpose OWL 2 DL classification.
  • Pellet (and its successor Openllet) — tableau-based, with good ABox reasoning, SPARQL-DL query support, incremental reasoning and explanation of entailments.
  • FaCT++ — a C++ tableau reasoner noted for fast TBox classification.
  • ELK — a specialised, highly parallel EL reasoner that classifies SNOMED CT in seconds.
  • Konclude, RacerPro, and rule-based materialisers such as RDFox and GraphDB's inference engine cover other points in the performance/expressiveness space.

A typical development loop: edit the ontology in Protégé, run HermiT, inspect the inferred hierarchy, notice that a class has turned red (unsatisfiable) or has moved under an unexpected parent, ask the tool to explain the entailment, and correct the offending axiom. The explanation facility — computing the minimal set of axioms responsible for an entailment, known as justifications — is what makes debugging large ontologies feasible at all.

Semantic Web Standards and Sharing

The Semantic Web stack, standardised by the W3C, turns the ideas above into an interoperable publishing infrastructure. Its premise is that data, not just documents, should be linkable across the web, with meaning attached in a machine-readable way.

RDF: triples and URIs. The Resource Description Framework represents every fact as a three-part statement — subject, predicate, object — and a set of such triples forms a directed labelled graph. Subjects and predicates are identified by URIs (more precisely IRIs), so names are globally unique and dereferenceable; objects may be URIs or literal values with datatypes and language tags. A blank node stands for an unnamed resource, typically used for reification of n-ary relations exactly as "event nodes" were used in classical semantic networks.

@prefix ex:   <http://example.org/plant#> .
@prefix rdf:  <http://www.w3.org/1999/02/22-rdf-syntax-ns#> .
@prefix xsd:  <http://www.w3.org/2001/XMLSchema#> .

ex:P101  rdf:type        ex:CentrifugalPump .
ex:P101  ex:installedIn  ex:UnitA .
ex:P101  ex:serialNumber "SN-44821" .
ex:P101  ex:ratedFlow    "350"^^xsd:decimal .

# reified n-ary fact: a maintenance event
ex:WO8842 rdf:type        ex:MaintenanceEvent ;
          ex:performedOn  ex:P101 ;
          ex:performedBy  ex:TechLee ;
          ex:startDate    "2024-05-03"^^xsd:date .

Three properties of this model matter in practice. First, it is schema-last: new predicates can be added to any resource at any time without altering a table definition, which suits heterogeneous and evolving data. Second, merging two datasets is simply taking the union of their triple sets; identifiers that coincide automatically join the graphs, which is why stable URIs are the foundation of linked data. Third, RDF by itself carries almost no semantics — it says a relation holds, not what the relation means. That is the job of the layers above it.

RDF Schema. RDFS adds a light modelling vocabulary: rdfs:Class, rdfs:subClassOf, rdf:Property, rdfs:subPropertyOf, rdfs:domain, rdfs:range, plus annotation terms rdfs:label and rdfs:comment. It supplies precisely the is-a hierarchy and property typing discussed earlier, with simple entailment rules:

OWL. The Web Ontology Language supplies description-logic expressiveness on top of RDF, giving exactly the constructors described earlier in a web-publishable syntax. Its main additions over RDFS:

  • Class constructors and axioms — owl:intersectionOf, owl:unionOf, owl:complementOf, owl:equivalentClass, owl:disjointWith, owl:oneOf (nominals).
  • Property restrictions — owl:someValuesFrom (∃), owl:allValuesFrom (∀), owl:hasValue, and cardinality limits owl:minQualifiedCardinality, owl:maxCardinality, owl:cardinality.
  • Property characteristics — owl:TransitiveProperty, owl:SymmetricProperty, owl:AsymmetricProperty, owl:FunctionalProperty, owl:InverseFunctionalProperty, owl:ReflexiveProperty, owl:inverseOf, and OWL 2 property chains.
  • Individual identity — owl:sameAs and owl:differentFrom, needed because RDF makes no unique-name assumption: two URIs may denote the same thing unless stated otherwise.
  • Profiles — OWL 2 EL, QL and RL, each a syntactic subset with better computational guarantees, plus OWL 2 DL (decidable, SROIQ(D)) and OWL 2 Full (RDF-compatible but undecidable).
ex:Pump  rdf:type  owl:Class ;
         rdfs:subClassOf ex:RotatingEquipment ;
         owl:disjointWith ex:Valve ;
         rdfs:subClassOf [ rdf:type owl:Restriction ;
                           owl:onProperty ex:hasComponent ;
                           owl:someValuesFrom ex:Impeller ] .

ex:CriticalAsset  owl:equivalentClass
     [ rdf:type owl:Class ;
       owl:intersectionOf ( ex:Equipment
                            [ rdf:type owl:Restriction ;
                              owl:onProperty ex:hasFailureConsequence ;
                              owl:someValuesFrom ex:SafetyConsequence ] ) ] .

ex:hasComponent  rdf:type  owl:TransitiveProperty ;
                 owl:inverseOf ex:componentOf .

With these axioms a reasoner will classify any piece of equipment whose failure has a safety consequence as a CriticalAsset without that label ever being typed by a human, will infer ex:Imp-7 ex:componentOf ex:P101 from the inverse, will propagate component membership transitively from sub-assemblies, and will flag an inconsistency if someone asserts that ex:P101 is also a Valve.

SPARQL. SPARQL is the standard query language and protocol for RDF. Queries are graph patterns: sets of triple patterns with variables, matched against the dataset, optionally against its inferred closure. The language supports SELECT, ASK, CONSTRUCT (which returns a new graph and is the standard way to write mapping and transformation rules), and DESCRIBE; modifiers include FILTER, OPTIONAL, UNION, GROUP BY, aggregation, property paths, sub-queries and federation across remote endpoints via SERVICE. SPARQL 1.1 Update adds INSERT/DELETE for manipulating graph data.

PREFIX ex:   <http://example.org/plant#>
PREFIX rdfs: <http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#>

SELECT ?pump ?unit (COUNT(?event) AS ?failures)
WHERE {
  ?pump  a            ex:Pump ;              # inferred types included
         ex:installedIn ?unit .
  ?pump  ex:hasComponent+ ?part .            # property path: any depth
  OPTIONAL {
    ?event a              ex:SealFailure ;
           ex:performedOn ?pump ;
           ex:startDate   ?d .
    FILTER (?d >= "2015-01-01"^^xsd:date)
  }
}
GROUP BY ?pump ?unit
HAVING (COUNT(?event) > 2)
ORDER BY DESC(?failures)

This single query answers the competency question posed earlier. Note that ?pump a ex:Pump retrieves centrifugal, positive-displacement and every other subclass automatically when run over an entailed graph — the payoff of putting the hierarchy in the data rather than in the application code. The + property path walks assemblies to arbitrary depth, something awkward in SQL without recursive CTEs.

Standards at industrial scale. These technologies are not laboratory curiosities:

  • Search engine knowledge graphs — the entity panels shown beside search results are driven by graphs of billions of triples about people, places, organisations, films and products, assembled from structured sources and from schema.org markup that site owners embed in pages as JSON-LD, RDFa or Microdata. The same markup powers rich results for recipes, events, job postings and product offers.
  • Product and parts catalogues — manufacturers and retailers publish product taxonomies and specifications so that attributes, compatibilities and substitutions can be matched across suppliers; GS1, eCl@ss and ISO 15926 serve this role in retail and process industries respectively.
  • Life sciences — SNOMED CT, the Gene Ontology, ChEBI, Uniprot and the Bio2RDF/EBI endpoints publish hundreds of millions of triples; the Gene Ontology's automated classification underpins daily genomics analysis.
  • Public and cultural data — Wikidata, DBpedia, EuroVoc, Europeana, national statistics and geospatial agencies expose SPARQL endpoints that anyone may query or federate against.
  • Enterprise integration — financial institutions use FIBO to reconcile instrument and counterparty definitions across systems; pharmaceutical firms use ontology-based data access to query dozens of legacy databases through one vocabulary.

Scale also forces pragmatic compromises. Very large graphs typically rely on cheap materialised inference (RDFS or OWL 2 RL rules applied forward over the store) rather than full DL tableau reasoning; identity management across sources (owl:sameAs chains) becomes a major engineering problem; provenance and versioning must be tracked with named graphs or PROV-O; and validation shifts to SHACL because open-world DL semantics cannot express "this record must have a date". Property graph databases offer an alternative with easier per-edge attributes but weaker standardised semantics. The enduring lesson is the one Woods identified fifty years ago: a graph is only as useful as the agreed meaning of its links, and RDF, RDFS, OWL and SPARQL are the current best answer to supplying that meaning in a form that machines — and organisations — can share.

Semantic networks represent knowledge as a graph: concepts become nodes, and labeled links encode the relationships between them. The most important link type is is-a, which builds a hierarchy of classes and instances and allows properties stated once at a general level to be inherited by everything below it. This makes graph-based representation compact and intuitive, but it also raises questions the course cares about — what exactly does a link mean, when may an inherited default be overridden, and how much reasoning can a system actually perform.

Formal ontologies answer those questions by giving the graph a precise semantics. Taxonomies and controlled vocabularies standardize the terms a community uses; description logics supply a family of decidable logics with concepts, roles, subsumption and classification; and Semantic Web standards such as RDF, RDFS and OWL package all of this so that vocabularies can be published, merged, and reasoned over by machines. Knowledge graphs are the large-scale industrial form of these ideas.

As you watch, keep three threads in mind. First, notice the trade-off between expressiveness and tractability — the more you can say, the harder inference becomes. Second, track how is-a and property inheritance appear in each presentation, whether it is called subsumption, subclass, or a typed edge. Third, think about how these symbolic structures relate to the statistical methods covered elsewhere in the course, since modern systems increasingly combine the two.

Probability Foundations for Reasoning Under Uncertainty

▶

Almost every interesting agent — a robot navigating a corridor, a medical diagnosis system, a speech recogniser, a self-driving car — must act long before it knows everything relevant to its decision. Classical logic gives an agent a crisp way to say what follows from what it knows, but it offers no way to say that one unproven conclusion is far more plausible than another. Probability theory fills exactly that gap: it provides a calculus of degrees of belief that is as rigorous as logic, that degrades gracefully when information is missing, and that tells an agent precisely how to revise its beliefs when new evidence arrives. This topic lays down the vocabulary, notation and arithmetic that the rest of the module depends on — random variables, atomic events, the axioms, joint distributions, marginalization, conditioning, and the prior/posterior distinction.

Why agents must reason under uncertainty

Uncertainty is not a sign of a badly built agent; it is an unavoidable consequence of embedding an agent in a real environment. Four distinct sources are usually identified, and they compound one another:

  • Partial observability. The agent's sensors see only a fragment of the world state. A taxi-driving agent cannot see whether the car two vehicles ahead is about to brake; a diagnostic agent cannot see the patient's arteries. The hidden part of the state is not unknowable in principle, merely unobserved right now.
  • Noisy or unreliable sensors. Even what the agent does observe is corrupted. A sonar returns 2.7 m when the wall is at 2.9 m; a lab test for a disease has a 5% false-positive rate. The mapping from world state to percept is many-to-many, so a percept never pins down the state exactly.
  • Nondeterministic action outcomes. Executing Grasp(cup) usually succeeds, but sometimes the cup slips. Executing Drive(90 minutes to airport) usually gets you there, but a burst water main may not care about your plan.
  • Sheer complexity. Sometimes the world is effectively deterministic and observable, but modelling it exactly is hopeless. Whether a particular patient's toothache is caused by a cavity depends on bacterial colonies, enamel microstructure and immune response; no agent will ever write down that model. Probability acts as a summary of the detail we deliberately refuse to represent.

Consider a logical agent trying to encode dental knowledge. The natural first rule is:

∀p  Toothache(p) ⇒ Cavity(p)

This is simply false: toothaches are also caused by gum disease, abscesses, impacted wisdom teeth, cracked fillings, or sinus infections. The logician's two repair strategies both fail:

  • Commit anyway. Keep the rule and accept that the agent will sometimes assert a cavity that is not there. The knowledge base becomes unsound — it licenses conclusions that are not entailed by reality.
  • Enumerate every cause. Write ∀p Toothache(p) ⇒ Cavity(p) ∨ GumDisease(p) ∨ Abscess(p) ∨ Sinusitis(p) ∨ …. This is true but useless: the disjunction is unbounded (a qualification problem), it gives the agent no guidance about which disjunct to act on, and building the reverse causal rule Cavity(p) ⇒ Toothache(p) fails too because many cavities are painless.

Probability lets the agent say something both true and useful: P(Cavity | Toothache) = 0.6. The number 0.6 is not a claim that the universe flips a biased coin when a tooth hurts. It is a claim about the agent's epistemic state: given everything this agent currently knows, in 60% of the worlds consistent with its evidence there is a cavity. A different agent, holding an X-ray, would legitimately assign a different number to the same patient. This is the Bayesian or subjectivist reading of probability, and it is the one used throughout AI. (The alternative frequentist reading, which grounds probability in long-run relative frequencies, gives the same mathematics; the axioms and all derived rules are identical.)

Crucially, degrees of belief are dynamic. Before any examination, the agent may hold P(Cavity) = 0.2. On observing a toothache it revises to 0.6; on additionally observing that the dental probe catches in the tooth it may revise to 0.87; on seeing a clean X-ray it may drop back to 0.05. Because belief revision is just arithmetic on distributions, the same machinery that supports perception also supports learning: the agent's model parameters can themselves be treated as random variables and updated from data. This is why probability, rather than ad-hoc certainty factors or fuzzy degrees, became the standard foundation for modern AI, and why decision theory (probability + utility) gives the normative definition of rational action: choose the action with maximum expected utility.

Random variables and domains

The basic element of a probabilistic model is the random variable: a named feature of the world that can take one of a set of values. By convention, variable names are capitalised (Cavity, Weather, Temperature) and values are lower case (true, rain, 21.5). The set of values a variable may take is its domain, and the domain must be exhaustive (some value always holds) and mutually exclusive (no two values hold at once).

Boolean variables. These are so common that they get shorthand: for a Boolean variable Cavity, write cavity for the proposition Cavity = true and ¬cavity for Cavity = false. Because the two values exhaust the domain, P(cavity) + P(¬cavity) = 1, so one number suffices.

Discrete variables. A discrete variable with k values needs k numbers, of which only k − 1 are free. We use bold P for the whole vector: P(Weather) = ⟨0.6, 0.1, 0.29, 0.01⟩ for the ordering ⟨sunny, rain, cloud, snow⟩, while plain P(Weather = rain) = 0.1 denotes a single number. This bold-vs-plain distinction is used constantly once we reach Bayesian networks, where a single equation such as P(X | Y) stands for an entire conditional probability table.

Continuous variables. If X can take any real value in an interval, then any individual value has probability zero — there are uncountably many of them, and they must share a total mass of 1. Instead we write a density p(x) with the property that probabilities come from integration:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫[a,b] p(x) dx      and      ∫[−∞,∞] p(x) dx = 1

For example, if the temperature at noon is uniformly distributed on [18, 26] °C, then p(x) = 1/8 per degree for x in that range and 0 elsewhere, so P(20 ≤ X ≤ 22) = 2 × 1/8 = 0.25. Note that a density may exceed 1 (here it would if the interval were narrower than one degree) — only the integral is constrained. The Gaussian density p(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) e^{−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)} is the workhorse of continuous modelling because sums of many small independent effects tend toward it and because it is closed under the operations of linear-Gaussian inference. Everything else in this module — marginalization, conditioning, Bayes' rule — carries over to the continuous case with sums replaced by integrals.

Propositions. A proposition is an assertion that can be true or false in a given world. The simplest kind is an atomic assertion that a variable takes a value: Cavity = true, Weather = rain, Temperature ≥ 22. Propositions combine with the usual logical connectives, and probability is defined over the resulting sentences:

  • cavity ∧ ¬toothache — a cavity with no pain
  • Weather = sunny ∨ Weather = cloud — dry weather
  • ¬(Alarm ∧ Burglary)

Atomic events. An atomic event (also called a possible world or sample point) is a complete specification of the state of the world: an assignment of a value to every variable in the model. If the model has variables Cavity, Toothache and Catch (the dentist's probe catches in the tooth), then there are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 atomic events, one of which is cavity ∧ ¬toothache ∧ catch. Atomic events have three properties that make them the foundation of everything that follows:

  • They are mutually exclusive: at most one can be the actual world, since any two differ on the value of some variable.
  • They are exhaustive: at least one must hold, since every variable has some value.
  • Every proposition is logically equivalent to the disjunction of the atomic events that entail it. Hence the probability of any proposition is the sum of the probabilities of its atomic events: P(a) = Σ_{ω ⊨ a} P(ω).

That last property is the engine of the whole topic: once you have a number for each atomic event, every query is a sum.

The axioms of probability

Kolmogorov's axioms are startlingly few. Everything else — the negation rule, inclusion–exclusion, Bayes' rule, variable elimination, particle filters — is derived from them.

Axiom / ruleStatementReading
Range0 ≤ P(a) ≤ 1Belief is bounded; 0 is total disbelief, 1 is total certainty
Normalisation (validity)P(true) = 1, P(false) = 0A necessarily true proposition is fully believed; a contradiction is fully disbelieved
Inclusion–exclusion (additivity)P(a ∨ b) = P(a) + P(b) − P(a ∧ b)Adding the two probabilities double-counts the worlds where both hold, so subtract the overlap once
Negation (derived)P(¬a) = 1 − P(a)Belief in a proposition and its negation must total 1
Variable normalisation (derived)Σ_{x ∈ dom(X)} P(X = x) = 1Some value of every variable must hold

The negation rule is a two-line consequence of the others. Since a ∨ ¬a is necessarily true and a ∧ ¬a is necessarily false:

P(a ∨ ¬a) = P(a) + P(¬a) − P(a ∧ ¬a)     (inclusion–exclusion)
        1 = P(a) + P(¬a) − 0             (validity axiom)
   P(¬a) = 1 − P(a)

The same argument generalises to any variable: the values of Weather are mutually exclusive, so all the P(a ∧ b) overlap terms vanish and the probabilities must sum to exactly 1. That is why ⟨0.6, 0.1, 0.29, 0.01⟩ is a legal belief about the weather but ⟨0.6, 0.1, 0.29, 0.1⟩ is not.

A worked use of inclusion–exclusion. Suppose a single card is drawn from a standard 52-card deck. Let a = "the card is a heart" and b = "the card is a face card (J, Q, K)". Then P(a) = 13/52, P(b) = 12/52, and P(a ∧ b) = 3/52 (jack, queen, king of hearts). Therefore P(a ∨ b) = 13/52 + 12/52 − 3/52 = 22/52 ≈ 0.423. Naively adding would give 0.481 and would count the three red-hearted face cards twice.

Why must a rational agent obey these axioms at all? The Dutch book argument (de Finetti) shows that an agent whose degrees of belief violate them will accept a combination of bets that loses money in every possible world. For instance, an agent believing P(a) = 0.4 and P(¬a) = 0.4 can be sold two bets that each look favourable to it yet guarantee a net loss whichever way the world turns out. Violating the axioms is thus not merely inelegant — it is demonstrably irrational.

Joint probability distributions

The full joint probability distribution assigns a probability to every atomic event. Because the atomic events are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, the entries are non-negative and sum to exactly 1, and — by the disjunction property above — the table answers any query about the domain. Here is the classic dentistry joint over Toothache, Catch and Cavity:

toothache ∧ catchtoothache ∧ ¬catch¬toothache ∧ catch¬toothache ∧ ¬catch
cavity0.1080.0120.0720.008

Check normalisation: 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 + 0.016 + 0.064 + 0.144 + 0.576 = 1.000. Now any question is a sum of cells:

  • P(cavity) = 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 = 0.2
  • P(toothache) = 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.016 + 0.064 = 0.2
  • P(cavity ∨ toothache) = 0.2 + 0.2 − (0.108 + 0.012) = 0.28, matching the six non-zero cells with at least one of the two true: 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 + 0.016 + 0.064 = 0.28.

So why not simply store the full joint for every application? Because of exponential blow-up. With n Boolean variables the table has 2n entries, and because they must sum to 1 exactly 2n − 1 of them are independent numbers that someone must specify.

Three separate problems follow, and all three matter:

  • Storage. A realistic medical domain with a few hundred symptoms and diseases would need more table entries than there are atoms in the observable universe.
  • Elicitation. Even if we could store it, no human expert can meaningfully state a billion numbers, and most of the required conjunctions (P of a precise combination of 30 symptoms) have no intuitive content at all.
  • Learning. Estimating 2n − 1 parameters from data requires astronomically many examples; almost every cell would be estimated from zero observations.

The way out is structure. Suppose Weather is genuinely unrelated to dental health. Then P(Weather, Toothache, Catch, Cavity) = P(Weather) P(Toothache, Catch, Cavity), and instead of 32 entries we need 4 + 8 = 12 (and only 3 + 7 = 10 free numbers). That is absolute independence. It is powerful but rare — few variables in a real domain are totally unrelated. The far more common and more useful pattern is conditional independence: Toothache and Catch are not independent (both indicate a cavity), but once you know whether there is a cavity, learning that the probe caught tells you nothing further about pain:

P(Toothache, Catch | Cavity) = P(Toothache | Cavity) P(Catch | Cavity)

Check it against the table: P(toothache | cavity) = 0.12/0.2 = 0.6, P(catch | cavity) = 0.18/0.2 = 0.9, and their product 0.54 equals P(toothache ∧ catch | cavity) = 0.108/0.2 = 0.54. This single assumption lets us rebuild the whole 7-number joint from just 5 numbers: P(cavity), P(toothache | cavity), P(toothache | ¬cavity), P(catch | cavity), P(catch | ¬cavity). Scaled up, this is exactly what a Bayesian network does, turning exponential tables into a network of small local ones — the central theme of the rest of the module.

Marginalization and conditioning

Two operations extract everything we want from a joint distribution. The first removes variables we do not care about; the second incorporates variables whose values we have observed.

Marginalization (summing out). If a joint distribution is defined over variables Y and Z, the distribution over Y alone is obtained by summing over every value of Z:

P(Y) = Σ_{z ∈ dom(Z)} P(Y, z)

The justification is simply the disjunction property of atomic events: the proposition Y = y is logically equivalent to the disjunction (Y = y ∧ Z = z₁) ∨ (Y = y ∧ Z = z₂) ∨ …, and since those disjuncts are mutually exclusive their probabilities add. The resulting P(Y) is called a marginal distribution — a name inherited from the practice of writing row and column totals in the margins of a printed table. For continuous variables the sum becomes an integral, p(y) = ∫ p(y, z) dz, but the meaning is identical.

Applied to the dentistry table, summing the cavity row over all four combinations of Toothache and Catch gives the marginal P(Cavity) = ⟨0.2, 0.8⟩. Summing instead over Cavity and Catch gives P(Toothache) = ⟨0.2, 0.8⟩. Here are all the one- and two-variable marginals of the table laid out explicitly:

A closely related identity, obtained by applying the product rule inside the sum, is conditioning as a rule of total probability:

P(Y) = Σ_z P(Y | z) P(z)

This says that the belief in Y is a weighted average of the beliefs in Y under each possible value of Z, weighted by how likely each value of Z is. It is used constantly: for example, if P(late | traffic) = 0.7, P(late | ¬traffic) = 0.1 and P(traffic) = 0.3, then P(late) = 0.7(0.3) + 0.1(0.7) = 0.21 + 0.07 = 0.28.

Conditional probability. The conditional probability of a given b is defined as

P(a | b) = P(a ∧ b) / P(b)      provided P(b) > 0

Operationally this is a two-step procedure on the joint table: (1) select the rows consistent with the evidence b, discarding all others; (2) renormalise the survivors by dividing by their total, so that they once again sum to 1. Step 1 captures the logical content of the evidence (worlds inconsistent with what we observed are eliminated), and step 2 preserves the axioms. Note the division is undefined when P(b) = 0: one cannot condition on evidence the model considers impossible.

Worked example. Compute P(Cavity | toothache) from the dentistry table. The cells consistent with toothache are:

cavity ∧ toothache ∧ catch0.1080.54
cavity ∧ toothache ∧ ¬catch0.0120.06
¬cavity ∧ toothache ∧ catch0.0160.08

Summing out Catch from the right-hand column gives P(Cavity | toothache) = ⟨0.54 + 0.06, 0.08 + 0.32⟩ = ⟨0.6, 0.4⟩. The agent's belief in a cavity has risen from the prior 0.2 to the posterior 0.6. Adding a second piece of evidence narrows it further: restricting to toothache ∧ catch leaves cells 0.108 and 0.016, totalling 0.124, so P(Cavity | toothache ∧ catch) = ⟨0.108/0.124, 0.016/0.124⟩ ≈ ⟨0.871, 0.129⟩. Conversely, evidence can push belief down: P(Cavity | ¬toothache) = (0.072 + 0.008)/0.8 = 0.1.

The product rule. Multiplying out the definition gives two equivalent forms of the same fact, since conjunction is commutative:

P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a)

Equating the right-hand two expressions and dividing yields Bayes' rule, P(a | b) = P(b | a) P(a) / P(b) — the single most important consequence of this topic, and the mechanism by which diagnostic beliefs are computed from causal models. Chained over many variables, the product rule also gives the chain rule:

P(X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ) = P(X₁) P(X₂ | X₁) P(X₃ | X₁, X₂) ⋯ P(Xₙ | X₁, …, Xₙ₋₁)

which is exact for any distribution and any variable ordering. Bayesian networks are nothing more than the chain rule with the conditioning sets shrunk down to each variable's parents using conditional independence.

The normalisation constant α. Notice that in the calculation above the denominator P(toothache) did not depend on the query variable Cavity: it was the same divisor for both entries. It is therefore common to postpone the division, compute the unnormalised numbers, and fix them up at the end. The standard notation writes

P(Cavity | toothache) = α P(Cavity, toothache)
                      = α ⟨0.108 + 0.012, 0.016 + 0.064⟩
                      = α ⟨0.12, 0.08⟩
                      = ⟨0.6, 0.4⟩          since α = 1/(0.12 + 0.08) = 5

Here α is simply "whatever constant makes the vector sum to 1". The convenience is substantial: the agent never needs to know P(e), the probability of the evidence, which in a large network can be the most expensive quantity to compute. The same trick appears throughout the module — in the enumeration algorithm, in variable elimination, in likelihood weighting, and in the forward algorithm for HMMs, where the normalising step also keeps the numbers from underflowing to zero over long sequences.

The general form of a probabilistic query combines all of these ideas. For a query variable X, observed evidence e, and hidden variables Y:

P(X | e) = α P(X, e) = α Σ_y P(X, e, y)

Read it right to left: sum out the hidden variables (marginalisation), keep only the entries consistent with the evidence (conditioning), then normalise. Every exact inference algorithm in this module is an attempt to perform this computation without actually building the exponentially large joint table.

Prior, posterior, and conditional beliefs

The vocabulary of belief states deserves to be made precise, because the distinction between prior and posterior drives the whole framework.

TermNotationMeaningDentistry example
Prior / unconditional probabilityP(X)Belief before any domain-specific evidence is obtainedP(cavity) = 0.2 for a randomly chosen patient
Posterior / conditional probabilityP(X | e)Belief after evidence e has been observedP(cavity | toothache) = 0.6
LikelihoodP(e | X)How probable the evidence would be under each hypothesisP(toothache | cavity) = 0.6
JointP(X, e)Belief in the conjunctionP(cavity ∧ toothache) = 0.12

Several points are worth emphasising.

Priors are relative, not absolute. There is no such thing as a truly unconditional probability; P(cavity) = 0.2 is really conditional on the agent's background knowledge — species, age distribution of the clinic's patients, era, diet. We call it a prior because it precedes the particular evidence under discussion. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior: after observing a toothache, 0.6 is the belief the agent carries forward into the next observation.

Evidence updates rather than erases. A common misreading is that conditioning throws away prior knowledge. The opposite is true — the posterior is built from the prior. Bayes' rule makes this explicit: P(X | e) = α P(e | X) P(X), in which the prior P(X) appears as a multiplicative factor. If the prior is tiny, even strong evidence may leave the posterior small; this is the base-rate effect. Suppose a disease has prevalence P(d) = 0.0001 and a test with P(+ | d) = 0.99 and P(+ | ¬d) = 0.01. Then

P(d | +) = α ⟨P(+|d)P(d), P(+|¬d)P(¬d)⟩
         = α ⟨0.99 × 0.0001, 0.01 × 0.9999⟩
         = α ⟨0.000099, 0.009999⟩
         ≈ ⟨0.0098, 0.9902⟩

A positive result from a 99%-accurate test leaves the probability of disease under 1%, because the prior was 10,000 times smaller for disease than for health. An agent that ignored the prior — effectively setting it uniform — would report 99% and be catastrophically wrong.

A posterior is itself a distribution. P(X | e) obeys every axiom: its entries are between 0 and 1, and they sum to 1 over the values of X for any fixed e. That is why α is always well defined and why the posterior can be used as the prior in the next update, or as the input to a decision-theoretic calculation of expected utility. Note carefully that it is not normalised over the evidence: P(cavity | toothache) + P(cavity | ¬toothache) = 0.6 + 0.1 = 0.7, which is not 1 and should not be — these are beliefs in different conditioning contexts, not alternative values of a single variable.

Use all the evidence you have. The principle of total evidence (also discussed under the heading of maximum entropy when choosing priors) requires that the agent condition on the most specific information available to it. If the agent knows toothache ∧ catch, it must not compute with P(Cavity | toothache) = 0.6 and discard the probe result; it must use P(Cavity | toothache ∧ catch) ≈ 0.871. Discarding evidence is the probabilistic analogue of ignoring a known fact in logic, and it can lead to demonstrably worse decisions. The corresponding caution in the other direction is that the agent should not invent structure it does not have: among all distributions consistent with its actual constraints, the maximum-entropy choice is the one that adds no unwarranted commitments — for example, assigning ⟨0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25⟩ to Weather when nothing whatsoever is known about the climate.

Putting it all together, the belief-update cycle of a probabilistic agent is: begin with a prior over the state, receive evidence, condition (select and renormalise), act, and treat the resulting posterior as the prior for the next step. The remainder of the module is devoted to performing this cycle efficiently when the joint distribution is far too large to write down — first by factoring it into a Bayesian network, then by reasoning about independence graphically, then by running exact and approximate inference algorithms over it, and finally by unrolling the cycle through time in Hidden Markov Models.

Intelligent agents almost never have complete information about the world they act in. Sensors are noisy, actions sometimes fail, and the future is only partly predictable. Probability theory gives us a principled language for this situation: instead of asserting that a fact is true or false, an agent maintains a degree of belief, a number between 0 and 1, and updates it as evidence arrives. This topic sets up that language — random variables and their domains, the axioms of probability, joint distributions over several variables, and the operations of marginalization (summing out variables you do not care about) and conditioning (focusing on the worlds consistent with what you have observed).

The vocabulary introduced here is used in every later part of the module. A Bayesian network is nothing more than a compact way of representing a joint distribution; d-separation is a statement about conditional independence; filtering in an HMM is repeated conditioning on new evidence. If the distinction between a prior P(X), a conditional P(X|e), and a posterior belief after evidence is clear to you now, the inference algorithms later will look like bookkeeping rather than magic.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, what exactly is the random variable, and what values can it take? Second, when a probability is written down, what is being conditioned on — what does the agent already know? Third, how does a new observation change the numbers? The first video gives the formal machinery of conditional distributions, the second shows the intuition behind belief revision, and the third is an optional look at continuous distributions for students curious about where Gaussians come from.

Conditional Independence and Bayes' Rule

▶

Probability theory gives an agent a principled way to hold graded beliefs about an uncertain world, but raw probability on its own does not scale. A domain described by n Boolean variables has 2ⁿ possible worlds, and the full joint probability distribution over those worlds needs 2ⁿ − 1 independent numbers. For even a modest medical or diagnostic domain with 30 variables, that is over a billion numbers — impossible to elicit from an expert, impossible to learn reliably from data, and impossible to sum over quickly. The escape route is structure: the observation that most variables in a real domain do not interact directly with most other variables, once we know the right intermediate facts. This topic builds the machinery that turns that observation into computation — conditional probability, the product and chain rules, Bayes' rule, and above all conditional independence, which is the single idea that makes probabilistic reasoning tractable.

Throughout we will use a small running example from dentistry, with three Boolean variables: Cavity (the patient has a cavity), Toothache (the patient reports toothache), and Catch (the dentist's steel probe catches in the tooth). The full joint distribution is given below; every row is one possible world, and the eight numbers sum to 1.

Conditional probability and the product rule. An unconditional (or prior) probability P(a) is the agent's degree of belief in proposition a before any evidence arrives. A conditional (or posterior) probability P(a | b) is the degree of belief in a given that everything the agent knows is summarised by b. The definition is:

P(a | b) = P(a ∧ b) / P(b),   defined whenever P(b) > 0

The division by P(b) has a clear geometric reading. Learning b eliminates every possible world in which b is false. The surviving worlds keep their relative weights, but those weights no longer sum to 1, so we rescale them by dividing by the total surviving mass P(b). Conditioning therefore narrows the set of possible worlds under consideration and renormalises what remains. Using the dentistry table:

P(toothache)          = 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.016 + 0.064 = 0.20
P(cavity ∧ toothache) = 0.108 + 0.012                 = 0.12
P(cavity | toothache) = 0.12 / 0.20 = 0.6

The four worlds in which toothache is false (w₃, w₄, w₇, w₈) have been discarded; of the remaining 0.20 of probability mass, 0.12 involves a cavity, giving 0.6. Note the contrast with the prior P(cavity) = 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 = 0.2. The evidence has tripled our belief.

Multiplying out the definition gives the product rule, which is the workhorse of everything that follows:

P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a)

In words: for both a and b to hold, b must hold and then a must hold given b — or equivalently, with the roles swapped. The symmetry of the two forms is exactly what will give us Bayes' rule. When written for whole variables rather than single propositions, P(X, Y) = P(X | Y) P(Y) is a set of equations, one for each combination of values of X and Y.

Two conceptual warnings are worth stating explicitly. First, a conditional probability is not a causal claim. P(cavity | toothache) = 0.6 does not say that toothache produces cavities; it says that among patients with toothache, 60% have a cavity. Probabilistic conditioning runs in whatever direction we point it, and the arithmetic is blind to which way causality actually flows. Conditional probabilities express updated belief, not mechanism. Second, P(a | b) is not the probability that "b causes a" nor the probability of a conditional statement in logic; it is simply a ratio of masses within the joint distribution.

A pleasant and important fact is that all the usual axioms of probability continue to hold inside a conditioned distribution. Fixing the evidence b and letting P_b(a) = P(a | b) defines a perfectly ordinary probability distribution: P_b(a) ≥ 0; P_b(true) = 1; P_b(a ∨ a′) = P_b(a) + P_b(a′) for mutually exclusive a and a′; and P_b(¬a) = 1 − P_b(a). Consequently every rule we derive for unconditional probabilities — marginalisation, the product rule, Bayes' rule itself — has a conditioned twin obtained by writing | b after every term. For instance P(a ∧ c | b) = P(a | c, b) P(c | b). This "background evidence stays along for the ride" principle is used constantly in Bayesian network inference.

The chain rule and the full joint distribution. The full joint distribution is a complete specification of the agent's beliefs: any question about the domain can be answered from it. The product rule, applied repeatedly, lets us decompose the joint into a product of conditionals. For three variables:

P(X₁, X₂, X₃) = P(X₃ | X₁, X₂) P(X₁, X₂)
              = P(X₃ | X₁, X₂) P(X₂ | X₁) P(X₁)

and in general the chain rule is

P(X₁, …, Xₙ) = ∏ᵢ₌₁ⁿ P(Xᵢ | X₁, …, Xᵢ₋₁)

This is an identity: it is true for every joint distribution, with no assumptions whatsoever. It is also true for any ordering of the variables, since nothing in the derivation privileged a particular sequence. Applied to the dentistry example we could write either

P(C, T, K) = P(C) P(T | C) P(K | C, T)
P(C, T, K) = P(K) P(T | K) P(C | K, T)

and both are exactly correct. This freedom matters enormously in practice: it is the licence to organise a model around causes first (Cavity before its symptoms) rather than symptoms first. Both orderings represent the same distribution, but — as we shall see — the causal ordering is the one in which many of the conditional terms simplify, because P(K | C, T) will turn out to equal P(K | C), whereas in the reverse ordering no such simplification occurs. Choosing a bad ordering yields a mathematically valid but needlessly dense factorisation.

Three operations let us extract answers from a joint distribution.

  • Marginalisation (summing out). To obtain the distribution over a subset of variables, sum over all values of the rest: P(Y) = Σ_z P(Y, z), where z ranges over all value combinations of the other variables Z. In the table, P(Cavity = true) = 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 = 0.2.
  • Conditioning. Substituting the product rule into marginalisation gives P(Y) = Σ_z P(Y | z) P(z), sometimes called the law of total probability. This is the form used when a model stores conditionals rather than joint entries — which is precisely how Bayesian networks store their parameters.
  • Normalisation. A query of the form P(Y | e) requires dividing by P(e), but we can often avoid computing P(e) directly. Write P(Y | e) = α P(Y, e), compute the unnormalised vector of values, then choose the constant α so the entries sum to 1.

Normalisation in action for the dentistry example:

P(Cavity | toothache) = α ⟨ P(cavity, toothache), P(¬cavity, toothache) ⟩
                      = α ⟨ 0.108 + 0.012, 0.016 + 0.064 ⟩
                      = α ⟨ 0.12, 0.08 ⟩
                      = ⟨ 0.6, 0.4 ⟩        (α = 1 / 0.20 = 5)

We never had to think about P(toothache) as a quantity in its own right; it appeared automatically as 1/α. The general query pattern, combining all three operations, is

P(X | e) = α P(X, e) = α Σ_y P(X, e, y)

where X is the query variable, e the observed evidence, and Y the hidden variables. This single formula answers any probabilistic question from a joint distribution — and its cost, O(2ⁿ) for n Boolean variables, is exactly the problem that conditional independence will address.

Bayes' rule: inverting the direction of inference. Equating the two forms of the product rule, P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a), and dividing by P(b) gives:

P(h | e) = P(e | h) P(h) / P(e)

posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence,   or   posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

Each term has a standard name and role. P(h) is the prior, the belief in hypothesis h before seeing evidence. P(e | h) is the likelihood, how well the hypothesis predicts the observed evidence. P(h | e) is the posterior, the updated belief. P(e) is the evidence or marginal likelihood.

The deep practical value of Bayes' rule is that it lets us compute a quantity we want but cannot easily estimate from quantities we can. Causal knowledge of the form P(effect | cause) is typically more stable and easier to obtain than diagnostic knowledge of the form P(cause | effect). A physiologist can tell you that roughly 70% of meningitis patients present with a stiff neck; that figure reflects the mechanism of the disease and holds across hospitals, seasons, and populations. The diagnostic figure P(meningitis | stiff neck), by contrast, depends on how common meningitis currently is — during an epidemic it rises sharply, even though nothing about the disease's physiology changed. Bayes' rule lets a system store the robust causal numbers and derive the fragile diagnostic ones on demand, plugging in whatever prior is appropriate.

Meningitis example
P(stiff neck | meningitis) = 0.7      (causal, stable)
P(meningitis)              = 1/50000  = 0.00002
P(stiff neck)              = 0.01

P(meningitis | stiff neck) = 0.7 × 0.00002 / 0.01 = 0.0014

Fewer than one in seven hundred stiff-necked patients has meningitis, despite the very high likelihood term. The low prior dominates.

As with any conditional query, the denominator is just a normalising constant. Since P(e) = Σ_h P(e | h) P(h) does not depend on which value of H we are scoring, we can write P(H | e) = α P(e | H) P(H), compute the unnormalised products for every value of H, and normalise at the end. For the meningitis case we would need P(stiff neck | ¬meningitis) to do this; the point is that one of the two routes is always available, and we pick whichever matches the numbers we actually possess.

Failing to multiply by the prior is the base-rate fallacy, perhaps the most consequential reasoning error in applied probability. Consider a screening test for a disease affecting 1 in 1000 people, with 99% sensitivity (P(+ | disease) = 0.99) and a 1% false-positive rate (P(+ | ¬disease) = 0.01).

QuantitySymbolic formValue

SensitivityP(+ | d)0.99
False-positive rateP(+ | ¬d)0.01
Unnormalised, diseaseP(+ | d) P(d)0.00099
Unnormalised, no diseaseP(+ | ¬d) P(¬d)0.00999

A positive result on a 99%-accurate test leaves only a 9% chance of disease. The intuition people reach for — "the test is 99% accurate, so I'm 99% likely to be ill" — silently substitutes the likelihood P(+ | d) for the posterior P(d | +), discarding the base rate. A frequency framing makes it vivid: in 100,000 people, about 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive, while 99,900 are healthy and about 999 of those also test positive. Of the 1098 positives, only 99 are genuine — 9%. Rare conditions are massively over-diagnosed whenever priors are ignored.

Finally, Bayesian updating is incremental: a posterior computed after one piece of evidence becomes the prior for the next. Suppose the patient above takes a second, independent test (independent given disease status) and it is also positive. Using the previous posterior 0.0902 as the new prior:

P(d | +₁, +₂) = α P(+₂ | d) P(d | +₁)
              = α ⟨ 0.99 × 0.0902,  0.01 × 0.9098 ⟩
              = α ⟨ 0.08930, 0.00910 ⟩
              ≈ ⟨ 0.907, 0.093 ⟩

Belief jumps to about 91%. The same answer is obtained by applying both likelihoods at once to the original prior, α ⟨0.99² × 0.001, 0.01² × 0.999⟩ ≈ ⟨0.907, 0.093⟩, which demonstrates order invariance: the final posterior does not depend on the sequence in which evidence arrives. A neat way to see this is in odds form, where Bayes' rule becomes "posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds": prior odds 1:999, each positive test multiplies by 99, so two tests give 99² : 999 = 9801 : 999 ≈ 9.81, i.e. a probability of 0.907. Crucially, this incremental form requires the conditional independence of the two tests given the disease; if both tests share a hidden flaw, the second provides far less new information than the formula suggests.

Independence and conditional independence. Two propositions or variables are absolutely (marginally) independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y)      ⟺      P(X | Y) = P(X)      ⟺      P(Y | X) = P(Y)

Independence is enormously valuable when it holds, because it lets the joint distribution split into separate factors. If a dentistry domain were extended with Weather, and weather were independent of all dental variables, then P(Cavity, Toothache, Catch, Weather) = P(Cavity, Toothache, Catch) P(Weather), reducing a 32-entry table (for a four-valued Weather) to an 8-entry table plus a 4-entry table. Unfortunately, absolute independence is rare in interesting domains: almost everything is weakly correlated with almost everything else, because variables share common causes. In our dental table, Toothache and Catch are definitely not independent — P(catch) = 0.108 + 0.072 + 0.016 + 0.144 = 0.34 while P(catch | toothache) = (0.108 + 0.016)/0.2 = 0.62.

Conditional independence is the far more common and far more useful notion. X and Y are conditionally independent given Z — written X ⫫ Y | Z — when:

Equivalently, in the forms that are most useful in derivations:

P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) P(Y | Z)
P(X | Y, Z) = P(X | Z)
P(Y | X, Z) = P(Y | Z)

The second form carries the intuition: once Z is known, learning Y adds nothing further about X. The information that Y carried about X was information about Z, and Z is now known directly, so Y becomes redundant. Note also that the three forms are equivalent only when the conditioning probabilities are non-zero, and that conditional independence is symmetric in X and Y.

The canonical generator of conditional independence is the common cause. Toothache and Catch are correlated, but only because both are caused by Cavity: the decay irritates the nerve (producing pain) and leaves a soft hole (producing a catch). The two symptoms do not influence one another directly. So once we know whether there is a cavity, the presence of a toothache tells the dentist nothing more about whether the probe will catch. Check this against the numbers:

P(catch | toothache, cavity) = 0.108 / (0.108 + 0.012) = 0.9
P(catch | cavity)            = (0.108 + 0.072) / 0.2   = 0.9     ✓

P(catch | toothache, ¬cavity) = 0.016 / (0.016 + 0.064) = 0.2
P(catch | ¬cavity)            = (0.016 + 0.144) / 0.8   = 0.2     ✓

So Toothache ⫫ Catch | Cavity holds exactly in this distribution, even though the two are strongly dependent unconditionally. The same pattern recurs everywhere: two medical tests for the same condition, two thermometers in the same room, two witnesses to the same event, two sensors on the same robot. In each case the readings are correlated, and in each case the correlation is fully explained by the shared underlying state.

It is essential to appreciate that conditional independence and absolute independence are logically unrelated — neither implies the other.

  • Conditionally independent but not absolutely independent: the dental example above. Toothache and Catch are dependent (0.62 vs 0.34) yet conditionally independent given Cavity. Conditioning on a common cause removes a dependence.
  • Absolutely independent but not conditionally independent: let Burglary and Earthquake be independent events, each of which can set off an Alarm. Before any observation, hearing about an earthquake tells you nothing about burglary. But given that the alarm has sounded, learning that there was an earthquake lowers the probability of a burglary — the earthquake already explains the alarm. This is explaining away, and it shows that conditioning on a common effect can create a dependence where none existed.

The following table summarises the two structural patterns, both of which will reappear as the core cases of d-separation in Bayesian networks.

A last caution: conditional independence is a property of a particular distribution relative to a particular conditioning set. X ⫫ Y | Z does not entail X ⫫ Y | Z, W, nor does it entail X ⫫ Y. Independence claims must always be stated together with what is being conditioned on.

Why conditional independence makes inference tractable. Return to the dental joint distribution. Written by the chain rule in the causal ordering, and then simplified using Toothache ⫫ Catch | Cavity:

P(Toothache, Catch, Cavity) = P(Toothache | Catch, Cavity) P(Catch | Cavity) P(Cavity)
                            = P(Toothache | Cavity) P(Catch | Cavity) P(Cavity)

Count the numbers. The original table needed 2³ − 1 = 7 independent parameters. The factored form needs 1 for P(Cavity), 2 for P(Toothache | Cavity) and 2 for P(Catch | Cavity) — just 5. A saving of two numbers is unimpressive, but the pattern generalises spectacularly. Suppose a single cause C with n effects E₁, …, Eₙ that are mutually conditionally independent given C. Then

P(C, E₁, …, Eₙ) = P(C) ∏ᵢ₌₁ⁿ P(Eᵢ | C)

This is the naive Bayes (or Bayesian-classifier) model. Its parameter count is 1 + 2n, i.e. O(n), against the 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1 entries of the unfactored joint. The contrast is worth tabulating.

The exponential-to-linear collapse buys four distinct advantages.

  • Storage and specification. Sixty-one numbers can be written down; two billion cannot. The model becomes representable at all.
  • Learning from data. Each parameter of the factored model is estimated from a comparatively large slice of the data — P(Eᵢ | c) uses every example with C = c, regardless of the other effects' values. Estimating a full joint entry instead requires examples matching all n+1 variables simultaneously, and with 30 variables almost every cell would be empty. Factorisation therefore dramatically reduces the sample complexity of learning and yields far lower-variance estimates.
  • Elicitation from experts. Asking a clinician "of patients with condition C, what fraction show symptom Eᵢ?" is a question they can answer from training and experience. Asking "what is the probability of this exact combination of twelve symptoms together with the disease?" is unanswerable. Small, locally meaningful tables map onto the way human experts actually hold knowledge.
  • Speed of inference. Diagnostic queries become cheap. With naive Bayes, P(C | e₁, …, eₙ) = α P(C) ∏ᵢ P(eᵢ | C) — a single pass multiplying n small factors, then normalising, instead of summing over exponentially many joint entries. Applied to the dental case with both symptoms observed: 
    P(Cavity | toothache, catch) = α P(Cavity) P(toothache | Cavity) P(catch | Cavity)
                             = α ⟨ 0.2 × 0.6 × 0.9,  0.8 × 0.1 × 0.2 ⟩
                             = α ⟨ 0.108, 0.016 ⟩
                             ≈ ⟨ 0.871, 0.129 ⟩
    which agrees exactly with reading the figures straight out of the full joint table (0.108 and 0.016), as it must.

These independence assumptions are precisely what a Bayesian network encodes graphically. A directed acyclic graph with one node per variable and an arrow from each direct cause to each direct effect is simply a picture of a chain-rule factorisation in which each variable has been made conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents:

P(X₁, …, Xₙ) = ∏ᵢ P(Xᵢ | Parents(Xᵢ))

Compare this with the unrestricted chain rule ∏ᵢ P(Xᵢ | X₁, …, Xᵢ₋₁): the network replaces the full set of predecessors with the much smaller parent set, and every arrow absent from the graph is an explicit assertion of conditional independence. The dental network is Toothache ← Cavity → Catch, a three-node fork; the missing arrow between the two symptoms is the claim Toothache ⫫ Catch | Cavity. The size of a node's conditional probability table is exponential only in its number of parents, so sparse graphs give compact models. This is also why the freedom to choose any variable ordering matters: ordering causes before effects produces a sparse network, whereas ordering effects before causes produces a tangled one that encodes the same distribution with far more parameters.

The trade-off must be stated honestly. Independence assumptions are assumptions, and if they are wrong the model is systematically — not just randomly — biased. Errors do not wash out with more data; the model converges confidently to the wrong answer. Naive Bayes applied to text, for example, treats the words in a document as conditionally independent given the topic, which is plainly false ("New" and "York" co-occur far more than the model allows). The practical consequence is well known: naive Bayes classifiers often pick the right class but produce wildly overconfident posteriors, because correlated evidence is counted as if it were independent and the likelihood terms multiply too aggressively. The same failure appears in the two-test example above: if both tests share a common flaw, treating them as conditionally independent given the disease over-counts the second positive result. Good modelling therefore means asking, for every absent arrow, "is there a path of influence here that my conditioning set does not block?" — and, where there is, either adding the arrow or introducing a hidden variable that genuinely explains the correlation. The art of probabilistic modelling lies exactly in trading a little fidelity for a great deal of tractability, with eyes open about which is being given up.

▶

A full joint probability distribution over a set of random variables is the most complete probabilistic model one can write down: it answers every possible query about the domain. Unfortunately, it is also almost always unusable. With n Boolean variables the joint has 2^n entries and therefore 2^n - 1 free parameters (one is fixed by the requirement that the entries sum to 1). For thirty Boolean variables that is more than a billion numbers — numbers that nobody can elicit from an expert, estimate from data, or store conveniently. A Bayesian network is the standard answer to this problem: it exploits the fact that in most real domains each variable is directly influenced by only a handful of others, and encodes those direct influences as a graph. The payoff is twofold. First, the model becomes compact enough to build and to reason with. Second, and just as importantly, the graph is an explicit, inspectable statement of the independence assumptions being made, so a modeller can argue about structure and about numbers separately.

From joint distributions to graphs

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) in which each node is a random variable — discrete or continuous — and each directed edge from X to Y asserts that X is a direct parent, i.e. a direct influence, on Y. "Acyclic" matters: you may not follow directed edges and return to where you started, because a cycle would make the definition of the joint distribution circular and ill-defined. Associated with each node Xi is a conditional probability table (CPT) specifying P(Xi | Parents(Xi)) — a complete probability distribution over the node's values for every combination of values of its parents.

The canonical example is Judea Pearl's burglar alarm network. You have installed an alarm at home that is fairly reliable at detecting burglaries, but which is also occasionally set off by minor earthquakes. Two neighbours, John and Mary, have promised to call you at work if they hear the alarm. John is conscientious but sometimes mistakes the telephone ringing for the alarm; Mary likes loud music and sometimes misses the alarm altogether. The structure is:

   Burglary        Earthquake
        \             /
         \           /
          v         v
            Alarm
           /     \
          v       v
   JohnCalls   MaryCalls

Note what the graph says and does not say. Burglary and Earthquake both influence the Alarm directly. Neither influences John or Mary directly: the only way a burglary causes John to call is by setting off the alarm, and that assumption is encoded by the absence of an edge from Burglary to JohnCalls. Likewise there is no edge between JohnCalls and MaryCalls: whatever correlation exists between their calls is entirely explained by the common cause, the alarm.

The mathematical content of the network is the chain rule factorization:

P(X1, X2, ..., Xn) = ∏(i = 1..n) P(Xi | Parents(Xi))

This is derived from the general chain rule of probability, P(x1,...,xn) = ∏i P(xi | x1,...,xi-1), which holds for any distribution. The network adds the claim that for a suitable ordering of the variables, P(xi | x1,...,xi-1) = P(xi | parents(xi)) where Parents(Xi) ⊆ {X1,...,Xi-1}. The conditioning set shrinks from "everything earlier" to "the few direct causes", and that is precisely the compression.

Because the factorization is an equality, any entry of the full joint can be recovered by multiplying the relevant CPT entries. For instance, the probability that the alarm sounds, both neighbours call, and there is neither a burglary nor an earthquake is:

P(j, m, a, ¬b, ¬e)
  = P(j | a) · P(m | a) · P(a | ¬b, ¬e) · P(¬b) · P(¬e)
  = 0.90 × 0.70 × 0.001 × 0.999 × 0.998
  ≈ 0.000628

Since every joint entry is recoverable, the network is a complete model: marginal, conditional and posterior queries are all computable from it (even if, in practice, we compute them with smarter algorithms than by reconstructing the whole joint).

Conditional probability tables

A CPT is organised with one row per assignment to the parents and one column per value of the node. For a Boolean node with k Boolean parents there are 2^k rows, and because each row is a distribution over two values that sums to 1, each row contributes only one independent parameter. Hence such a node requires 2^k independent numbers. More generally, a node with v values and parents having v1,...,vk values requires (v - 1) · ∏j vj parameters. By convention we often display only the "true" column for Boolean nodes and leave the complement implicit.

Where do the numbers come from? Three routes, often combined:

  • Expert elicitation. A domain expert supplies values such as "if the alarm goes off, John calls about nine times out of ten". Experts are generally better at estimating causal conditionals (P(effect | cause)) than diagnostic ones, which is one reason for preferring causal structure.
  • Learned frequencies. Given a data set of complete observations, the maximum-likelihood estimate of a CPT entry is simply a count ratio: P(xi | pai) = N(xi, pai) / N(pai). Because sparse parent sets mean each row pools many records, estimates are far more reliable than estimating the full joint from the same data. Smoothing (e.g. adding a pseudo-count of 1 to each cell) avoids assigning probability 0 to events merely unobserved in a finite sample.
  • A mix. Common in practice: structure and rare-event priors from experts, frequent-event parameters from data, with expert priors acting as Bayesian pseudo-counts that are gradually overwhelmed as data accumulates.

Network semantics and local independence

There are two equivalent ways to say what a Bayesian network means, and it is worth being fluent in both.

The global semantics defines the network as shorthand for a joint distribution: the joint is the product of all the CPTs, as in the chain rule factorization above. This view is what you use when computing — enumeration, variable elimination and sampling all ultimately multiply CPT entries.

The local semantics states an independence property: each variable is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. Formally, for each X, X ⊥ NonDescendants(X) | Parents(X). In the alarm network, JohnCalls is conditionally independent of Burglary, Earthquake and MaryCalls given Alarm — once you know whether the alarm sounded, learning that a burglary occurred tells you nothing further about whether John will call. The restriction to non-descendants is essential: JohnCalls is certainly not independent of its descendants given its parents, and in a longer chain knowing a downstream effect does change beliefs upstream.

These two statements are mathematically equivalent: a distribution factorises according to the graph if and only if it satisfies the local Markov property with respect to the graph. Global semantics is easier to compute with; local semantics is easier to think with when deciding whether a proposed structure is honest.

A third, stronger local property concerns the Markov blanket of a node X, written MB(X), which consists of:

  • the parents of X,
  • the children of X, and
  • the other parents of those children (sometimes called the node's "spouses").

The theorem is that a node is conditionally independent of every other node in the network given its Markov blanket: X ⊥ (AllOthers \ MB(X)) | MB(X). The spouses must be included because, as we shall see with colliders, observing a shared child creates a dependency between its parents.

The Markov blanket is not merely a curiosity: it is the computational heart of Gibbs sampling, where each variable is resampled from its distribution given its blanket, a quantity proportional to P(x | parents(x)) · ∏children y P(y | parents(y)) and therefore cheap to evaluate locally no matter how large the network is.

Finally, a point of modelling discipline: missing edges are strong claims. An edge that is present merely permits a dependency; the CPT may still make the influence negligible. An edge that is absent forbids a direct dependency outright and cannot be repaired by tuning numbers. Consequently, when you are unsure whether a direct influence exists, the conservative move is to include the edge: an unnecessary edge costs parameters and inference time but leaves the model capable of representing the true distribution, whereas a missing necessary edge makes the model structurally wrong and no amount of data will fix it. The trade-off is real — a network with too many edges is harder to elicit and may overfit — so the usual advice is to add edges you doubt only when the resulting CPT remains elicitable.

Constructing a network in practice

Network construction proceeds in two stages: choose and order the variables, then determine parents and fill in CPTs.

Choosing variables. Good variables are:

  • Relevant to the queries you intend to ask or to the evidence you expect to observe — if a variable is never queried, never observed, and never needed to mediate a dependency, leave it out;
  • Mutually exclusive in their values, so that exactly one value is true at a time (a variable Weather ∈ {sunny, rain, cloudy, snow} is well formed; a variable whose values are "raining" and "cold" is not, because both can hold);
  • Exhaustive, so that at least one value is always true — this often means adding a catch-all value such as other or none. Together, mutual exclusivity and exhaustiveness guarantee that the distribution over the variable sums to exactly 1.

It also pays to fix the granularity carefully. Modelling Temperature as a Boolean Hot may be too coarse to capture the dependency you care about; modelling it to the nearest 0.1°C makes CPTs impossible to fill. Discretising into a few meaningful bands is usually the right compromise.

The construction algorithm. Once variables are chosen:

1. Order the variables X1, ..., Xn so that causes come before their effects.
2. For i = 1 to n:
     a. Choose the MINIMAL subset Parents(Xi) ⊆ {X1, ..., Xi-1}
        such that  P(Xi | X1, ..., Xi-1) = P(Xi | Parents(Xi)).
     b. Draw an edge from each member of Parents(Xi) to Xi.
     c. Fill in the CPT P(Xi | Parents(Xi)).

Because every parent is drawn from variables earlier in the ordering, the result is guaranteed acyclic. And because step 2a enforces an equality, the product of the CPTs is guaranteed to equal the true joint — the network is consistent with the real distribution by construction, with no redundant and possibly contradictory numbers anywhere.

Why causal order matters. The algorithm works for any ordering, but the resulting networks differ enormously in size and intelligibility. Compare three orderings of the alarm domain:

OrderingCharacterEdges requiredTypical parameter required
Burglary, Earthquake, Alarm, JohnCalls, MaryCallsCausal (causes first)4P(JohnCalls | Alarm) — easy
MaryCalls, JohnCalls, Alarm, Burglary, EarthquakeDiagnostic (symptoms first)6P(Earthquake | Alarm, Burglary) — hard

Work through the diagnostic ordering to see why it degrades. Starting with MaryCalls, we ask whether JohnCalls is independent of it: no — if Mary calls, the alarm probably sounded, so John is more likely to call too. Edge MaryCalls → JohnCalls. Next, Alarm clearly depends on both calls, so two more edges. Then Burglary: given the Alarm, do the calls tell us anything more? No — so Burglary gets a single parent, Alarm. Finally Earthquake: given the Alarm, knowing a burglary occurred does matter, because a burglary explains away the alarm and makes an earthquake less likely; so Earthquake needs both Alarm and Burglary as parents. The result is a six-edge network that encodes exactly the same distribution as the four-edge causal one, but whose numbers include quantities such as P(Earthquake | Alarm, ¬Burglary) that no seismologist or homeowner can estimate directly. The third, arbitrary ordering is worse still: it ends up fully connected, with 31 parameters — the full joint in disguise, and no independence captured at all.

The general lesson is that causal orderings typically produce far fewer edges and more intuitive probabilities than diagnostic orderings. Causal conditionals are stable properties of mechanisms (how reliably does an alarm trigger given a break-in?), whereas diagnostic conditionals bundle together the mechanism and the prevalence of the causes, so they change whenever the population changes. A practical heuristic: for each candidate variable ask "what directly brings this about?" rather than "what does this tell me about?", and let the arrows run from the answer to the question.

A short worked construction illustrates the whole procedure. Suppose we want to model a car that may fail to start. Variables: Battery (charged/flat), FuelInTank (yes/no), Starts (yes/no), Lights (on/off), FuelGauge (full/empty). A causal ordering is Battery, FuelInTank, Starts, Lights, FuelGauge. Battery and FuelInTank are roots. Starts depends on both (you need charge and fuel), so it has parents {Battery, FuelInTank}. Lights depend only on the battery — given the battery state, fuel and starting tell us nothing more — so Parents(Lights) = {Battery}. FuelGauge depends on the fuel level, and (in a slightly more careful model) also on the battery, since a dead battery leaves the gauge unpowered; take Parents(FuelGauge) = {FuelInTank, Battery}. The graph is:

  Battery ----------------+-------------------+
      |                   |                   |
      v                   v                   v
   Lights              Starts  <---- FuelInTank ----> FuelGauge
                                                  (also <- Battery)

Parameter count: 1 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 4 = 12, against 31 for the joint. More importantly, each number is something a mechanic could estimate: P(Starts = yes | Battery = charged, Fuel = yes) = 0.98, P(Lights = on | Battery = flat) = 0.02, and so on.

Reading independence with d-separation

Local semantics tells us about each node and its parents, but we often want a general question answered: given evidence E, are sets X and Y conditionally independent? D-separation ("directed separation") is a purely graphical criterion that answers it without touching a single number. The idea is to treat the graph as a network of pipes along which dependency can flow, and ask whether evidence blocks every pipe between X and Y.

Consider an undirected path (a sequence of edges ignoring their direction) between a node in X and a node in Y. Every interior node Z on that path sits in one of exactly three local configurations, and each has its own blocking rule:

ConfigurationShapeNameBlocked whenOpen when
ChainX → Z → YCausal chain / mediatorZ is observedZ is unobserved
ForkX ← Z → YCommon causeZ is observedZ is unobserved
ColliderX → Z ← YCommon effect / v-structureNeither Z nor any descendant of Z is observedZ or a descendant of Z is observed

The first two rules match intuition. In a chain Burglary → Alarm → JohnCalls, learning that John called raises the probability of a burglary — information flows. But if you already know whether the alarm sounded, John's call adds nothing about burglary: the mediator, once observed, absorbs the entire influence. In a fork JohnCalls ← Alarm → MaryCalls, the two calls are correlated through their common cause, but once the alarm's state is known they become independent — this is the classic "controlling for the confounder" situation.

The collider rule runs the other way and is the one students most often get backwards. In Burglary → Alarm ← Earthquake, with nothing observed, burglary and earthquake are independent — the a priori chance of an earthquake has nothing to do with whether a thief is at work. But condition on the alarm sounding, and they become dependent: if you then learn there was an earthquake, the earthquake explains away the alarm and the probability of a burglary drops. Observing a common effect couples its causes. The rule extends to descendants because observing anything downstream of the collider gives indirect information about the collider itself: in the alarm network, learning JohnCalls = true (a descendant of Alarm) is enough to make Burglary and Earthquake dependent, even though Alarm itself is unobserved.

Putting the rules together: a path is blocked by evidence E if at least one node on it is blocked by the rules above; the path is active (or "d-connecting") if every node on it is open. Then:

X and Y are d-separated by E  ⇔  EVERY undirected path from any node in X
                                    to any node in Y is blocked by E.

d-separated  ⇒  X ⊥ Y | E  in every distribution that factorises over the graph.

Two practical remarks on that last line. D-separation is sound: whatever it declares independent really is independent, in every distribution consistent with the DAG — the conclusion follows from the graph alone and never depends on the particular CPT numbers. The converse direction is weaker: if X and Y are d-connected, they are dependent in almost all distributions over the graph, but a special choice of parameters can create an accidental independence that the graph does not advertise. So d-separation tells you what the model guarantees, not everything that might happen to be true.

A slightly larger example shows how to handle multiple paths. Extend the network with a node Radio reporting an earthquake, with Earthquake → Radio, and a node Neighbourhood that influences both Burglary and a new node Vandalism. Ask: is Radio d-separated from Vandalism given Alarm? Enumerate the paths: Radio ← Earthquake → Alarm ← Burglary ← Neighbourhood → Vandalism. Walk it node by node. At Earthquake we have a fork, unobserved, so open. At Alarm we have a collider, and Alarm is observed, so open. At Burglary we have a chain Alarm ← Burglary ← Neighbourhood — Burglary is unobserved, so open. At Neighbourhood we have a fork, unobserved, so open. Every node is open, the path is active, and therefore Radio and Vandalism are not d-separated given Alarm: hearing an earthquake report lowers suspicion of a burglary, which lowers our estimate that this is a rough neighbourhood, which lowers the probability of vandalism. Add Burglary to the evidence set and the same path is blocked at Burglary (chain, observed) and the two become independent again — a nice illustration that adding evidence can create independences and also destroy them, depending on which configuration the evidence lands in.

Notice that the Markov blanket result is simply a corollary of d-separation. Every path leaving X must exit through a parent (blocked by the observed parent, as a chain or fork), through a child along a chain (blocked by the observed child), or through a child acting as a collider — and that collider is observed, so it is open, but the path must then continue through one of the child's other parents, which are also in the blanket and block it. Hence conditioning on parents, children and spouses seals X off from the rest of the graph.

Two everyday uses of the criterion close the topic. First, verifying modelling assumptions: after drawing a structure, pick pairs of variables whose relationship you understand well, read off what the graph claims about them, and check it against domain knowledge. If the graph says two symptoms are independent given a disease and the clinician objects that one symptom aggravates the other, you have found a missing edge — this is far cheaper than discovering the error after the model is deployed. Second, pruning irrelevant variables before inference: for a query P(X | E), any variable that is d-separated from X by E contributes nothing and can be deleted. A standard, easily checked special case is that every node that is neither an ancestor of a query variable nor an ancestor of an evidence variable is irrelevant and may be dropped — its CPT always sums away to 1. In the alarm network, a query for P(Burglary | JohnCalls = true) allows us to discard MaryCalls entirely, shrinking the summations that enumeration or variable elimination must perform. In large diagnostic networks with hundreds of nodes, this pruning step often removes the great majority of the graph and is the difference between an intractable query and an instant one.

A full joint probability distribution answers every probabilistic question you could ask about a set of variables, but it is hopelessly large: n binary variables require 2^n - 1 numbers. Bayesian networks solve this by exploiting conditional independence. Each variable becomes a node in a directed acyclic graph, arrows point from direct causes (parents) to effects, and each node carries a conditional probability table giving the distribution of that variable given its parents. The product of these local tables reconstructs the full joint distribution, usually with far fewer parameters.

This topic covers both halves of that idea. Inference in Bayesian Networks

▶

A Bayesian network is a compact, factored representation of a joint probability distribution over a set of random variables. Building the network is only half the story: the reason we build it is to ask questions of it. Inference is the process of computing the probability of some things we care about given some things we have observed. This topic examines what that computation actually involves, two exact algorithms (enumeration and variable elimination) that compute the answer precisely, three sampling algorithms (rejection sampling, likelihood weighting, and Gibbs sampling) that approximate it, and the computational considerations that determine which method is appropriate for a given network.

Throughout, we will use the classic burglary–alarm network as a running example. Its structure is Burglary → Alarm, Earthquake → Alarm, Alarm → JohnCalls, Alarm → MaryCalls. All five variables are Boolean; we write b for Burglary = true and ¬b for Burglary = false, and similarly for the others. The conditional probability tables (CPTs) are:

The inference problem. Every inference query partitions the network's variables into three disjoint roles. The query variables X are the ones whose distribution we want. The evidence variables E are those whose values e we have observed and therefore fix. Everything else is a hidden or nuisance variable Y — not asked about and not observed, but still part of the model and therefore still influencing the answer. In the query P(Burglary | JohnCalls = true, MaryCalls = true), the query variable is Burglary, the evidence variables are JohnCalls and MaryCalls, and the hidden variables are Earthquake and Alarm. Note that a variable's role is a property of the query, not of the network: in a different query Earthquake might be the query variable and Burglary hidden.

The semantics of a Bayesian network give the full joint distribution as a product of CPT entries, one per node:

P(x₁, …, xₙ) = Π P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ))

From the joint, every query is answered by the same two mechanical operations: marginalise (sum out) the hidden variables, then normalise so the remaining distribution over the query variable sums to one:

P(X | e) = α P(X, e) = α Σ_y P(X, e, y)

where α is the normalising constant 1 / Σ_x Σ_y P(x, e, y). The normalisation step is what lets us ignore the denominator P(e) of Bayes' rule during the computation and recover it at the end. This equation is conceptually complete — everything else in this topic is about computing it without enumerating a table of size exponential in n.

Query types. Three families of question are standard:

  • Posterior marginals: P(X | e) for one variable or a small set, e.g. "given that both neighbours called, how likely is a burglary?" This is the most common query and the one the algorithms below target by default.
  • Most probable explanation (MPE), sometimes called the most likely assignment: find the single joint assignment to all non-evidence variables that maximises P(y | e). Here we replace the summation operator by a maximisation and keep back-pointers to recover the argmax. Crucially, the MPE is not obtained by taking the most likely value of each variable separately — the variable-by-variable maximum may be a jointly improbable or even impossible configuration.
  • Maximum a posteriori (MAP) assignment: maximise over a chosen subset of variables while summing out the rest, i.e. argmax_x Σ_y P(x, y | e). Because this mixes max and sum operators, which do not commute, MAP is strictly harder than MPE — it sits in the complexity class NPPP rather than merely being NP-hard.

Complexity. Exact inference in general Bayesian networks is NP-hard. The standard proof reduces 3-SAT to the problem: build a network whose root variables are the propositional variables (each true with probability 0.5), with deterministic nodes implementing each clause and a final conjunction node; then P(output = true) > 0 exactly when the formula is satisfiable, and the actual value of P(output = true) equals the fraction of satisfying assignments, making the counting version #P-hard. Two consequences matter for practice. First, no algorithm will be efficient on every network, so we must look for structural properties (sparsity, low treewidth) that make particular networks tractable. Second — a result many students find surprising — even *approximating* P(X | e) to within an arbitrary multiplicative tolerance is NP-hard in the general case, so sampling methods escape worst-case hardness only in the sense that they often behave well in practice, not in the sense that they provide a guaranteed polynomial-time solution.

Exact inference by enumeration. The enumeration algorithm takes the marginalisation equation literally, but substitutes the network factorisation for the joint before summing. For P(B | j, m):

P(B | j, m) = α Σ_e Σ_a P(B) P(e) P(a | B, e) P(j | a) P(m | a)

A naive implementation would, for each of the four (e, a) combinations, multiply five numbers and add the result — around 4 × 5 operations per value of B. But P(B) does not depend on e or a, and P(e) does not depend on a, so these factors can be moved outside the summations they are constant with respect to:

P(B | j, m) = α P(B) Σ_e P(e) Σ_a P(a | B, e) P(j | a) P(m | a)

This nested form corresponds exactly to a depth-first recursive evaluation tree: the outer loop branches on e, each branch contains an inner loop branching on a, and the leaves are products of CPT entries. Evaluating the B = true branch:

BranchInner sum over AlarmValue
e = true0.95 × 0.90 × 0.70 + 0.05 × 0.05 × 0.010.598525
e = false0.94 × 0.90 × 0.70 + 0.06 × 0.05 × 0.010.592230

Weighting these by P(e) = 0.002 and P(¬e) = 0.998 gives 0.59224259, and multiplying by P(b) = 0.001 gives P(b, j, m) = 0.00059224. The parallel computation for ¬b yields P(¬b, j, m) = 0.00149186. Normalising:

P(B | j, m) = α ⟨0.00059224, 0.00149186⟩ = ⟨0.284, 0.716⟩

So the posterior probability of burglary rises from a prior of 0.001 to about 28.4% — a factor of nearly 300 — but is still well below 50%, a nice illustration of base-rate effects that pure "the alarm went off, both neighbours called" reasoning misses.

Pseudocode for the recursive version:

function ENUMERATION-ASK(X, e, bn) returns a distribution over X
    Q ← an empty distribution over X
    for each value xᵢ of X do
        Q(xᵢ) ← ENUMERATE-ALL(VARS(bn), e extended with X = xᵢ)
    return NORMALIZE(Q)

function ENUMERATE-ALL(vars, e) returns a real number
    if EMPTY(vars) then return 1.0
    V ← FIRST(vars)                      // vars in topological order
    if V has value v in e then
        return P(v | parents(V)) × ENUMERATE-ALL(REST(vars), e)
    else
        return Σ_v P(v | parents(V)) × ENUMERATE-ALL(REST(vars), e with V = v)

Cost of enumeration. For a network of n Boolean variables the evaluation tree has O(2ⁿ) leaves, so time is exponential; but because the recursion explores one root-to-leaf path at a time and keeps only the current assignment on the stack, space is only O(n). The fatal inefficiency is repeated subcomputation. In the example, the inner products P(j | a) P(m | a) — namely 0.63 for a and 0.0005 for ¬a — are recomputed in every one of the four outer branches. In a larger network a subtree may be re-evaluated an exponential number of times. Enumeration therefore behaves like a naive recursive Fibonacci implementation, and the fix is analogous: compute each intermediate result once and store it. That is precisely what variable elimination does.

Variable elimination: factors. A factor is a table mapping each assignment of a set of variables (its scope) to a non-negative number. Each CPT is a factor; so are the intermediate results produced during elimination, which are generally not probability distributions and need not sum to one. The algorithm proceeds right-to-left through the expression, storing intermediate factors instead of recomputing them.

Step 1 is to build the initial factor list, one factor per node, with evidence variables instantiated. Instantiation means slicing the CPT down to the observed row, which removes the evidence variable from the factor's scope. For P(B | j, m) we obtain:

FactorScopeSource
f₁(B)BP(B)
f₂(E)EP(E)
f₃(A, B, E)A, B, EP(A | B, E)
f₄(A)AP(j | A), i.e. ⟨0.90, 0.05⟩
f₅(A)AP(m | A), i.e. ⟨0.70, 0.01⟩

Step 2 repeatedly eliminates hidden variables. To eliminate a variable V: gather every factor whose scope contains V, multiply them together pointwise (the product's scope is the union of their scopes), then sum the product over the values of V, and put the resulting factor back in the pool. Pointwise product means (f × g)(assignment) = f(restriction) × g(restriction); for example multiplying f₄ and f₅ gives f₄₅(a) = 0.63 and f₄₅(¬a) = 0.0005 — computed once, which is exactly the saving over enumeration.

function ELIMINATION-ASK(X, e, bn) returns a distribution over X
    factors ← [ ]
    for each variable V in ORDER(bn.VARS) do          // reverse topological or heuristic order
        factors ← [MAKE-FACTOR(V, e)] + factors

    return NORMALIZE(POINTWISE-PRODUCT(factors))

Pruning irrelevant variables. Before any arithmetic, we can delete nodes that cannot affect the answer. A variable that is not an ancestor of a query variable or of an evidence variable is irrelevant: summing its CPT over its own values yields 1, so it contributes nothing. In the query P(JohnCalls | b), the variable MaryCalls is a leaf and not an ancestor of anything we care about, so it can be dropped entirely. More generally, any variable d-separated from the query by the evidence is irrelevant, and the "every variable that is not an ancestor of query or evidence" rule is a cheap, easily computed sufficient condition. In large networks modelling an entire domain but queried about one small corner, pruning can remove the overwhelming majority of nodes before inference begins.

Elimination order and treewidth. The cost of variable elimination is dominated by the size of the largest intermediate factor created, which is exponential in the number of variables in that factor's scope. Different orderings produce dramatically different intermediate factors. Eliminating A before E above produced a factor over two variables; a different ordering in a larger network might produce a factor over ten, costing 2¹⁰ entries. Formally, an ordering defines an induced graph: starting from the moralised undirected graph (parents of each node joined by an edge, directions dropped), eliminate variables in order, and whenever a variable is eliminated, connect all of its remaining neighbours into a clique. The induced width of the ordering is the size of the largest resulting clique minus one, and the treewidth of the network is the minimum induced width over all orderings. Variable elimination runs in time and space O(n · d^(w+1)) where d is the largest domain size and w the induced width — polynomial in the number of nodes but exponential in treewidth. Finding the ordering that minimises induced width is itself NP-hard, so practical systems use greedy heuristics evaluated on the fly:

  • Min-degree (min-neighbours): next eliminate the variable with the fewest neighbours in the current induced graph. Cheap and often surprisingly good.
  • Min-fill: next eliminate the variable whose elimination adds the fewest new edges ("fill-in" edges) to the graph. Slightly more expensive to compute but usually produces better orderings.
  • Min-weight: like min-degree but weighting each neighbour by its domain size, which matters when variables have very different cardinalities.

A useful intuition: eliminating a variable "marries" all of its neighbours, so you want to remove loosely connected variables first and postpone hubs. In the burglary network, eliminating A early is acceptable because it has only three neighbours; in a network where one node is connected to twenty others, eliminating it first would create a factor over twenty variables and the computation would be hopeless.

Polytrees. A polytree (singly connected network) is one in which there is at most one undirected path between any two nodes. Polytrees have treewidth bounded by the maximum number of parents of any node, independent of network size, so variable elimination — eliminating leaves inward toward the query — runs in time and space linear in the number of nodes (for fixed maximum in-degree). Chains and trees are special cases. Networks with loops in the underlying undirected graph, such as the classic Cloudy → {Sprinkler, Rain} → WetGrass network, are multiply connected; their treewidth exceeds the maximum in-degree, and for grid-like structures treewidth grows with the square root of the number of nodes, which is where exact methods eventually break down. Clustering or junction-tree algorithms generalise this picture by merging nodes into clusters until the result is a polytree, then propagating messages between clusters; they share variable elimination's complexity profile but amortise the cost across many queries, computing all posterior marginals in roughly twice the work of computing one.

Approximate inference: prior sampling. When exact methods are infeasible, we trade exactness for tractability by generating random samples from the network and estimating probabilities as frequencies. The basic generator is prior sampling: visit the variables in topological order (parents always before children) and sample each from its CPT row selected by the values already assigned to its parents. For the burglary network:

sample Burglary from ⟨0.001, 0.999⟩           → ¬b
sample Earthquake from ⟨0.002, 0.998⟩         → ¬e
sample Alarm from P(A | ¬b, ¬e) = ⟨0.001, …⟩  → ¬a
sample JohnCalls from P(J | ¬a) = ⟨0.05, …⟩   → ¬j
sample MaryCalls from P(M | ¬a) = ⟨0.01, …⟩   → ¬m
event: (¬b, ¬e, ¬a, ¬j, ¬m)

Because each variable is drawn from its conditional distribution given its already-sampled parents, the probability that this procedure generates a particular complete event is exactly the product of CPT entries for that event — that is, exactly its joint probability. Consequently the fraction of N samples matching any pattern converges to the true probability of that pattern as N → ∞: the estimator is consistent, and with standard error shrinking as 1/√N. Note that convergence rate depends on N, not on the size of the network, which is what makes sampling attractive for very large models.

Rejection sampling. Prior sampling answers unconditional queries. To handle evidence, rejection sampling generates full prior samples and simply throws away every sample that disagrees with the evidence; the posterior is estimated from the surviving samples:

P̂(X = x | e) = count(X = x and e among samples) / count(e among samples)

The method is trivially easy to implement and gives consistent, asymptotically unbiased estimates. Its weakness is severe waste. Suppose we want P(Burglary | j, m). The evidence j ∧ m has prior probability roughly 0.0021, so about 998 of every 1000 samples are discarded; to accumulate even 100 usable samples we must generate around 48,000. The problem compounds multiplicatively: with k binary evidence variables each of prior probability around 0.1, the acceptance rate is about 10⁻ᵏ, falling exponentially in the number of evidence variables. Real applications — a medical network conditioned on twenty observed symptoms and test results, where the particular combination may have prior probability 10⁻¹⁵ — make rejection sampling completely impractical. The deeper diagnosis is that rejection sampling ignores the evidence while generating, and only consults it afterwards.

Likelihood weighting. Likelihood weighting repairs this by never generating an inconsistent sample. It is a form of importance sampling. Evidence variables are not sampled at all: they are clamped to their observed values. Non-evidence variables are sampled in topological order from their CPTs given their parents, exactly as in prior sampling. Because we forced the evidence rather than drawing it, each sample is generated from the wrong distribution, and we compensate by attaching a weight: start at w = 1 and, every time we pass an evidence variable Eᵢ, multiply w by P(eᵢ | parents(Eᵢ)) using the parent values in the current sample.

function WEIGHTED-SAMPLE(bn, e) returns an event and a weight
    w ← 1; x ← e
    for each variable Vᵢ in topological order do
        if Vᵢ is an evidence variable with value vᵢ in e then
            w ← w × P(vᵢ | parents(Vᵢ))
        else
            x[Vᵢ] ← a random sample from P(Vᵢ | parents(Vᵢ))
    return x, w

Running this on P(Rain | Sprinkler = true, WetGrass = true) in the sprinkler network: start w = 1; sample Cloudy from ⟨0.5, 0.5⟩, say cloudy; Sprinkler is evidence, so w ← 1 × P(sprinkler | cloudy) = 0.1; sample Rain from P(R | cloudy) = ⟨0.8, 0.2⟩, say rain; WetGrass is evidence, so w ← 0.1 × P(wetGrass | sprinkler, rain) = 0.1 × 0.99 = 0.099. The sample (cloudy, sprinkler, rain, wetGrass) is recorded with weight 0.099. The estimate is the weighted fraction:

P̂(X = x | e) = Σ{weights of samples with X = x} / Σ{weights of all samples}

Every sample now counts for something, so no work is discarded, and for the burglary query above likelihood weighting is roughly 500 times more sample-efficient than rejection sampling. However, it is not a complete cure. The sampled non-evidence variables are drawn purely from their priors, with no knowledge of evidence further down the network — information flows only forward, never backward. If the evidence sits near the bottom of the topological order, most samples will be generated in regions of the state space that the evidence makes implausible, and they will receive tiny weights. The effective sample size — roughly (Σw)² / Σw² — then collapses: a handful of samples with comparatively large weights dominate the estimate, and the variance becomes large even though the point estimate remains consistent. Likelihood weighting is therefore at its best when evidence is sparse and appears near the roots of the network, and degrades as evidence becomes more extensive or more "downstream".

MCMC and Gibbs sampling. Markov chain Monte Carlo takes a fundamentally different approach: instead of generating each sample independently from scratch, it generates a sequence of states in which each state is a small random perturbation of the previous one. The sequence is a Markov chain engineered so that its stationary distribution is exactly the posterior P(X | e); once the chain has settled, the visited states are (dependent) samples from the posterior and can be counted as frequencies.

Gibbs sampling is the most common MCMC variant for Bayesian networks. Initialise by fixing all evidence variables to their observed values and assigning the non-evidence variables arbitrarily (randomly, or by one likelihood-weighted sample). Then repeat: pick one non-evidence variable — cycling through them in order, or at random — and resample it from its distribution conditioned on the current values of all other variables, leaving everything else unchanged. Each such move is one step of the chain, and each resulting state is recorded as a sample.

The key practical fact is that conditioning on all other variables reduces to conditioning on the variable's Markov blanket: its parents, its children, and its children's other parents. A node is conditionally independent of the rest of the network given its blanket, so the resampling distribution is computed locally:

P(xᵢ | mb(Xᵢ)) = α P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ)) × Π_{Yⱼ ∈ children(Xᵢ)} P(yⱼ | parents(Yⱼ))

For Cloudy in the sprinkler network with Sprinkler and WetGrass observed and Rain = rain currently assigned, the blanket is {Sprinkler, Rain} and we resample from α ⟨P(c)P(s|c)P(r|c), P(¬c)P(s|¬c)P(r|¬c)⟩ = α ⟨0.5×0.1×0.8, 0.5×0.5×0.2⟩ = ⟨0.444, 0.556⟩. The computation touches only a handful of CPT entries regardless of how large the network is, which is why Gibbs sampling scales to models with thousands of nodes.

Because the chain starts in an arbitrary state, its first states reflect that arbitrary choice rather than the posterior. The initial stretch — the burn-in period — is therefore discarded, and only subsequent states are counted. Choosing the burn-in length (hundreds? millions of steps?) is a matter of judgement supported by diagnostics such as running several chains from dispersed starting points and checking that between-chain and within-chain variance agree (the Gelman–Rubin statistic), or inspecting trace plots and autocorrelation. Successive samples are correlated, so N Gibbs samples carry less information than N independent ones; some practitioners "thin" the chain by keeping every k-th state, though simply running longer is usually more efficient.

Gibbs sampling's great advantage is that it accommodates evidence anywhere in the network: clamping a leaf node is no harder than clamping a root, because conditioning is built into every local update rather than applied once at generation time. Its great weakness is slow and hard-to-diagnose convergence. If the posterior has two well-separated high-probability regions, a single-variable-at-a-time sampler may need an astronomically long time to move between them, because the intermediate states are improbable. The extreme case is near-deterministic CPTs: if a CPT entry is exactly 0, certain transitions have probability zero and the chain may be unable to reach parts of the state space at all — the chain is no longer irreducible and Gibbs sampling can converge to the wrong answer. Entries very close to 0 or 1 produce the same pathology in practice, with the chain becoming stuck in one basin for a very long time. Remedies include block sampling (resampling several correlated variables jointly), Metropolis–Hastings proposals that make larger moves, or reformulating the model to remove hard constraints.

Choosing an inference method. Translating the table into practice:

  • Enumeration is appropriate for very small networks — a handful of variables — and as a teaching device or a correctness oracle against which to test faster implementations. It should not be the production choice for anything of realistic size.
  • Variable elimination is the default exact method whenever the treewidth is small: polytrees, chains, trees, sparse networks, and networks that prune down to a small relevant subnetwork for the query at hand. If many different queries will be asked of the same evidence, a junction-tree algorithm amortises the work better. A sensible engineering practice is to compute a heuristic elimination order first, estimate the largest factor size, and only fall back on approximation if that estimate exceeds the memory budget.
  • Likelihood weighting is the method of choice when the network is too large for exact inference but the evidence is limited and sits near the top of the topological ordering — for instance, when a few root-level context variables are observed and we want a distribution over downstream outcomes. It is simple, embarrassingly parallel (samples are independent), and needs no convergence diagnostics.
  • Gibbs sampling or other MCMC is the right tool for large, densely connected networks with extensive evidence, unlikely evidence, or evidence embedded deep in the network — precisely the regimes where rejection sampling accepts nothing and likelihood weighting's weights degenerate. The price is the need to monitor convergence and the risk of poor mixing with near-deterministic structure.
  • Rejection sampling is rarely a practical choice in its own right; its value is conceptual, as the simplest correct way to see how conditioning can be implemented by sampling, and as the baseline against which likelihood weighting's improvement is understood.

Finally, keep the theoretical ceiling in view. Exact inference is NP-hard, and approximating a posterior to within an arbitrary tolerance is also NP-hard in general. There is therefore no universally efficient method, and the practical art lies in matching the algorithm to the structure of the particular network and the particular query: exploiting low treewidth where it exists, exploiting conditional independence to prune irrelevant variables, and choosing a sampler whose generating process is as close as possible to the posterior one actually wants to explore.

A Bayesian network compactly encodes a joint probability distribution over many variables, but building the network is only half the job. The other half is inference: given evidence about some variables, compute the posterior distribution over the variables you actually care about. This topic covers how that computation is done, from exact methods that sum over the joint distribution to approximate methods that estimate probabilities by drawing samples.

Temporal Models and Hidden Markov Models

▶

Most probabilistic models studied so far describe a static world: a fixed set of random variables, one body of evidence, and a single posterior to compute. Many real problems are not like this. A robot moves through a building, a patient's condition evolves in intensive care, a speech signal unfolds millisecond by millisecond, and a market price drifts from day to day. In all of these cases the quantity we care about changes, the evidence arrives continuously, and any sensible answer must be revised as new data appear. Temporal probabilistic models — of which the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is the canonical discrete case — extend the machinery of joint distributions, conditional independence and Bayes' rule across time, so that belief about a hidden world state can be maintained, projected forward, revised in hindsight, and used to recover the single most plausible history of events.

Reasoning about processes that change over time. The standard device is to chop the continuous flow of time into a sequence of time slices. Each slice t contains two kinds of variables:

  • State variables X_t — the aspects of the world we care about but cannot see directly: the true position of a robot, the phoneme currently being uttered, whether it is actually raining, the true blood-glucose level of a patient.
  • Evidence variables E_t — the things we actually observe at that slice: laser range readings, an acoustic feature vector, whether the director arrives carrying an umbrella, a noisy sensor reading.

Time is normally discretised into fixed intervals — every 10 ms for speech frames, every 100 ms for a control loop, every day for a weather model. The choice of interval is a modelling decision: too coarse and genuine dynamics are lost between slices; too fine and the computational cost of running the recursion rises with no gain in information. Crucially, because the slices are uniform, the resulting Bayesian network is not a one-off hand-built structure but a repeated template that is "unrolled" for as many steps as required. The same parent–child pattern (X_{t-1} → X_t, X_t → E_t) appears in every slice.

This repetition has a direct practical payoff: the same conditional probability tables are reused at every time step. We do not need a separate table for day 1, day 2 and day 3 — we need one transition table and one sensor table, which are applied over and over. This is what makes it possible to reason about sequences of arbitrary, even unbounded, length with a finite and small set of parameters. It also changes the character of the inference question. Instead of "given this fixed evidence set, what is the posterior?", we ask "given the stream of observations seen so far, what is my current belief, and how do I update it in constant time when the next observation arrives?"

The Markov assumption. If we allowed X_t to depend on every earlier state, the conditional probability table for X_t would grow without bound as t increases — an unusable model. The first-order Markov assumption cuts this dependency to the minimum:

P(X_t | X_0:t-1) = P(X_t | X_{t-1})

In words: the present state screens off the past from the future. Once you know the state at time t-1, nothing about times 0 … t-2 adds any information about X_t. Graphically, the unrolled network is a simple chain, and the chain structure immediately gives conditional independences: X_{t+5} is independent of X_{t-3} given X_t, because every path between them passes through the observed-or-conditioned intervening state in a chain configuration. Distant time slices are decoupled by the slices between them.

A second-order (or higher-order) Markov model relaxes this by conditioning on more history, e.g. P(X_t | X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}). This genuinely increases expressive power — a second-order model of position can encode momentum, which a first-order model of position alone cannot — but the transition table grows from N × N to N × N × N entries, and parameter estimation becomes correspondingly data-hungry.

There is a better remedy when the first-order assumption appears too restrictive: enrich the state variable instead of increasing the order. The trick is to fold the needed history into the present state so that the process becomes Markov again. Examples:

  • Tracking an aircraft with position alone violates the first-order assumption, because tomorrow's position depends on where it was yesterday as well as today. Define the state as (position, velocity) and the process becomes first-order: the new position and velocity are determined (noisily) by the current pair.
  • In the rain-and-umbrella world, rain today depends not only on rain yesterday but on the season. Adding Season_t (and perhaps Temperature_t, Pressure_t) to the state restores the Markov property at a higher level of description.
  • In part-of-speech tagging, if a bigram tag model is too weak, one can define the state as the pair of the last two tags — which is mathematically identical to a second-order model but lets us keep using first-order algorithms unchanged.

This is a general and important insight: "higher order" and "bigger state" are two views of the same fix, and the bigger-state view lets all the standard first-order algorithms be reused verbatim.

Transition and sensor models. A temporal model is fully specified by three ingredients:

  • A prior P(X_0) over the initial state.
  • A transition model P(X_t | X_{t-1}), describing how the hidden state evolves — the "physics" or dynamics of the domain. In robot localisation this is the motion/odometry model; in weather it is the day-to-day persistence of rain; in speech it is the probability of staying in a phoneme versus advancing to the next.
  • A sensor model (also called the observation or emission model) P(E_t | X_t), which embodies the sensor Markov assumption: the evidence at time t depends only on the current state, not on earlier states or earlier evidence. If a sensor's reading really does depend on the previous reading (for example, a drifting or sticky sensor), the honest fix is again to enrich the state — e.g. add a SensorBias_t or SensorBroken_t variable — rather than to quietly violate the assumption.

Stationarity is the further assumption that these two models do not change with t: the probability of rain following rain is the same in slice 5 as in slice 500. Stationarity is what allows one fixed set of parameters to cover a sequence of arbitrary length, and it is distinct from "the process is static" — the world changes, but the laws governing the change do not.

Putting these together, the joint distribution over all states and all evidence up to time T factorises as

P(X_0, X_1:T, E_1:T) = P(X_0) · ∏_{t=1..T} P(X_t | X_{t-1}) · P(E_t | X_t)

Every term on the right is one of the two reusable tables (or the prior). This factorisation is the whole content of the model, and every inference algorithm below is simply a clever way of summing or maximising over this product without enumerating it explicitly.

A concrete running example. The classic illustration is the "umbrella world": a security guard works underground and cannot see the weather. The hidden state R_t (Rain) is Boolean; the evidence U_t is whether the director arrives carrying an umbrella. The model is:

Model componentConditionProbability of TrueProbability of False

Structure of a Hidden Markov Model. An HMM is the special case in which the state is described by a single discrete variable with N possible values. This restriction buys a very convenient matrix formulation:

  • The transition model becomes an N × N matrix T with T_ij = P(X_t = j | X_{t-1} = i). Each row sums to 1 (it is a conditional distribution over the next state). For the umbrella world, with state order (rain, no-rain), T = [[0.7, 0.3], [0.3, 0.7]].
  • The sensor model, for a particular observed value e_t, becomes an N × N diagonal observation matrix O_t whose i-th diagonal entry is P(e_t | X_t = i). For U_1 = true, O_1 = [[0.9, 0], [0, 0.2]]; for U_3 = false, O_3 = [[0.1, 0], [0, 0.8]]. The diagonal form is what turns "multiply each state's belief by its likelihood" into a single matrix product.

The chain is called hidden precisely because the X variables are never observed: we see only the evidence they generate, and must invert the sensor model (via Bayes' rule) to reason backwards from effect to cause. A plain Markov chain, by contrast, has observable states.

The single-variable restriction is less limiting than it looks, because multiple state variables can be merged into one "mega-variable" by taking their Cartesian product. A domain with variables Position (100 values), Velocity (20 values) and Battery (5 values) can be expressed as an HMM with N = 100 × 20 × 5 = 10,000 states. The catch is that this growth is exponential in the number of variables: the transition matrix has N² = 10⁸ entries, which is already uncomfortable and quickly becomes impossible. Dynamic Bayesian networks (DBNs) avoid the blow-up by keeping the state factored and exploiting conditional independence within a slice; an HMM is the degenerate DBN with one state variable, and every DBN can in principle be flattened into an HMM.

Core inference tasks on temporal models. Four queries dominate, and each is a different way of slicing the same joint distribution.

1. Filtering (state estimation): compute P(X_t | e_1:t) — the belief about the current state given all evidence to date. This is the workhorse query for any agent acting in real time. It is computed by a two-phase recursion:

Predict:  P(X_t | e_1:t-1) = Σ_{x_{t-1}} P(X_t | x_{t-1}) · P(x_{t-1} | e_1:t-1)
Update:   P(X_t | e_1:t)   = α · P(e_t | X_t) · P(X_t | e_1:t-1)

Matrix form:  f_{1:t} = α · O_t · Tᵀ · f_{1:t-1}

The prediction step is marginalisation over the previous state (rolling the belief forward through the dynamics, which always spreads out the distribution); the update step is Bayes' rule applied with the sensor likelihood (which sharpens it), followed by normalisation with α. Working the umbrella example with U_1 = true, U_2 = true:

Notice that the belief vector f_{1:t} is a sufficient statistic for the entire observation history: once we have it, the earlier evidence can be discarded. This is why filtering runs online in constant time and constant memory per step — O(N²) time per step for a dense transition matrix, independent of how long the sequence has been running.

2. Prediction: compute P(X_{t+k} | e_1:t) for k > 0 — the state at some future time, with no evidence beyond t. Algorithmically this is just filtering with the update step omitted: repeatedly apply the transition model to the current belief. In the umbrella world, starting from ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩ after day 2 and predicting forward with no further sightings gives ⟨0.653, 0.347⟩, then ⟨0.561, 0.439⟩, then ⟨0.525, 0.475⟩ … converging to the stationary distribution ⟨0.5, 0.5⟩. This decay is universal for ergodic chains: prediction without evidence loses information, and beyond some mixing time the forecast is worthless. The faster the chain mixes (the closer the transition probabilities are to uniform), the shorter the useful prediction horizon.

3. Smoothing: compute P(X_k | e_1:t) for 0 ≤ k < t — a belief about the past informed by evidence that arrived afterwards. Hindsight is genuinely more accurate than real-time estimation, which is why smoothing is used for offline analysis, trajectory reconstruction, and as the E-step when learning HMM parameters. The estimate factorises into a forward message and a backward message:

P(X_k | e_1:t) = α · f_{1:k} × b_{k+1:t}
b_{k+1:t} = P(e_{k+1:t} | X_k) = Σ_{x_{k+1}} P(e_{k+1}|x_{k+1}) · P(e_{k+2:t}|x_{k+1}) · P(x_{k+1}|X_k)

where × is element-wise multiplication and the backward message is initialised to the all-ones vector. For the umbrella world, smoothing P(R_1 | u_1, u_2): the backward message is b_{2:2} = ⟨0.9(0.7)+0.2(0.3), 0.9(0.3)+0.2(0.7)⟩ = ⟨0.69, 0.41⟩, so the smoothed estimate is α⟨0.818 × 0.69, 0.182 × 0.41⟩ = α⟨0.564, 0.075⟩ = ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩ — higher than the filtered value of 0.818, because the second umbrella retrospectively confirms that day 1 was probably wet too. Running the forward pass once, caching all f vectors, then sweeping backwards gives the whole smoothed sequence in O(N²t) time and O(Nt) space: the forward–backward algorithm.

4. Most likely explanation (MLE / decoding): compute argmax_{x_1:t} P(x_1:t | e_1:t) — the single best whole sequence of states. This is not the same as stringing together the individually most likely states at each step, because the pointwise-best states may form a transition-improbable or even impossible path. The Viterbi algorithm solves it with a recursion identical in shape to filtering, except that the summation is replaced by maximisation:

m_{1:t+1} = P(e_{t+1} | X_{t+1}) · max_{x_t} ( P(X_{t+1} | x_t) · m_{1:t} )

Here m_{1:t} holds, for each state value, the probability of the best path that ends in that state having generated the evidence so far. Alongside each entry we store a back-pointer to the predecessor state that achieved the maximum; once the end of the sequence is reached we take the largest final entry and follow the pointers backwards to read off the optimal path. The cost is the same O(N²t) time as forward–backward, with O(Nt) space for the back-pointers. In the umbrella world with the evidence sequence [true, true, false, true, true], Viterbi recovers the path [rain, rain, no-rain, rain, rain] — the single dry day is "explained away" by the absent umbrella even though the persistence of the transition model resists switching states twice in a row. In practice the products underflow quickly, so implementations work with log-probabilities, turning the products into sums:

log m_{1:t+1}(j) = log P(e_{t+1} | j) + max_i ( log T_ij + log m_{1:t}(i) )

The unifying property is recursive computation. Every one of these algorithms maintains a fixed-size message (a vector of N numbers) and updates it by a constant amount of work per time slice. Nothing re-reads the whole history. This is exactly the behaviour an embedded agent needs: a robot localising at 20 Hz performs the same small matrix operation twenty times a second forever, with memory that never grows. By contrast, naively applying variable elimination to the unrolled network from scratch at each new observation would cost O(t) work per step and O(t) memory — fine for a short recording, hopeless for a continuously running system. One practical caveat: because the forward message is a product of many small likelihoods, it must be renormalised at each step (which filtering does anyway, via α) or held in log-space, otherwise it underflows to zero within a few hundred steps.

Applications to sequential data. The HMM's combination of a simple hidden chain, a noisy sensor channel and linear-time exact inference has made it one of the most widely deployed probabilistic models.

  • Speech recognition. The acoustic waveform is cut into ~10 ms frames and converted into feature vectors (classically MFCCs). The hidden states are sub-phone units — typically three states per phoneme, modelling its beginning, middle and end — chained together according to a pronunciation dictionary and a language model. The transition model encodes how long a phone is held (self-loops) and which phone may follow; the sensor model gives P(acoustic features | sub-phone state), historically as a Gaussian mixture and in modern hybrid systems as the output of a neural network. Decoding a spoken utterance into words is literally a Viterbi search over this enormous composite HMM, usually with beam pruning because the state space runs to millions.
  • Robot localisation. The hidden state is the robot's pose, typically a discretised grid of (x, y, θ) cells over a known map. The transition model is the motion/odometry model: given the commanded or measured wheel movement, where might the robot now be, allowing for slip and drift. The sensor model scores a ring of laser or sonar range readings against the distances that would be expected from each candidate cell on the map. Running the filtering recursion is exactly "Markov localisation": the belief typically starts uniform (the kidnapped-robot problem), stays multi-modal while the robot is in a symmetric corridor, and collapses to a single sharp peak as soon as a distinctive feature such as a doorway or corner is sensed. Smoothing is then used offline to produce a cleaner reconstruction of the path actually travelled, and the same ideas scale up to particle filters when the state is continuous.
  • Object tracking. A pedestrian, vehicle or radar contact is followed through time with state (position, velocity) — the enriched state that restores the Markov property — and evidence consisting of noisy, sometimes missing detections from a camera or radar. The transition model is a constant-velocity dynamics model plus process noise; the sensor model accounts for measurement error. Because the transition model carries belief forward even in the absence of evidence, the tracker can coast through occlusions: if the target disappears behind a pillar for a few frames, the predicted distribution spreads out but remains centred on where the target should be, and re-locks when a detection reappears. With Gaussian states and linear dynamics this is the Kalman filter — the continuous analogue of the same forward recursion.
  • Bioinformatics. In gene finding, the observed sequence is the string of nucleotides and the hidden states label each position as exon, intron, splice site, promoter or intergenic region; the transition model encodes the grammar of gene structure and the sensor model the base-composition statistics of each region, with Viterbi producing the predicted gene annotation. Profile HMMs do the analogous job for sequence alignment, with match, insert and delete states capturing the ways a family member can diverge from a consensus protein or DNA motif.
  • Natural language processing. In part-of-speech tagging the hidden states are tags (noun, verb, determiner, …) and the observations are words; the transition model is the tag-bigram probability P(tag_t | tag_{t-1}) and the sensor model the emission probability P(word | tag). Viterbi then resolves ambiguity contextually — in "the plans book quickly" versus "the book plans quickly", the identical word forms receive different tags because only one tag sequence is jointly probable. Named-entity recognition, chunking, and word segmentation for languages without spaces are all handled by the same template.
  • Other domains. Gesture and activity recognition from accelerometer streams, handwriting recognition, fault and anomaly detection in machinery, financial regime-switching models, and sleep-stage classification from EEG all reuse the identical structure; only the meaning of the states and the form of the emission distribution change.

Across all of these, the modelling discipline is the same: decide what the hidden state must contain for the first-order and sensor Markov assumptions to be defensible, specify a transition model for how that state evolves, specify a sensor model for how it reveals itself, assume stationarity so the parameters can be shared, and then choose the inference task — filtering for acting now, prediction for looking ahead, smoothing for analysing the past, Viterbi for explaining the whole sequence.

HMM Inference Tasks and Algorithms

▶

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is the simplest useful model of a world that changes over time and that we can only observe indirectly. The world is summarised at each moment by a single discrete random variable — the state — whose value we never see. What we do see is a stream of noisy evidence variables, one per time step, each generated by the hidden state at that moment. Almost every practical question we can ask about such a system (Where am I now? Where will I be in ten minutes? Where was I three minutes ago, now that I know what happened since? What is the single most plausible history that explains everything I saw?) turns out to be answerable by a small family of closely related dynamic-programming recursions: the forward recursion, the backward recursion, and the Viterbi recursion. This topic develops those recursions from first principles, works them numerically on a canonical example, and explains how to choose among them.

The HMM framework and inference setup

Let Xt denote the state variable at time step t and Et the evidence variable observed at that step. A discrete HMM over a state space of size S is completely specified by three ingredients:

  • A prior P(X0) — a distribution over the S possible initial states, before any observation has arrived. It encodes everything we believe at time zero.
  • A transition model P(Xt | Xt-1) — an S × S matrix T whose entry Tij = P(Xt = j | Xt-1 = i) gives the probability of moving from state i to state j. Each row sums to 1.
  • A sensor (emission/observation) model P(Et | Xt) — the probability of each possible observation given each possible state.

Both the transition and sensor models are assumed stationary: the same matrix and the same emission probabilities are reused at every time step. This is what makes it possible to describe an infinitely long process with a finite number of parameters, and it is why we can write a single recursion and apply it over and over.

Two independence assumptions give the model its tractability.

  • The (first-order) Markov assumption: P(Xt | X0:t-1, E1:t-1) = P(Xt | Xt-1). The next state depends on the whole past only through the immediately preceding state. The state variable is required to be a sufficient statistic for the future: if it is not, the fix is to enlarge the state (e.g. include velocity as well as position, or use a second-order model whose state is the pair (Xt-1, Xt)).
  • The sensor Markov assumption: P(Et | X0:t, E1:t-1) = P(Et | Xt). The reading at time t is produced by the state at time t and nothing else; past readings add no further information once the current state is known.

Together these give the full joint distribution over any finite horizon as a product of local factors:

P(X0:t, E1:t) = P(X0) · ∏ from i=1 to t of [ P(Xi | Xi-1) · P(Ei | Xi) ]

Graphically this is a Bayesian network shaped like a chain: X0 → X1 → X2 → ..., with a dangling child Ei hanging off each Xi. The prior plus the two models are sufficient to answer every query, because every query is just a marginal or conditional of this joint, and the joint is fully determined by them.

Notation: e1:t abbreviates the observed sequence e1, e2, …, et. The four standard inference tasks are then

  • Filtering: compute P(Xt | e1:t) — the belief about the present.
  • Prediction: compute P(Xt+k | e1:t) for k > 0 — belief about the future.
  • Smoothing: compute P(Xk | e1:t) for 0 ≤ k < t — a revised belief about the past.
  • Most likely explanation (MLE / decoding): compute argmaxx1:t P(x1:t | e1:t) — the single best whole trajectory.

The running example: the umbrella world

A guard works in an underground facility and cannot see the weather. Each day the guard observes only whether the director arrives carrying an umbrella. The hidden state is Rt ("it rains on day t"), the evidence is Ut ("the director carries an umbrella on day t"). The model is:

Filtering: maintaining the current belief state

The key insight is that filtering can be done recursively: there exists a function f such that

P(X(t+1) | e(1:t+1)) = f( e(t+1), P(Xt | e(1:t)) )

i.e. the new belief depends on the old belief and the newest observation only. The old evidence need never be stored or revisited. The derivation uses nothing but the three operations of probabilistic inference — conditioning, marginalisation, and Bayes' rule — plus the two Markov assumptions:

P(X(t+1) | e(1:t+1))
  = P(X(t+1) | e(1:t), e(t+1))
  = α · P(e(t+1) | X(t+1), e(1:t)) · P(X(t+1) | e(1:t))      [Bayes' rule, conditioned on e(1:t)]
  = α · P(e(t+1) | X(t+1)) · P(X(t+1) | e(1:t))               [sensor Markov assumption]
  = α · P(e(t+1) | X(t+1)) · Σ over xt of
            P(X(t+1) | xt) · P(xt | e(1:t))                   [marginalise over Xt; Markov assumption]

Here α is a normalising constant. The formula has two clearly separated phases:

  • Predict (time update): push the current belief one step forward through the transition model, P(Xt+1 | e1:t) = Σxt P(Xt+1 | xt) P(xt | e1:t). This phase always increases (or at best preserves) uncertainty, because it mixes the belief through a stochastic matrix.
  • Update (measurement update): multiply the predicted distribution pointwise by the likelihood of the new observation, P(et+1 | Xt+1). This phase generally decreases uncertainty, because it down-weights states that would have been unlikely to produce what we saw.
  • Normalise: the pointwise product is not a distribution; divide by its sum so the entries total 1. The sum that you divide by is itself meaningful — it equals P(et+1 | e1:t), the one-step predictive likelihood of the observation, and the product of these constants over the whole sequence is the data likelihood P(e1:t).

Writing the belief as a column vector f1:t and letting Ot be the diagonal matrix whose i-th diagonal entry is P(et | Xt = i), the whole step compresses to the matrix form

f(1:t+1) = α · O(t+1) · Tᵀ · f(1:t)      with f(1:0) = P(X0)

Let us run it on the umbrella world, observing an umbrella on day 1 (u1) and again on day 2 (u2).

Day 1. Predict: P(R1) = 0.7×0.5 + 0.3×0.5 = 0.5 for rain, and likewise 0.5 for no rain — the symmetric prior is a fixed point of this transition matrix, so prediction changes nothing. Update with u1: multiply by the likelihoods ⟨0.9, 0.2⟩ to get ⟨0.9×0.5, 0.2×0.5⟩ = ⟨0.45, 0.10⟩. Normalise by 0.55: P(R1 | u1) = ⟨0.818, 0.182⟩. One umbrella sighting has lifted belief in rain from 0.5 to roughly 0.82.

Day 2. Predict: 0.7×0.818 + 0.3×0.182 = 0.627; the predicted vector is ⟨0.627, 0.373⟩ — note that it has drifted back toward 0.5, the inevitable cost of a time step. Update with u2: ⟨0.9×0.627, 0.2×0.373⟩ = ⟨0.565, 0.075⟩, normalise by 0.639 to obtain P(R2 | u1, u2) = ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩.

FORWARD-STEP(f, e):              # f is a length-S vector, e the new observation
    for each state j:
        pred[j] = Σ over i of  T[i][j] * f[i]        # predict

    return NORMALISE(new)

Cost. Each step is one matrix–vector product plus a pointwise multiplication: O(S²) arithmetic operations, or O(S) if the transition structure is sparse. Crucially, the cost and the memory are constant per step and independent of t: we keep one vector of S numbers and overwrite it. This is what makes filtering suitable for an agent that must run forever — a robot localising itself, a speech recogniser consuming audio frames, a credit-card system scoring transactions as they arrive. A practical caveat: after many steps the unnormalised numbers underflow, so implementations either renormalise every step (as above, which is standard) or work with log-probabilities.

Prediction: projecting belief into the future

Prediction is filtering with the update phase deleted. Having filtered up to time t, we simply apply the transition model repeatedly:

P(X(t+k+1) | e(1:t)) = Σ over x(t+k) of P(X(t+k+1) | x(t+k)) · P(x(t+k) | e(1:t))

In matrix form, P(Xt+k | e1:t) = (Tᵀ)k f1:t. No normalisation is needed, because a stochastic matrix maps distributions to distributions exactly.

Because no evidence is injected, each extra step forward can only add uncertainty. Continuing the umbrella example from ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩ at day 2 and predicting forward with no further umbrella sightings:

The sequence converges to ⟨0.5, 0.5⟩. That limit is the stationary distribution of the Markov chain: the unique vector π satisfying π = Tᵀπ (here, by symmetry, the uniform distribution). For any ergodic chain — one that is irreducible and aperiodic, which holds whenever every transition probability is strictly positive — the powers (Tᵀ)k converge to a matrix all of whose columns equal π, so the prediction forgets its starting point entirely. Once that has happened, prediction is uninformative: the answer is the same whether we saw ten umbrellas or none, and the evidence has no lasting influence on the far future.

How fast this happens is governed by the mixing time of the chain, which is controlled by the magnitude of the second-largest eigenvalue modulus |λ₂| of T; the deviation from stationarity shrinks roughly like |λ₂|k. For the umbrella transition matrix the eigenvalues are 1 and 0.4, so each day's prediction moves 60 % of the remaining distance to 0.5 — which is exactly the pattern of the table (0.383 → 0.153 → 0.061 → 0.025 excess above 0.5). A "sticky" world with transition probabilities of 0.99 would have λ₂ = 0.98 and a mixing time of hundreds of steps, so useful forecasts would extend much further. The practical rule: the predictive horizon of an HMM is a property of the transition model, not of how much evidence you have gathered. Gathering more data sharpens f1:t but does not extend the horizon.

Smoothing and the forward–backward algorithm

Smoothing asks for P(Xk | e1:t) with k < t: what should we now believe about a past time slice, in the light of everything that happened afterwards? Hindsight genuinely helps — if it rained for the next five days, that raises our belief that it was already raining on day 1, because the transition model makes rain persistent.

Split the evidence at k and apply Bayes' rule plus conditional independence (given Xk, the past evidence and the future evidence are independent — this is exactly d-separation in the chain):

P(Xk | e(1:t)) = α · P(Xk | e(1:k)) · P(e(k+1:t) | Xk)
               = α · f(1:k) × b(k+1:t)

where × denotes pointwise (component-by-component) multiplication. The first factor f1:k is exactly the filtered belief already computed by the forward recursion — nothing new is needed. The second factor bk+1:t is a likelihood, not a distribution (its entries need not sum to 1), and it obeys its own recursion running backwards:

P(e(k+1:t) | Xk) = Σ over x(k+1) of
       P(e(k+1) | x(k+1)) · P(e(k+2:t) | x(k+1)) · P(x(k+1) | Xk)

or in matrix form bk+1:t = T · Ok+1 · bk+2:t, with the base case bt+1:t = ⟨1, 1, …, 1⟩ (there is no evidence after the end, so the likelihood of "nothing" is 1 for every state).

Worked smoothing in the umbrella world. We saw umbrellas on days 1 and 2 and want P(R1 | u1, u2). The forward message is f1:1 = ⟨0.818, 0.182⟩ from before. The backward message for day 2 onward is

b(2:2)[rain]    = Σ_{r2} P(u2 | r2) P(∅ | r2) P(r2 | R1 = rain)
                = 0.9·1·0.7 + 0.2·1·0.3 = 0.63 + 0.06 = 0.69
b(2:2)[no rain] = 0.9·1·0.3 + 0.2·1·0.7 = 0.27 + 0.14 = 0.41

Multiply pointwise and normalise: α⟨0.818 × 0.69, 0.182 × 0.41⟩ = α⟨0.5646, 0.0746⟩ = ⟨0.883, 0.117⟩. Belief that day 1 was rainy rises from the filtered 0.818 to the smoothed 0.883, because the day-2 umbrella is retrospective evidence for day-1 rain. (The numerical coincidence with P(R2|u1,u2) is an artefact of this symmetric model, not a general rule.)

The naïve way to smooth a whole sequence would be to redo this separately for each k, costing O(t²). The forward–backward algorithm avoids that: run the forward recursion once from 1 to t, caching every message f1:1, …, f1:t; then run the backward recursion once from t down to 1, and at each k combine the stored forward message with the current backward message. Total cost O(t·S²) time — linear in sequence length — with O(t·S) space for the cache.

FORWARD-BACKWARD(e(1:t), prior):
    f[0] ← prior
    for i = 1 to t:  f[i] ← NORMALISE( O[i] · Tᵀ · f[i-1] )    # store every f
    b ← ⟨1,...,1⟩
    for i = t down to 1:
        sv[i] ← NORMALISE( f[i] × b )                           # smoothed estimate
        b ← T · O[i] · b                                        # roll b back one step
    return sv[1..t]

The O(t·S) space requirement is the real limitation: it rules out running the algorithm forever on a streaming signal. Two remedies are common. First, fixed-lag smoothing computes P(Xt-d | e1:t) for a fixed lag d — the system reports a slightly delayed but better-informed estimate, keeping only the last d messages in a sliding window, which gives constant time and space per step (for HMMs, by incrementally multiplying the new observation matrix in at the front of the window and dividing the old one out at the back). A speech recogniser that lags half a second behind the speaker, or a GPS track-cleaner that reports your position as of two seconds ago, is doing exactly this. Second, for the whole-sequence case one can use a divide-and-conquer or checkpointing scheme that trades a logarithmic factor of time for a square-root or logarithmic reduction in space.

Smoothing is also the E step of the Baum–Welch (EM) algorithm that learns HMM parameters from unlabelled sequences: the smoothed single-slice posteriors and the smoothed pairwise posteriors P(Xk, Xk+1 | e1:t) ∝ f1:k[i] · T[i][j] · P(ek+1|j) · bk+2:t[j] become the expected counts from which the new transition and sensor tables are re-estimated.

Most likely explanation and the Viterbi algorithm

Suppose the umbrella appears on days 1, 2, 4 and 5 but not on day 3, and we want the single most probable weather sequence argmaxr1:5 P(r1:5 | u1:5). Enumerating all St sequences (here 2⁵ = 32, but 2¹⁰⁰ for a longer run) is hopeless. The saving observation is a dynamic-programming one: the most likely path to a given state at time t+1 must consist of the most likely path to some state at time t, extended by one step. So we only need to keep, for each state, the best path ending there.

Define m1:t[i] = maxx1:t-1 P(x1:t-1, Xt = i, e1:t). The recursion is the forward recursion with the summation replaced by a maximisation — max-product instead of sum-product:

m(1:t+1)[j] = P(e(t+1) | X(t+1)=j) · max over i of ( T[i][j] · m(1:t)[i] )
ptr(t+1)[j] = argmax over i of ( T[i][j] · m(1:t)[i] )

The second line is the back-pointer: for each state at the new time step we record which predecessor achieved the maximum. When the final step is reached, we take the largest entry of m1:t, and then trace the back-pointers backwards from it to recover the entire optimal path. This back-tracking phase is what distinguishes Viterbi from a mere score computation.

Why Viterbi is not the same as per-slice argmax. A tempting shortcut is to smooth every time step and then take the most probable state at each step independently. This gives the sequence of marginally most likely states, which is a different object from the jointly most likely sequence, and it can be catastrophically wrong: the resulting sequence may have probability zero under the model. Imagine a three-state system in which the transition A → B is forbidden (T[A][B] = 0); if slice 7 is 51 % A and slice 8 is 51 % B, per-slice argmax outputs "…A, B…", an impossible trajectory that no amount of post-processing can rescue. Viterbi, because it maximises the joint probability of the whole path, can never return a path of probability zero if any positive-probability path exists. The flip side: Viterbi's single best path may itself have tiny absolute probability and may not maximise the number of individually correct slices. Choose per-slice smoothing when you are scored per time step; choose Viterbi when you need a globally coherent, legal sequence (a word transcription, a gene annotation, a decoded bit stream).

Cost and implementation notes. Viterbi performs the same number of arithmetic operations as the forward pass: O(t·S²) time, i.e. linear in sequence length and quadratic in the number of states. Unlike filtering, it needs O(t·S) space to store the back-pointer lattice, since the path cannot be recovered without it. Because the m values are products of many small numbers, real implementations work in logarithms, where max-product becomes a max-plus recursion, log mt+1[j] = log P(et+1|j) + maxi(log T[i][j] + log mt[i]), which is numerically stable and uses only additions and comparisons. Viterbi may also be read as a shortest-path computation in a layered graph (the "trellis") whose edge weights are negative log probabilities.

Several structural points tie the family together:

  • Filtering and prediction are the online pair. They need only the current message, so an agent can run them in a perception–action loop forever. Prediction is just filtering with the eyes closed, and its value decays on the timescale of the chain's mixing time.
  • Smoothing is the offline (or lagged) task. It needs information from the future, so either the sequence must be complete or the answer must be delayed by d steps. In exchange it is strictly more accurate than filtering for any slice before the present, because it conditions on strictly more evidence.
  • Viterbi shares filtering's recursive skeleton — same lattice, same O(t·S²) work, same message-passing shape — and differs only in using max where filtering uses Σ, plus the book-keeping of back-pointers. Sum-product answers "how much total probability flows into this state?"; max-product answers "what is the single heaviest path into this state?".
  • Everything here is a specialisation of general Bayesian-network inference. The forward recursion is precisely variable elimination run left-to-right on the chain-structured network, eliminating X0, then X1, and so on; the forward and backward messages are the intermediate factors. The reason exact inference is cheap here — linear rather than exponential — is that the chain has treewidth 1, so every intermediate factor involves a single variable and never grows. The conditional independences that justify the recursions (future ⊥ past given present; evidence ⊥ everything else given its own state) are exactly what d-separation reports on the chain graph. Smoothing's factorisation f × b is the two-way message passing of the junction-tree/belief-propagation algorithm applied to a chain, and the same algorithms generalise: to continuous Gaussian states they become the Kalman filter and the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother; to richer state factorisations they become dynamic Bayesian networks, where exact inference may again become intractable and particle filtering or other approximations take over.

A checklist for choosing. Ask three questions. (1) Which slice do I need? Present → filtering; future → prediction; past → smoothing; the whole path → Viterbi. (2) Can I wait? If decisions must be made now, you are limited to the forward pass; if you can tolerate a delay d, fixed-lag smoothing buys accuracy cheaply; if the data are archival, use full forward–backward. (3) Do I need a coherent sequence or per-slice estimates? If a downstream consumer requires a legal trajectory, use Viterbi even though per-slice posteriors would score better on per-slice accuracy; if you need calibrated uncertainty at each step, use smoothing, because Viterbi returns a path with no probabilities attached to individual slices.

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) describes a system that changes over time through a sequence of hidden states that we cannot observe directly. Instead, at each time step we receive an observation (evidence) that depends probabilistically on the current hidden state. The model is specified by three pieces: an initial state distribution, a transition model giving the probability of moving from one state to the next, and an emission (sensor) model giving the probability of each observation given a state. Because the state at time t depends only on the state at time t-1, and the observation depends only on the current state, we can reason about long sequences efficiently with dynamic programming instead of enumerating every possible state path.

This topic organizes HMM reasoning into four standard inference tasks. Filtering asks for the belief about the current state given all evidence so far, and is computed recursively by the forward algorithm. Prediction projects that belief forward past the last observation using only the transition model. Smoothing asks about a past state given all the evidence, including evidence that arrived later, and is handled by combining forward messages with backward messages (the forward-backward algorithm). Most likely explanation asks for the single best state sequence as a whole, which is the job of the Viterbi algorithm. These tasks matter in this course because they turn the general Bayesian-network machinery of the module into a practical, tractable procedure for sequential data such as speech, part-of-speech tagging, robot localization, and gene sequence analysis.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, which evidence is being conditioned on in each task, and how does that change the recursion? Second, what is stored in each cell of the dynamic programming table, and how is it computed from the previous column? Third, where does Viterbi differ from the forward algorithm: Viterbi replaces the sum over predecessor states with a maximum and keeps backpointers so the best path can be recovered. Tracking those differences will make the four tasks feel like variations on one pattern rather than four separate formulas.

Decision Theory and Utility

▶

Probabilistic inference tells an agent what the world is likely to be like; it says nothing about what the agent should do. An agent that has computed a beautifully calibrated posterior distribution over the state of a patient, a market, or a robot's surroundings is still paralysed until it is given a second ingredient: a statement of what it wants. Decision theory is the formal bridge between these two ingredients. In its slogan form, decision theory = probability theory + utility theory, and its central prescription is that a rational agent should select the action that maximises its expected utility. This topic develops that prescription carefully: how a decision problem is specified, what a utility function is and what parts of it carry meaning, which axioms of preference force the existence of a utility function in the first place, how attitudes toward risk show up in the shape of a utility curve, how utilities are elicited from real people over outcomes with many attributes, and finally how to put a price on information itself.

From probability to decisions. A decision problem is specified by four components. First, a set of available actions A = {a₁, a₂, …} that the agent can actually execute. Second, a set of possible outcomes or result states S = {s₁, s₂, …}, described in enough detail that the agent's preferences over them are well defined. Third, a probability model P(s | a, e) giving the chance of each outcome if action a is taken, conditioned on everything the agent has observed (its evidence e). Fourth, the agent's preferences, summarised by a utility function U(s) that maps each outcome to a real number. Nothing else is needed: once these four components are fixed, the "right" action is determined.

Where do these components come from in a practical AI system? The probabilities come from exactly the inference machinery studied earlier in the module. A Bayesian network over medical variables, conditioned on observed symptoms and test results, yields P(Disease | Symptoms); a hidden Markov model, filtered over a stream of sensor readings, yields a belief state P(X_t | e_{1:t}) over the current location of a robot. In both cases, the output of inference is precisely the probability distribution over the states that matter for the decision. The preferences, by contrast, do not come from inference — they are supplied by the designer, the user, or the organisation on whose behalf the agent acts. A robot's sensor model is a factual matter that can be measured; whether it is worse to collide with a wall or to arrive ten seconds late is a value judgement that must be stated.

The two ingredients combine through the expected utility of an action:

EU(a | e) = Σ_s  P(Result(a) = s | a, e) · U(s)

and the agent's prescription, the principle of maximum expected utility (MEU), is

action = argmax_a  EU(a | e)

Consider a tiny but complete example. You are leaving the house and must decide whether to carry an umbrella. Inference over a weather model, conditioned on this morning's forecast, gives P(Rain) = 0.3. Your preferences over the four joint outcomes, measured on an arbitrary 0–100 scale, are:

ActionOutcome if Rain (P = 0.3)UtilityOutcome if No Rain (P = 0.7)UtilityExpected Utility
Take umbrellaDry, slightly encumbered70Dry, carrying dead weight600.3(70) + 0.7(60) = 63
Leave umbrellaSoaked, ruined clothes0Unencumbered and dry1000.3(0) + 0.7(100) = 70

MEU recommends leaving the umbrella, because 70 > 63. Note how sensitive this is to the probability: if the forecast had instead supported P(Rain) = 0.5, the expected utilities would be 65 and 50, and the recommendation would flip. The decision depends jointly on beliefs and preferences, and changing either can change the answer. Note also that the recommendation can be wrong in hindsight — it rains 30% of the time, and on those days you get soaked. MEU is a rule for acting well under uncertainty, not a guarantee of good outcomes.

Influence diagrams (decision networks). Real decision problems involve many random variables, and writing out a flat table of outcomes becomes impossible. An influence diagram, or decision network, extends a Bayesian network with two new node types so that a decision problem can be represented compactly and exploited by the same factorisation ideas:

  • Chance nodes (conventionally drawn as ovals) are ordinary random variables with conditional probability tables, exactly as in a Bayesian network.
  • Decision nodes (rectangles) represent points at which the agent chooses a value. They have no CPT; their value is selected by the agent, not sampled from nature. Arcs into a decision node are information links: they indicate what the agent will know at the moment it decides.
  • Utility nodes (diamonds) represent the agent's utility function. Their parents are the variables the utility depends on, and the node is annotated with a table or function giving U for each combination of parent values.

For the umbrella problem, the network has a chance node Weather, a chance node Forecast with parent Weather, a decision node Umbrella with an information link from Forecast, and a utility node with parents Weather and Umbrella. Evaluating the diagram follows a fixed recipe: (1) set the evidence variables to their observed values; (2) for each possible value of the decision node, set the decision node to that value and run standard probabilistic inference (enumeration or variable elimination) to compute the posterior over the utility node's parents; (3) compute the expected utility; (4) return the action with the highest value. In other words, decision-network evaluation is a thin wrapper around the inference algorithms already available, which is exactly why these representations are attractive.

A practical benefit of the diagram is that the structure makes irrelevance visible. If a chance node is not an ancestor of a utility node, it cannot affect any expected utility and can be pruned. If a variable is d-separated from the utility node's parents given the evidence and the decision, observing it cannot change the chosen action — a graphical shortcut to the value-of-information results developed at the end of this topic.

Utility functions and preferences. The utility function is not an arbitrary scoring device bolted onto the problem; it is a numerical summary of an underlying preference relation. We write A ≻ B for "the agent strictly prefers A to B", A ~ B for "the agent is indifferent between A and B", and A ≽ B for "A is preferred or the agent is indifferent". A utility function represents these preferences when

U(A) > U(B)   if and only if   A ≻ B
U(A) = U(B)   if and only if   A ~ B

The objects over which preferences are held are not only deterministic outcomes but lotteries: probability distributions over outcomes. A lottery is written L = [p, A; 1−p, B], meaning "outcome A with probability p, outcome B otherwise". Lotteries are the natural objects here because every action in an uncertain world is a lottery over outcomes — choosing to leave the umbrella is nothing more than accepting the lottery [0.3, Soaked; 0.7, Unencumbered]. The point of a utility function is precisely that it summarises preferences over this enormous space of lotteries with a single number per outcome, because the utility of a lottery is just the expectation of the utilities of its prizes:

U([p₁, s₁; p₂, s₂; …; pₙ, sₙ]) = Σ_i p_i · U(s_i)

Affine invariance. Utilities are not measured on an absolute scale. If U represents an agent's preferences, then so does any positive affine transformation

U'(s) = a·U(s) + b,    with a > 0

The proof is immediate: expectation is linear, so EU'(α) = a·EU(α) + b for every action α, and since a > 0 the ranking of actions by EU' is identical to the ranking by EU. In the umbrella example, utilities of (70, 60, 0, 100) and utilities of (7.0, 6.0, −3.0, 13.0) — obtained with a = 0.1, b = −3 — yield expected utilities (63, 70) and (3.3, 4.0) respectively; the same action wins. Utility is therefore an interval scale, like temperature in Celsius: differences and ratios of differences are meaningful, but the zero point and the unit are conventions. It is meaningless to say "this outcome has utility 50 and is therefore twice as good as one with utility 25", but it is meaningful to say "the improvement from s₁ to s₂ is twice the improvement from s₂ to s₃".

This freedom is exploited to fix a scale. Choose the best conceivable outcome in the problem, call it s⊤, and set U(s⊤) = 1; choose the worst conceivable outcome s⊥ and set U(s⊥) = 0. This is the normalised utility scale, and every other outcome receives a number in [0, 1]. The whole function is then pinned down, and it becomes possible to compare the utility assessments of different analysts working on the same problem — provided they agree on the endpoints.

Maximum expected utility in depth. MEU is a prescription about averaging. It stands in contrast to two other decision rules that appear in the literature and in everyday reasoning. Maximax chooses the action with the best possible outcome, ignoring how unlikely that outcome is — the behaviour of the compulsive optimist. Maximin chooses the action whose worst outcome is least bad, ignoring how unlikely that disaster is — the behaviour of the compulsive pessimist, and the standard rule in adversarial game playing where the "uncertainty" is actually an opponent trying to hurt you. MEU does neither; it weights every outcome by its probability and takes the average. When the uncertainty is genuine indifferent nature rather than a malicious adversary, MEU is the rule justified by the axioms below.

Because MEU averages, an action with a small chance of a very large payoff can outrank a safe action with a modest payoff. Suppose a drug-development team can either (i) license an existing compound for a certain profit of utility 10, or (ii) pursue a novel compound with a 5% chance of a blockbuster worth utility 300 and a 95% chance of total failure worth 0. Then EU(i) = 10 while EU(ii) = 0.05 × 300 = 15, and MEU prefers the risky programme. This is the correct recommendation on the stated utilities, and the pharmaceutical industry's portfolio strategy is built on exactly this arithmetic: run many such programmes and the law of large numbers delivers the average. The caveat, developed in the risk-attitudes section, is that the numbers must be utilities, not dollars. If the firm would be bankrupted by 19 consecutive failures, then the utility of failure is far below 0 on the scale where the blockbuster is 300, and the arithmetic changes.

Two further properties of MEU deserve emphasis. First, the probabilities in the expectation must be conditioned on all available evidence. An agent that ignores a relevant observation is not performing MEU; it is performing MEU on a misspecified problem. Second, MEU does not guarantee the best outcome on any single trial. A doctor who prescribes the treatment with the highest expected utility may still lose the patient; a poker player who makes the highest-EU call may still be beaten by the river card. The guarantee is asymptotic and statistical: over a long sequence of independent decisions, the agent that maximises expected utility accumulates more total utility, with probability approaching one, than any agent following a different policy. Judging a decision by its outcome rather than by the information available when it was made is the error known as resulting.

The axioms of rational preference. A natural objection to all of the above is that the utility function looks like an arbitrary modelling assumption. Why should an agent's desires be summarisable by a single real number per outcome, and why should lotteries be valued by a linear average of those numbers rather than by some other aggregation? The answer is the von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem: if an agent's preferences satisfy a short list of qualitative constraints, then a utility function with exactly these properties must exist, and it is unique up to positive affine transformation. The constraints are stated over lotteries, where A, B, C denote arbitrary lotteries (deterministic outcomes being the degenerate case).

  • Orderability (completeness). For any two lotteries, exactly one of A ≻ B, B ≻ A, A ~ B holds. The agent cannot refuse to have an opinion. This is less innocuous than it looks — it asserts that the agent can compare, say, a career change with a medical procedure — but an agent that must act has effectively expressed a comparison anyway.
  • Transitivity. If A ≻ B and B ≻ C, then A ≻ C. This is the axiom with the most famous enforcement mechanism. Suppose an agent holds C ≻ B, B ≻ A, and A ≻ C. Start it holding A. Since it prefers C, it will pay a cent to swap A for C; since it prefers B to A… and cycling around, it will pay a cent to swap C for B, then B for A, arriving back where it started three cents poorer. This money pump can be run indefinitely, which is a direct behavioural demonstration that intransitive preferences are self-defeating.
  • Continuity. If A ≻ B ≻ C, then there exists some probability p such that [p, A; 1−p, C] ~ B. Intermediate outcomes can always be matched by a gamble between an extreme good and an extreme bad outcome. This axiom is what makes numerical measurement possible at all — it guarantees that the standard-gamble elicitation procedure described below always terminates.
  • Substitutability (independence). If A ~ B, then [p, A; 1−p, C] ~ [p, B; 1−p, C] for any p and C. Equally preferred lotteries can be swapped inside a larger lottery without changing its value; the same holds with ≻ replacing ~. This is the axiom that forces utilities to combine linearly in the probabilities, and it is the one most frequently violated by human subjects (see the Allais paradox).
  • Monotonicity. If A ≻ B, then [p, A; 1−p, B] ≽ [q, A; 1−q, B] if and only if p ≥ q. More chance of the better prize is better. An agent violating this would, bizarrely, pay to reduce its chance of winning something it says it prefers.
  • Decomposability ("no fun in gambling"). A compound lottery is equivalent to the simple lottery with the same overall outcome probabilities: [p, A; 1−p, [q, B; 1−q, C]] ~ [p, A; (1−p)q, B; (1−p)(1−q), C]. Only the final probabilities over outcomes matter, not the mechanism or number of stages by which they are generated. An agent who enjoys the process of gambling does not violate this axiom so much as misdescribe its outcomes: the thrill belongs in the outcome description.

Given these axioms, the existence of U can be constructed rather than merely asserted. Order all outcomes, let s⊤ and s⊥ be best and worst, and set U(s⊤) = 1, U(s⊥) = 0. For any intermediate outcome s, continuity guarantees a unique p with s ~ [p, s⊤; 1−p, s⊥]; define U(s) = p. Monotonicity guarantees this p is unique, substitutability and decomposability guarantee that replacing every prize in a lottery by its equivalent s⊤/s⊥ gamble preserves preference, and the resulting reduced lottery has s⊤-probability equal to Σ p_i U(s_i). Hence preference between lotteries is ranked exactly by expected utility. The MEU principle is therefore not an extra assumption layered on top of probability theory — it is a theorem about any agent whose preferences are internally coherent.

Risk attitudes and the utility of money. Money is the most studied domain for utility because the outcomes are naturally ordered and numerically measurable. Crucially, utility is not linear in money for most agents. Consider the classic test: would you accept a fair coin flip paying £1,000 on heads and £0 on tails, or take £400 for certain? The expected monetary value (EMV) of the gamble is £500, yet most people take the £400. Their preferences are perfectly consistent with the axioms; the function U is simply concave in money.

Two definitions organise the discussion. The certainty equivalent (CE) of a lottery is the sure amount of money the agent regards as exactly as good as the lottery: U(CE) = EU(lottery). The risk premium (or insurance premium) is

Risk premium = EMV(lottery) − CE(lottery)

Diminishing marginal utility of money is precisely the statement that the pain of losing a given sum exceeds the pleasure of gaining it.

Insurance. The risk premium is exactly what makes an insurance market possible. Suppose a homeowner faces a 1% annual chance of a £200,000 fire loss. The EMV of the loss is £2,000. If the homeowner's utility is concave, the certainty equivalent of this lottery may be a loss of, say, £3,500 — meaning the homeowner is willing to pay up to £3,500 for full cover. The insurer, who pools thousands of near-independent policies and is therefore effectively risk neutral (by the law of large numbers its aggregate payout is nearly deterministic), is willing to sell cover for anything above £2,000 plus administrative costs. Any premium between those figures makes both parties better off in expectation of utility, even though the transaction is a pure loss in expectation of money for the buyer. The gap, £3,500 − £2,000 = £1,500, is the risk premium and is the economic space in which the industry operates.

Risk seeking over losses. Empirically, the utility curve for money is not uniformly concave. Below the agent's current wealth level it often becomes convex: a person already facing certain bankruptcy may rationally prefer a gamble with a small chance of escape to a certain ruin, because utility is already near its floor and there is little further to lose. The same shape explains why a trader sitting on a catastrophic loss may double down. The empirically fitted curve is therefore typically S-shaped — concave over gains, convex over losses, with a kink at the reference point — which is the central observation of Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory.

Descriptive anomalies. The axioms are normative (they say how a coherent agent ought to behave), not descriptive (they do not say how people do behave). Two classic experiments show the gap.

The Allais paradox targets substitutability. Consider two choices (payoffs in thousands of pounds):

ChoiceOptionLotteryMajority preference

Choosing A over B implies U(2400) > 0.33·U(2500) + 0.66·U(2400) + 0.01·U(0), i.e. 0.34·U(2400) > 0.33·U(2500) + 0.01·U(0). Choosing D over C implies the exact reverse inequality. No utility function whatsoever can rationalise both choices, yet the majority of subjects make both. The driver is the certainty effect: the jump from 0.99 to 1.00 probability of a good outcome feels far more valuable than the numerically identical jump from 0.33 to 0.34, which violates substitutability.

The Ellsberg paradox targets the assumption that all uncertainty can be represented by a single probability. An urn contains 90 balls: 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are some unknown mixture of black and yellow. Most people prefer a bet on red over a bet on black (suggesting they believe P(black) < 1/3), yet also prefer a bet on black or yellow over a bet on red or yellow (suggesting P(black) + P(yellow) > 1/3 + P(yellow), i.e. P(black) > 1/3). The two preferences are flatly contradictory under any probability assignment. The phenomenon is called ambiguity aversion: people dislike bets whose probabilities are themselves unknown, independently of risk. For an AI designer, the practical lesson is that systems eliciting preferences or probabilities from humans must expect and correct for these systematic distortions, and that a deployed decision system may be rejected by users whose intuitions are prospect-theoretic even when its recommendations are provably coherent.

Eliciting utilities. Since utility functions are not observable, they must be constructed by questioning. The workhorse technique is the standard gamble, which is a direct implementation of the constructive proof sketched above. Fix the best outcome s⊤ at utility 1 and the worst s⊥ at 0. For any intermediate outcome s, ask: "for what probability p are you indifferent between receiving s for certain and a lottery that yields s⊤ with probability p and s⊥ with probability 1 − p?" The answer is the utility: U(s) = p. In practice the indifference point is found by bisection — "would you accept at p = 0.9? at p = 0.5?" — narrowing the interval until the subject hesitates.

A medical illustration: let s⊤ = "20 further years in perfect health" (U = 1) and s⊥ = "immediate death" (U = 0). A patient considering a hip replacement is asked about the intermediate state "20 years with chronic moderate pain". If the patient is indifferent between that state and a surgical gamble with a 0.85 chance of perfect health and a 0.15 chance of death on the table, then U(chronic pain) = 0.85. This number can now be combined with the surgeon's probabilistic model of outcomes to compute expected utilities for operating versus not operating. Two related scales appear in health and safety economics: the micromort, a one-in-a-million chance of death (people typically require roughly £20–50 to accept one, which is far more than they will pay to avoid one — a reference-point effect), and the QALY, the quality-adjusted life year, which is essentially a standard-gamble utility multiplied by duration.

Elicitation is laborious and noisy, so any structure that reduces the number of questions is valuable — which brings us to outcomes described by several attributes.

Multi-attribute outcomes and dominance. Realistic outcomes are vectors: siting an airport involves attributes such as Cost, Noise, Deaths, Construction time, and Disruption. Writing a utility number for every combination of attribute values is hopeless if each attribute has many levels. Multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT) attacks this in two ways: by eliminating options without fully specifying the utility function, and by decomposing the utility function into simple pieces.

Strict dominance is the cheapest tool. If option X is at least as good as option Y on every attribute and strictly better on at least one, then under any monotone utility function X is preferred, and Y can be discarded without further analysis. Consider four airport sites:

Attribute values are themselves usually uncertain, so dominance must be generalised. Stochastic dominance compares distributions rather than point values. Option X first-order stochastically dominates Y on an attribute if, for every threshold t, X's cumulative distribution lies at or below Y's (for an attribute where more is better):

F_X(t) ≤ F_Y(t)   for all t, with strict inequality somewhere

Equivalently, X's distribution is Y's distribution shifted uniformly toward better values, so P(X ≥ t) ≥ P(Y ≥ t) everywhere. Whenever this holds, E[U(X)] ≥ E[U(Y)] for every increasing utility function U — concave, linear or convex — so the dominated option can be pruned regardless of risk attitude. For example, if the cost of construction at site A is uniformly distributed over £2.8–3.4bn and at site B over £3.0–3.8bn, A stochastically dominates B on cost even though their ranges overlap and A is not certain to be cheaper. Because attributes in decision networks are usually linked by monotone relationships (more flights → more noise → lower utility), stochastic dominance can often be established by qualitative reasoning over the network's structure, without numerical integration — the basis of qualitative probabilistic networks.

Preference structure and additive value functions. When dominance fails, the utility function must be decomposed. The key structural condition for the deterministic case (where attribute values are known) is preferential independence. A pair of attributes {X₁, X₂} is preferentially independent of the rest X₃…Xₙ if the preference between two outcomes that differ only in X₁ and X₂ does not depend on the fixed values of the others. For instance, a traveller's trade-off between journey time and fare is plausibly the same whether the weather is wet or dry; weather matters to overall satisfaction, but it does not change how many pounds a saved minute is worth.

If every subset of attributes is preferentially independent of its complement, the attributes are mutually preferentially independent (MPI), and a theorem of Debreu guarantees that preferences can be represented by an additive value function:

V(x₁, …, xₙ) = Σ_i  w_i · V_i(x_i)

where each V_i is a single-attribute value function (often normalised to [0,1]) and the weights w_i encode the trade-offs, commonly normalised so that Σ w_i = 1. The saving is enormous: instead of eliciting one number for each of the exponentially many attribute combinations, the analyst elicits n one-dimensional curves plus n weights. Multiplicative utility. Additivity fails whenever attributes interact. A holiday with good weather and a good hotel may be worth more than the sum of the two improvements (complementarity); a car that is both very fast and very fuel-efficient may be worth less than the sum of the parts if the buyer will only ever use one characteristic (substitutability). For three mutually utility-independent attributes the general representation is the multiplicative utility function:

U = k₁U₁ + k₂U₂ + k₃U₃
  + k k₁k₂ U₁U₂ + k k₂k₃ U₂U₃ + k k₁k₃ U₁U₃
  + k² k₁k₂k₃ U₁U₂U₃

where each U_i is a normalised single-attribute utility and k is a single interaction parameter determined by the k_i (if Σ k_i = 1 then k = 0 and the form collapses to the additive case; k > 0 indicates complementarity, k < 0 substitutability). The practical point is that even with interactions, the number of elicited parameters grows linearly in the number of attributes (n single-attribute curves, n weights, one interaction constant) rather than exponentially. This is the same methodological move as the Bayesian network itself: exploit a conditional-independence-like structure to replace an exponential table with a product of small local factors.

The value of information. So far the agent has been passive about evidence: it conditions on whatever it happens to know. But real agents can acquire information — order a blood test, take another sonar reading, run a market survey, query an oracle — usually at a cost. Decision theory answers the question "is this test worth running?" with the value of perfect information (VPI).

Let the agent's current evidence be e, so its best action and value are

MEU(e) = max_a  Σ_s P(s | e) U(s, a)

Suppose it could observe the exact value of a random variable E_j before deciding. If the observed value turned out to be e_j, the agent would then achieve MEU(e, e_j). But the agent does not yet know which value it will see, so it averages over the possibilities using its current posterior:

VPI_e(E_j) = ( Σ_{e_j} P(E_j = e_j | e) · MEU(e, e_j) ) − MEU(e)

In words: the expected value of acting optimally after seeing the evidence, minus the value of acting optimally now. The crucial asymmetry is that the max sits inside the sum in the first term and outside in the second; information is valuable exactly because it lets the choice of action adapt to what is learned.

A worked example. An oil company may buy drilling rights to a block for £10m. If the block contains oil (prior probability 0.4) the rights are worth £40m; if not, they are worth £0. Buying has EU = 0.4(40 − 10) + 0.6(0 − 10) = 12 − 6 = £6m; not buying has EU = 0. So MEU = £6m and the company buys. Now consider a perfectly accurate seismic survey that reveals whether oil is present:

Observe where the £6m comes from: it is exactly the 0.6 × £10m loss that the company now avoids by walking away from dry blocks. Information is valuable only insofar as it prevents mistakes.

This yields the three structural properties of VPI.

  • Information has zero value if it cannot change the action. If the same action is optimal for every possible value of E_j, then MEU(e, e_j) = Σ_s P(s | e, e_j)U(s, a*) for the same a* throughout, and averaging over e_j recovers MEU(e) exactly, giving VPI = 0. In the oil example, if the rights cost only £1m, the company would buy regardless of the survey (EU(buy) = 0.4(39) + 0.6(−1) = £15m versus £0, and even a "no oil" report leaves… well, it would then not buy, so the survey still has some value) — but make the payoff structure such that buying is optimal under every report and the survey becomes literally worthless. This is the decision-theoretic justification for not ordering a test whose result will not alter the treatment plan, and it is the formal cousin of d-separation: if a variable is irrelevant to the utility node given current evidence, observing it cannot help.
  • VPI is never negative. For any variable and any evidence, VPI_e(E_j) ≥ 0. The proof is the standard fact that the maximum of an average never exceeds the average of maxima: max_a Σ_{e_j} P(e_j|e) EU(a | e, e_j) ≤ Σ_{e_j} P(e_j|e) max_a EU(a | e, e_j). The left-hand side is the value of committing to one action in advance; the right-hand side is the value of waiting and adapting. Since the agent can always ignore the new evidence and do what it was going to do anyway, it can never be made worse off in expectation. Note the careful wording: a particular observation can certainly make the agent's situation look worse (learning that the block is dry reduces the value of the opportunity from £6m to £0m), and information can be harmful in other frameworks — if it must be revealed to a competitor, or if the agent is irrational, or if the "utility" ignores the psychological cost of bad news. Under MEU with a fixed utility function and private observations, however, free information is never a bad deal.
  • VPI is non-additive but order-independent. Non-additivity means VPI_e(E_j, E_k) ≠ VPI_e(E_j) + VPI_e(E_k) in general. Two tests that measure the same underlying quantity are sub-additive: a second, redundant seismic survey adds nothing once the first has been run, so its marginal value collapses to zero. Conversely two tests can be super-additive: if a drilling decision requires both that oil is present and that the pipeline route is viable, neither fact alone changes the decision (you still would not drill), so each has VPI = 0 in isolation, while jointly they are worth a great deal. Order-independence means VPI_e(E_j) + VPI_{e,e_j}(E_k) = VPI_e(E_k) + VPI_{e,e_k}(E_j): the total value of acquiring both pieces of evidence is the same regardless of the sequence in which they are obtained, which is reassuring but does not mean the greedy ordering is optimal.

Imperfect information. Real tests are noisy, and the same machinery handles them with one change: instead of observing the state variable S directly, the agent observes a test result T linked to S by a sensor model P(T | S), and the inner MEU(e, t) is computed from the posterior P(S | e, t) obtained by Bayes' rule. The result is the value of imperfect information, which is always between 0 and the VPI of the underlying variable. Suppose the seismic survey in the oil example is only 80% accurate in each direction: P(T = pos | Oil) = 0.8, P(T = pos | ¬Oil) = 0.2. Information-gathering agents. These results combine into a simple and widely used control loop. At each step, the agent computes VPI_e(E_j) − Cost(E_j) for every observation it could make, performs the observation with the largest positive net value, incorporates the result into e, and repeats; when no observation has positive net value, it stops deliberating and executes the current MEU action. This myopic or greedy policy assumes that only one more observation will be made, which is why it undervalues super-additive combinations such as the oil-and-pipeline pair above, and why a non-myopic analysis (solving the full sequential decision problem) is sometimes required. In practice the greedy rule works well and is the standard algorithm behind sequential diagnosis in medical expert systems, test selection in fault diagnosis, next-best-view planning and sensor scheduling in robotics, experimental design, and active learning, where a learner chooses which unlabelled example to have labelled next. In all of these the same question is being asked: given what I already believe, how much would this measurement change what I do, and is that worth what it costs?

Rational Decision Making Under Uncertainty

Rational Decision Making Under Uncertainty

▶

Probabilistic inference tells an agent what the world is likely to be; it does not, by itself, tell the agent what to do. A diagnostic system that reports P(Meningitis | stiff neck) = 0.0002 has not yet made a decision, because the right action depends not only on how probable the disease is but on how bad it would be to miss it and how costly the treatment is. Decision theory supplies the missing half of the picture: it marries a probabilistic model of the world with a utility function that quantifies preferences, and it defines rationality as the maximisation of expected utility. This topic develops that machinery — utilities, the MEU principle, decision networks (influence diagrams), the algorithm for evaluating them, the value of information — and then applies it to two settings in which uncertainty is unavoidable: medical testing and treatment, and robot action selection under noisy sensing.

Decision theory: combining probability and utility. The basic objects of decision theory are outcome states. A utility function U(s) assigns a single real number to each outcome state s, where larger numbers mean "more desirable". The number is a measure of preference, not of any physical quantity: it may fold together money, health, time, risk, embarrassment and anything else the agent cares about. The existence of such a function is not an arbitrary modelling convenience; it follows from a small set of constraints on preferences (orderability, transitivity, continuity, substitutability, monotonicity and decomposability). If an agent's preferences over lotteries obey those axioms, then there exists a utility function such that the agent prefers lottery A to lottery B exactly when A has higher expected utility. Agents that violate the axioms — for instance an agent with cyclic preferences A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A — can be turned into a money pump, paying repeatedly to be shuffled around the cycle, which is a concrete operational demonstration of irrationality.

Given a model of the world, the expected utility of an action a in the light of evidence e is the probability-weighted average of the utilities of its possible result states:

EU(a | e) = Σ_s P(RESULT(a) = s | a, e) · U(s)

Here P(RESULT(a) = s | a, e) is exactly the kind of posterior that Bayesian-network inference produces: condition on what has been observed, condition on the action being taken, and marginalise out everything that does not matter to the utility. The Maximum Expected Utility (MEU) principle then says that a rational agent should choose

action* = argmax_a EU(a | e)

MEU is deceptively simple. All of the difficulty is pushed into the two inputs: estimating P(s | a, e) accurately (an inference problem) and eliciting U(s) honestly (a preference-elicitation problem). It is worth stressing that MEU does not mean "pick the action most likely to succeed", nor "pick the action with the best possible outcome", nor "pick the action that is best if the most likely state is true". It means: average over all the ways the world might be, weighted by belief.

Utility is not money. If utility were linear in money, no one would buy insurance, because insurers must charge more than the expected loss in order to stay in business. The resolution is that utility over wealth is typically concave: each additional pound is worth slightly less than the one before. Concavity implies risk aversion. Consider an agent with wealth £100,000 facing a 1% chance of a £20,000 loss, and suppose their utility of wealth is U(w) = √w.

OptionOutcome(s)Expected utilityCertainty equivalent
No insurance£100,000 with p = 0.99; £80,000 with p = 0.010.99(316.23) + 0.01(282.84) = 315.90315.90² ≈ £99,793
Full insurance at premium c£100,000 − c with certainty√(100,000 − c)£100,000 − c

The gamble is worth the same to this agent as a sure £99,793, so they will accept any premium up to about £207 — even though the expected loss is only £200. The £7 gap is the risk premium, and it is the insurer's profit margin. The same curvature, read in the other direction, explains why a convex region of the utility curve (common for agents already deep in debt, or for lottery players treating a ticket as entertainment) produces risk-seeking behaviour. Concavity also dissolves the St. Petersburg paradox, in which a gamble with infinite expected monetary value commands only a modest price from real people: its expected utility is finite. In domains where money is a poor currency, specialised utility scales are used — micromorts (one-in-a-million chances of death) for safety decisions, and QALYs (quality-adjusted life years) for medical ones.

Affine invariance. Utility functions are determined only up to a positive affine transformation. If

U'(s) = a·U(s) + b,   with a > 0

then for any two actions, EU'(a₁) > EU'(a₂) if and only if EU(a₁) > EU(a₂), because expectation is linear: EU'(a) = a·EU(a) + b. Consequently, the absolute magnitude of a utility is meaningless; only ratios of differences carry information. This is why utilities are often normalised, fixing the best conceivable outcome at U = 1 and the worst catastrophe at U = 0, with intermediate outcomes elicited by the standard-gamble method: "What probability p of the best outcome (versus the worst) would make you indifferent to receiving s for certain?" The answer is U(s) on the normalised scale. Note what affine invariance does not permit: a nonlinear re-scaling such as squaring all utilities can reverse preferences and is therefore not allowed, and adding a constant to only some outcomes is of course not an affine transformation of the whole function.

Decision networks (influence diagrams). A decision network extends a Bayesian network with two new node types so that a single graphical object can represent beliefs, actions, outcomes and preferences simultaneously.

Two conventions deserve emphasis because they are a frequent source of confusion. First, an arc into a chance or utility node is a statement about the world — "the value of the child depends on the value of the parent". An arc into a decision node is a statement about the agent's epistemic situation — "the agent will have observed this variable before it has to choose". Deleting an information link does not change physics; it changes what the agent is allowed to condition its choice on. Second, decision nodes have no probability tables. A decision node is not a random variable to be predicted; solving the network is precisely the process of working out what value it should take.

A compact running example: an agent must decide whether to take an umbrella. Weather ∈ {Rain, NoRain} is a chance node with prior P(Rain) = 0.3. Forecast ∈ {Rainy, Dry} is a chance node with Weather as its parent. Umbrella ∈ {Take, Leave} is the decision node, and because the agent hears the forecast before leaving the house, there is an information link Forecast → Umbrella. The utility node has parents Weather and Umbrella. Note there is no arc Weather → Umbrella: the agent cannot observe the true weather at decision time, only the forecast.

Evaluating a decision network. For a network with a single decision node, the evaluation algorithm is a loop over actions wrapped around a standard Bayesian-network inference call:

function EVALUATE-DECISION-NETWORK(network, evidence e) returns best action and its EU
    best_EU ← −∞
    for each possible value d of the decision node D do
        set D ← d                                   # treat as an intervention, not an observation
        P ← INFERENCE(network, query = Parents(U), evidence = e ∪ {D = d})
        eu ← Σ_x P(x) · U(x, d)                     # x ranges over value assignments to Parents(U)
        if eu > best_EU then best_EU ← eu; best_action ← d
    return best_action, best_EU

Spelled out as the five canonical steps:

  • Step 1 — instantiate the evidence. Fix every variable the agent has already observed to its observed value. In the umbrella example, suppose Forecast = Rainy.
  • Step 2 — fix the decision node. Set the decision node to one candidate value. Because the agent is setting the value rather than learning it, no probabilistic updating flows backwards through the decision node; it behaves like a clamped input.
  • Step 3 — run Bayesian-network inference. Compute the posterior distribution over the utility node's parents given the evidence and the chosen action. Any exact or approximate method will do: enumeration for tiny networks, variable elimination when the graph is larger but still tractable, and likelihood weighting or Gibbs sampling when exact inference is infeasible. Here, P(Rain | Rainy) = (0.3 × 0.8) / (0.3 × 0.8 + 0.7 × 0.2) = 0.24 / 0.38 = 0.632.
  • Step 4 — take the utility-weighted average. EU(Take | Rainy) = 0.632(70) + 0.368(20) = 44.21 + 7.37 = 51.58 and EU(Leave | Rainy) = 0.632(0) + 0.368(100) = 36.84.
  • Step 5 — return the maximiser. Take wins, so the optimal action given a rainy forecast is to carry the umbrella. Repeating the whole computation with Forecast = Dry gives P(Rain | Dry) = 0.06/0.62 = 0.097, EU(Take) = 0.097(70) + 0.903(20) = 24.84, EU(Leave) = 90.32, so the agent leaves the umbrella. The pair of (observation → action) rules is the policy, and it is the real output of solving a decision network: not a single action, but a rule specifying an action for every possible information state.

    Two refinements are worth knowing. When there are several decision nodes ordered in time, the network is solved by backward induction: the last decision is optimised first, for every combination of information that could precede it, and its optimal expected utility is folded back into the network as a new utility function over the earlier variables. This is the same dynamic-programming idea that underlies the Viterbi algorithm and the Bellman equation. And if the decision network is solved repeatedly for many evidence settings, the per-action inference calls share a great deal of structure, so variable elimination with cached factors is dramatically cheaper than re-running enumeration from scratch each time.

    Value of information. So far the evidence e has been taken as given. But real agents can usually choose what to find out: order a blood test, take another radar sweep, ask the user a clarifying question, run a simulation. Each of these costs something, so the agent needs a principled way of deciding whether the knowledge is worth the price. The value of perfect information (VPI) answers this question by computing, before the observation is made, how much better the agent expects its decision to become once the observation is in hand.

    Let α be the action that currently maximises expected utility given evidence e, so EU(α | e) = max_a Σ_s P(s | a, e) U(s). Suppose the agent could observe the value of some chance variable E_j exactly. If the observed value turned out to be e_jk, the agent would then choose the action α_{e_jk} that is best in that posterior world. The agent does not yet know which value it will see, so it averages over them using its current beliefs:

    VPI_e(E_j) = ( Σ_k P(E_j = e_jk | e) · EU(α_{e_jk} | e, E_j = e_jk) ) − EU(α | e)

    Read the formula as "expected utility of deciding after learning E_j, minus expected utility of deciding now". Three properties follow directly and are worth internalising:

    • VPI is never negative. VPI_e(E_j) ≥ 0 for every variable and every agent. The reason is that the agent is free to ignore the new information: whatever it learns, it can always fall back on α, which guarantees at least EU(α | e) in expectation. Extra options cannot hurt a maximiser. (Information can of course make a particular outcome worse — a test may reveal that the treatment you were about to give is useless — but it cannot lower the expectation taken before the test.)
    • VPI is zero when the observation cannot change the decision. If α_{e_jk} = α for every possible value e_jk, the first term collapses to Σ_k P(e_jk | e) EU(α | e, e_jk) = EU(α | e) and the difference is exactly zero. This is the single most useful practical test: before computing anything, ask "is there any result of this test that would make me act differently?" If not, the test is worthless no matter how accurate it is.
    • VPI is not additive, and it is order-independent. Learning E_1 may destroy most of the value of learning E_2 (two redundant sensors), so VPI(E_1, E_2) ≤ VPI(E_1) + VPI(E_2) in the usual redundant case, though complementary observations can also be super-additive. On the other hand the value of acquiring both, in either order, is the same: VPI_e(E_1) + VPI_{e,E_1}(E_2) = VPI_e(E_2) + VPI_{e,E_2}(E_1).

    Qualitatively, the value of information is largest in the middle ground. If the agent is already certain which action is best — one option dominates by a wide margin — then no plausible observation will flip the decision and VPI ≈ 0. If two actions are nearly tied in expected utility but the difference in their utilities across states is large, then even a moderately informative observation can tip the balance, and the gain from tipping it correctly is substantial, so VPI is high. VPI also scales with the discriminating power of the observation: a test whose result is almost independent of the state of interest produces almost no posterior movement and therefore almost no change of action. The shape of VPI as a function of the current probability is characteristically "tent-like": zero at both extremes, peaking near the decision boundary.

    This yields a simple information-gathering agent:

    function INFORMATION-GATHERING-AGENT(e) returns an action
    loop
        j* ← argmax_j ( VPI_e(E_j) / Cost(E_j) )        # or argmax of VPI − Cost
        if VPI_e(E_{j*}) > Cost(E_{j*}) then
            request observation of E_{j*}; add result to e
        else
            return argmax_a EU(a | e)                   # stop gathering, act

    Because this version considers observations one at a time, it is myopic: it assumes the next observation will be the last before acting. Myopia can undervalue a pair of cheap tests that are individually uninformative but jointly decisive (for example, two weak indicators that only become conclusive together), so in critical applications the agent evaluates small bundles of observations or solves the full sequential problem. In practice the greedy rule is a very good heuristic and is what underlies expert systems that ask you questions in a sensible order, active-learning schemes that choose which example to label, and experimental-design procedures that choose which measurement to take next.

    Case study: medical diagnosis and testing. Consider a screening-and-treatment problem. The chance node Disease ∈ {present, absent} has a prior equal to the prevalence in the patient population.     Case study: robot and agent decisions under sensor noise. A mobile robot never knows its true state. What it has, after running the forward algorithm on its Hidden Markov Model, is the belief state b_t(x) = P(X_t = x | e_{1:t}): a full probability distribution over locations, obstacle configurations or intentions, conditioned on the entire history of noisy sensor readings. The connection to this topic is direct — the filtered belief state is exactly the posterior distribution that appears inside the expected-utility sum. The HMM supplies the probabilities; the decision network supplies the utilities; MEU combines them:

    action* = argmax_a Σ_x b_t(x) · U(x, a)        (for immediate, one-step utilities)

This is the central lesson of the case study — acting on the maximum a posteriori state is not the same as acting rationally, and the gap between them is exactly where safety failures live. The two coincide only when utilities are symmetric across states or when the belief is nearly deterministic.

Each of these sacrifices some immediate utility to buy a better-informed decision, and VPI is the principled test of whether the trade is worth making. Equally, the framework tells the agent when to stop asking: once no available observation has a VPI exceeding its cost, further deliberation is irrational and the agent should act.

    Finally, these one-shot computations generalise to the sequential case. When actions change the state of the world and utility accrues over time, the natural model is a Markov decision process (MDP), in which the agent seeks a policy π(s) maximising expected cumulative (usually discounted) reward, computed by value iteration or policy iteration via the Bellman equation U(s) = R(s) + γ max_a Σ_{s'} P(s' | s, a) U(s'). When the state is also hidden — the robot case — the model becomes a partially observable MDP (POMDP), and the key theoretical result is that the belief state is a sufficient statistic for the history: an optimal policy can be written as a mapping from belief states to actions, π*(b). A POMDP is therefore an MDP over the (continuous) space of belief states, which is why filtering and decision theory fit together so neatly. It is also in the POMDP setting that information gathering ceases to be a bolt-on heuristic: an optimal POMDP policy automatically performs sensing actions when they pay off, because their long-run value is captured inside the Bellman recursion rather than estimated myopically by VPI.

Up to this point in the module you have used probability to describe what an agent believes about the world. Rational decision making adds the second half of the picture: what the agent wants. Decision theory combines a probability distribution over possible states with a utility function over outcomes, and recommends the action with the highest expected utility. This single principle underlies everything in this topic.

Decision networks (also called influence diagrams) extend Bayesian networks with two new node types: decision nodes, which the agent controls, and utility nodes, which score outcomes. Evaluating such a network means setting each possible decision in turn, running ordinary Bayesian network inference to get a distribution over the relevant chance variables, computing expected utility, and choosing the maximum. The value of information extends this further by asking what a piece of evidence would be worth before you pay for it — a question that is central to the medical testing case study (should we order the test?) and to the robot case study (should we take another noisy sensor reading, or act now?).

The videos below approach decision making under uncertainty from applied and practical angles rather than from the formal expected-utility derivation. As you watch, keep translating what you hear back into the vocabulary of the topic: where is the probability model, where is the utility or cost, what is the decision node, and what evidence would change the recommended action? Pay particular attention to how real systems handle the trade-off between gathering more information and committing to an action, since that trade-off is exactly what value of information quantifies.

Introduction

So far in this module, probabilistic models have described a single snapshot of the world: a fixed set of variables, a network structure, and queries about one moment in time. Temporal models extend this idea to processes that unfold over time, such as a robot moving through a building, a patient's condition evolving, or the stream of sounds in a spoken sentence. The key simplification is the Markov assumption: the future is conditionally independent of the past given the present state, which keeps the model compact no matter how long the sequence becomes.

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) adds a second idea. The true state is hidden and never observed directly; instead, at each time step we see an observation generated from the current state. The model is therefore specified by three pieces: an initial state distribution, a transition model giving the probability of moving from one state to the next, and a sensor (emission) model giving the probability of an observation given the state. From this structure come the standard inference tasks you will practice in this module — filtering (the state now), smoothing (a past state given later evidence), prediction, and finding the most likely state sequence with the Viterbi algorithm.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, which variables are hidden and which are observed? Second, where exactly does the Markov assumption get used, and what would break without it? Third, how do the transition and sensor probabilities combine, step by step, to produce a belief about the current state? Note that the videos vary in focus — one covers HMMs directly, one covers Markov chains without hidden state, and one covers a related but distinct undirected model — so read the descriptions to see what each one is and is not meant to teach you.

About this video

A focused lecture on Hidden Markov Models built on the treatment in Jurafsky and Martin's Speech and Language Processing, which means it is likely to introduce hidden states, observations, transition probabilities, and emission probabilities, and to frame the standard HMM inference problems. Expect the classic sequence-labeling framing from natural language processing, where the hidden states are things like part-of-speech tags and the observations are words. Slides accompany the talk, so definitions and model diagrams should be easy to follow and review.

Why this video

This is the most directly on-topic video for the module: it addresses HMM structure, the transition and sensor models, and the inference tasks in one compact lecture, and it is anchored to a standard, widely used textbook treatment.

About this video

An overview lecture from Stanford's CS221 artificial intelligence course introducing Markov networks, an undirected graphical model in which variables influence their neighbors through factors rather than through directed parent-child links. It is likely to cover the graph structure, factors or potentials, and how such models represent dependencies among many variables at once. Treat this as a complementary view of graphical models rather than a lecture on HMMs specifically.

Why this video

Coming from Stanford's own AI course, it gives an authoritative look at a second family of graphical models, which helps you see what is particular to the directed, time-indexed structure of an HMM and what is common to probabilistic graphical modeling generally.

About this video

An accessible explanation of Markov chains starting from the historical dispute that motivated them, then building up the idea that the next state depends only on the current one. The description points to applications including next-word prediction on a phone keyboard, PageRank, and Markov chain Monte Carlo, so expect concrete examples of transition probabilities at work on real sequential data.

Why this video

It targets the Markov assumption and the transition model, the two foundations an HMM is built on, and its everyday examples make the idea of reasoning about a process that changes over time concrete before you add hidden state.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

These videos mainly support MO5. The HMM lecture supplies the vocabulary and model structure — hidden states, transition probabilities, emission probabilities — that the forward, forward-backward, and Viterbi algorithms operate on, and the Markov chain video grounds the transition model and the Markov assumption that make those recursive algorithms efficient in the first place. Note that the videos are an introduction, not a substitute for worked practice: the actual computation required by MO1 and MO5, and the comparison of exact and approximate inference in MO4, will come from the lecture notes, textbook sections, and problem sets, where you should hand-execute filtering and Viterbi on a small HMM.

Introduction

Almost everything an intelligent agent knows about the world is uncertain, and probability theory is the standard language for representing that uncertainty. This topic builds that language from the ground up: conditional probability P(a|b) and the product rule P(a,b) = P(a|b)P(b), the chain rule that factors a full joint distribution into a product of conditionals, and Bayes' rule, which lets you flip a conditional around so that an easy-to-estimate likelihood P(effect|cause) becomes the posterior belief P(cause|effect) you actually want.

The second half of the topic is about size. A full joint distribution over n Boolean variables needs 2^n numbers, which is hopeless for realistic problems. Independence and, more importantly, conditional independence let you break that table into much smaller pieces: once you know the cause, two symptoms may carry no further information about each other. This factoring is exactly what a Bayesian network encodes graphically, so the ideas here are the foundation for everything that follows in this module.

As you watch, keep a few questions in mind. Which quantity is the prior, which is the likelihood, and what role does the denominator (the normalizing constant) play? Why is it often easier to estimate P(test positive|disease) than P(disease|test positive)? And when a speaker multiplies probabilities together, ask yourself what independence assumption is quietly being used. Try to redo at least one worked example on paper before moving on.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos give you the computational core needed for MO1: the sum and product rules, joint versus marginal distributions, and Bayes' rule applied to concrete numbers, which is exactly the skill set for extracting posteriors from a joint distribution by marginalization and conditioning. They also lay the groundwork for MO2 and MO3, since a Bayesian network is just a chain-rule factorization of the joint made compact by conditional independence, and d-separation is a graphical test for the independence relations introduced here. The efficiency argument running through the topic, that factoring a joint into conditionals shrinks the number of parameters you must store and the work you must do, is the same argument that motivates the comparison of exact and approximate inference in MO4 and the recursive filtering and smoothing algorithms for HMMs in MO5.

Introduction

A Bayesian network compactly encodes a joint probability distribution over many variables, but building the network is only half the job. The other half is inference: given evidence about some variables, compute the posterior distribution over the variables you actually care about. This topic covers how that computation is done, from exact methods that sum over the joint distribution to approximate methods that estimate probabilities by drawing samples.

The exact methods start with inference by enumeration, which is conceptually simple but can require summing over an exponential number of assignments. Variable elimination improves on this by pushing summations inward and caching intermediate results as factors, so repeated work is avoided; its cost depends on the structure of the network rather than just the number of variables. Because exact inference is intractable in general for large, densely connected networks, we also study sampling-based approaches: direct and rejection sampling, likelihood weighting (which fixes evidence and weights samples instead of discarding them), and Markov chain Monte Carlo methods such as Gibbs sampling, which wander through the space of assignments and converge to the correct posterior over time.

As you watch, keep two questions in mind. First, mechanically: can you follow each algorithm step by step on a small network and get the same numbers? Second, comparatively: for a given query and network, which method would you choose, and what is the tradeoff between exact answers, computational cost, and the variance or convergence behavior of a sampling estimate?

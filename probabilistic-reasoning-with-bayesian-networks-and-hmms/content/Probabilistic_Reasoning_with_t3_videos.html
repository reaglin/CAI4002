Introduction

A full joint probability distribution answers every probabilistic question you could ask about a set of variables, but it is hopelessly large: n binary variables require 2^n - 1 numbers. Bayesian networks solve this by exploiting conditional independence. Each variable becomes a node in a directed acyclic graph, arrows point from direct causes (parents) to effects, and each node carries a conditional probability table giving the distribution of that variable given its parents. The product of these local tables reconstructs the full joint distribution, usually with far fewer parameters.

This topic covers both halves of that idea. The representational half is the chain-rule factorization, the meaning of the CPTs, and the local semantics that say a node is independent of its non-descendants given its parents. The practical half is the craft of building a network: choosing the right variables, ordering nodes so that edges follow causal direction and stay sparse, and filling in the numbers. Finally, d-separation gives you a purely graphical test for reading conditional independence off the structure, using the three connection patterns (chain, fork, and collider) and the subtle fact that conditioning on a collider or its descendant opens rather than blocks a path.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, how does independence actually reduce the parameter count — count the table entries before and after. Second, what does each arrow commit you to, and what happens to the network if you reverse the variable ordering. Third, when you trace a path between two nodes, which connection pattern are you looking at and is the middle node in the evidence set? Sketch small networks by hand while watching; d-separation only becomes intuitive once you have traced a dozen paths yourself.

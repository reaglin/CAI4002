Introduction

Probability tells an agent what is likely to be true; decision theory tells it what to do about it. This topic connects the two: once a Bayesian network or HMM gives you beliefs over outcomes, you still need a way to rank those outcomes and choose an action. Utility functions supply that ranking, expected utility combines utilities with probabilities, and the principle of Maximum Expected Utility (MEU) says a rational agent picks the action with the highest expected utility.

We also look at why this is the right thing to do. The axioms of rational preference (orderability, transitivity, continuity, substitutability, monotonicity, decomposability) imply that any agent with consistent preferences behaves as if it is maximizing the expectation of some utility function. From there we examine attitudes toward risk — why the utility of money is usually concave, producing risk aversion and a gap between expected monetary value and certainty equivalent — how utilities are elicited from people, how multi-attribute outcomes are handled, and how to compute the value of information before paying for a sensor, a test, or an extra observation.

The two videos below approach this from outside the usual AI textbook framing. The first is a finance treatment of choosing among risky options by trading off expected return against risk, which is a concrete instance of risk attitudes and preference over uncertain outcomes. The second situates decision making inside agent architectures, where utility-based agents are one point on a spectrum. While watching, keep asking: what are the outcomes, what numbers score them, and where does the probability distribution over outcomes come from?

About this video

An explanation of the Markowitz mean-variance model and Modern Portfolio Theory, including the mathematics of combining assets with different expected returns and variances, the efficient frontier, and a short overview of CAPM. Expect to see expected value and variance used together to compare uncertain prospects, rather than expected value alone.

Why this video

It is a clear, worked illustration of risk attitudes and the utility of money: an agent that only maximized expected monetary value would ignore variance, and this video shows why real decision makers do not. Use it to make the abstract idea of risk aversion and concave utility concrete.
